### Executive Summary

Mawson Station was established in 1954. Situated in Mac. Robertson Land, it is the oldest continuously occupied Australian Antarctic station and was Australia’s first permanent base south of the Antarctic Circle.

Mawson Station was listed on the Commonwealth Heritage List in 2004 in recognition of the site’s unique demonstration of the evolution of polar construction methodology between the early 1950s and late 1970s. National recognition of the site’s cultural heritage significance was formalised by its inclusion on the Register of the National Estate in 2001 (now a non-statutory archive), and the Commonwealth Heritage List in 2004.

As the furthest station from mainland Australia, the movement of people and resources for management tasks, such as a heritage monitoring and maintenance, is challenging. Mawson Station is also a working (and aging) station that requires infrastructure upgrades to maintain operational safety and efficiency standards. Other pressures, such as the impacts of a changing climate, also present a challenge for effective management of heritage values.

This Heritage Management Plan maintains Australia’s commitment to environmental stewardship and scientific endeavours across East Antarctica, while simultaneously recognising and valuing the aspects of the station that make it historically significant. In this way, the plan is acknowledgement of the importance of protecting and managing Mawson Station’s heritage values for future generations.

Under the guidance of successive Heritage Management Plans, conservation works have been carried out at the site. To continue to protect the recognised values of the site, a comprehensive on-site Heritage Assessment will be undertaken within the first two years of this Plan’s making to inform further monitoring and analysis of key pressures and support management decisions. The Plan also provides guidance around on- and off- site activities for anyone interested in, or engaged with, Mawson Station, and clearly sets out how the Commonwealth Heritage values of the site, as described on the Commonwealth Heritage List, will be managed and protected.

The management actions within this plan will help to ensure that the heritage values of Mawson Station are identified, protected, conserved, presented and transmitted to all generations, and aims to preserve a tangible connection to Australia’s past, present and ongoing presence in Antarctica.

### Chapter 1: Introduction

#### 1.1 Context

Mawson Station is the oldest continuously occupied Australian Antarctic station, as well as Australia’s first permanent base south of the Antarctic Circle.

This site has been integral to Australia’s contribution to international scientific research within the polar region. It demonstrates the evolution of building methods in Antarctica, and provides insight into early (pre-1980s) station life. The evolution of the station fabric and layout in particular captures the station’s transformation from the prefabricated system (usually Post-tensioned Box or PTB construction) to the Australian Antarctic Building System (AANBUS).

Mawson Station continues to play a vital role in Australia’s presence and activity in the region. In accordance with the Commonwealth Heritage listing, this management plan incorporates the entire Mawson Station site. The primary purpose of this plan is therefore to identify, protect, conserve, present and transmit, to all generations, the heritage values of Mawson Station. As the listing has a focus on a concentration of historic buildings within what is referred to within this plan as the ‘central precinct’, so too does this plan pay particular attention to these structures. Additionally, and in line with the original listing, the heritage value of the historic buildings lies primarily on the external structures and their arrangement, not the internal configurations within the buildings.

#### 1.2 The need for a Heritage Management Plan

Mawson Station was listed on the Register of the National Estate in 2001 and transferred to the Commonwealth Heritage List in 2004 due to its retention of the most complete surviving collection of buildings demonstrating the evolution of Australia’s early approach to polar construction methodology.

The *Environment Protection and Biodiversity Conservation Act 1999* (EPBC Act) requires that a Heritage Management Plan is prepared to provide for the protection and management of the heritage values of listed sites. The *Mawson Station Heritage Management Plan 2025* has been prepared in accordance with the requirements for managing Commonwealth Heritage places (Division 3A of the EPBC Act) and is structured in accordance with the *Working Together Managing Commonwealth Heritage Places, A guide for Commonwealth Agencies* (Commonwealth of Australia 2019).

#### 1.3 Public consultation

As required under the EPBC Act and the EPBC Regulations, the public must be consulted on the replacement of management plans prepared for National and Commonwealth Heritage places, and the Minister must also seek and consider comments from the Australian Heritage Council.

An invitation for public comment on the review of the *Mawson Station Heritage Management Plan 2014*–*2019* was held between 17 April 2020 – 14 May 2020. A second invitation for public comment on the draft management plan was subsequently held between 15 December 2023 – 15 February 2024. Two formal submissions were received during this period.

#### 1.4 Plan objectives

The overarching objectives of this plan are three-fold: to comply with the relevant legislative requirements for listed Commonwealth Heritage places as defined under the EPBC Act; to fulfil the Department’s broader commitments to heritage as defined within the *Corporate Plan 2023–24, Department of Climate Change, Energy, the Environment and Water* (DCCEEW 2023); and to align with Australia’s national interests as articulated within the *Australian Antarctic Strategy and 20 Year Action Plan Update* (AAD 2022).

In relation to the first objective, management plans for Commonwealth Heritage places are prepared to assist Commonwealth agencies manage their heritage sites appropriately, and to guide future works and developments. This includes the identification, protection, conservation, presentation and transmission of the Commonwealth Heritage values. This management plan fulfils those requirements for Mawson Station as described under Division 3A, Section 341S of the EPBC Act. Schedule 7A of the EPBC Regulations sets out the matters to be addressed within a heritage management plan, and Appendix A identifies the relevant parts of this plan that comply with the schedule.

In relation to the second objective, this management plan contributes to the fulfillment of one of the four outcomes of the Department’s Corporate Plan. This outcome aims to ‘protect and sustainably manage Australia’s natural, historic and Indigenous heritage places for future generations’ (DCCEEW 2023, 44).

Lastly, in relation to the third objective, this management plan aligns with two of Australia’s national interests in the region as described in the *Australian Antarctic Strategy and 20 Year Action Plan Update*. In managing the Commonwealth Heritage values of Mawson Station site effectively, Australia is:

 promoting the rules and norms of environmental protection within the Antarctic Treaty system, under which historic values are protected (AAD 2022, 11)

 demonstrating its best practice leadership in environmental stewardship by undertaking appropriate management, visitation and conservation of its heritage sites (AAD 2022, 16).

#### 2.1 Natural Environment

The juxtaposition of the old station buildings with the new station buildings, against the backdrop of the natural landscape of the ice cap and mountains, is one of the listed heritage values, under criterion (e) for Mawson Station as a Commonwealth Heritage place.

Mawson Station (67°36'S, 62°53'E) lies approximately 5200 km south-south-west of Perth, Australia. It occupies an ice-free, rock outcrop of 900 by 700 m on the south-eastern shore of Horseshoe Harbour, in Holme Bay, Mac. Robertson Land (Figure 2). The Station site rises to 33 m above sea level, with steep ice cliffs to the east and west of the site.

[image: Map produced by the Australian Antarctic Data Centre in 2011 shows the southern half of Australia (with identification of Hobart) and the three Australian Antarctic stations of Mawson, Davis and Casey, the Australian sub-Antarctic station of Macquarie Island, and Heard Island and McDonald Islands.]

Figure 3: Australian station locations in Antarctica and the sub-Antarctic

Source: Australian Antarctic Data Centre, 2011

The local geology that surrounds Mawson Station comprises ice-polished, moraine-mantled granite (charnockite) known as Mawson Rock. There are few flat areas, and rocky outcrops (roche moutonnées) formed by past northward movement of ice have produced a micro-topography of smooth southern slopes and step-like cliff-lets on the northern aspect.

Around 35 km inland of Mawson Station, the surface of the ice sheet rises to around 1000 m above sea level. From about 15 to 50 km south and south-west, four parallel narrow chains of rocky peaks (nunataks) collectively known as the Framnes Mountains (Mount Henderson and the Masson, David and Casey Ranges) project 300 to 400 m above the ice sheet and from 900 to 1500 m above sea level.

[image: Aerial photograph of the Framnes Mountain range located south and south-west of Mawson Station.]

Figure 4: Aerial view of the Framnes Mountain range located south and south-west of Mawson Station

Source: Justin Chambers, 2013

The area’s terrestrial vegetation is limited to algae, mosses and lichens. Mosses (*Bryum pseudo-triquetrum, Grimmia lawiana* and *Grimmia antarctici*) typically form cushions, turfs or hummocks in gravels with northerly aspects and in the path of melt water. Twenty-one species of lichen have been recorded in the area, the most common of which are *Buellia frigida* and *Xanthoria elegans*.

Weddell seals (*Leptonychotes weddelli*) occur on sea ice near the station, on coastal islands and at tide cracks in the sea ice during late spring and early summer. Leopard seals (*Hydrurga leptonyx*) and crabeater seals (*Lobodon carcinophagus*) are occasionally present on sea ice close to shore. Antarctic fur seals (*Arctocephalus gazelle*), elephant seals (*Mirounga leonina*) and Ross seals (*Ommatophoca rossi*) are present in the wider region.

Although the site is not considered a significant breeding area or access route for birds, Wilson’s storm petrels (*Oceanites oceanicus*) and snow petrels (*Pagodroma nivea*) breed at the site within or adjacent to the station. Antarctic petrels (*Thalassoica antarctica*), Cape petrels (*Daption capense*), Southern giant petrels (*Macronectes giganteus*), Antarctic fulmars (*Fulmarus glacialoides*) and Antarctic skuas (*Catharacta maccormickii*) can sometimes be seen flying around the station. Adélie penguins (*Pygoscelis adeliae*) and all the above flying species except the Antarctic petrel and southern fulmar breed on nearby islands. Adélie penguins, and occasionally emperor penguins (*Aptenodytes fosteri*), moult near the station.

Mean monthly maximum temperatures at Mawson Station range from 2.5°C in January to -15.2°C in August. Mean monthly minimums range between -2.7°C in January to -21.4°C in August with extremes of +10.6°C (9 January 1974) and -36.0°C (17 July 1985). The mean annual wind speed is 40 km per hour with frequent prolonged periods of strong south-easterly katabatic winds from the ice cap up to gusts exceeding 140 knots. The region is characterised by very low absolute humidity and low precipitation. It frequently has periods of intensified winds, blizzards and low visibility associated with the passage of major low-pressure systems. Katabatic winds also occur during high pressure systems and are intensified as they are funnelled off the plateau through the nearby mountain ranges.

#### 2.3 Building Methodologies

##### Pre-1970s: The post-tensioned box series

Mark I and Mark II buildings

As Australia’s first Antarctic station, Mawson Station was, by default, a test site for an evolving prefabricated panel system of building construction – the post-tensioned box (PTB) design. This system allowed for rapid assembly in harsh wind conditions, a necessity in the challenging Antarctic environment. The first two versions, Mark I and Mark II, were developed for and installed in the early 1950s on Heard Island and Macquarie Island.

The PTB building types were readily transportable by ship and small unloading craft, quickly erected by unskilled labourers (i.e. scientific parties), and easy to heat and maintain. The remaining examples of Mark I (Weddell – Figure 5) and Mark II (Magnetic Absolute Hut – Figure 12) buildings found at Mawson Station were moved there in 1954 from Heard Island after several years of service.

Mark III buildings

The Mark III building method was employed from 1954 and evolved as the Australian Antarctic program gained familiarity with Antarctic conditions. Although aspects of the building style remained the same as its predecessors, various alterations and improvements were made. Key amongst these was a change in the method of fixing the panels to the building frame. Instead of bolting (Mark I) or using external brackets to join the panels together (Mark II), the panels were attached using horizontal clamping rods which ran through each panel. These rods were then tightened from the outside of the building to compress rubber seals, and the exterior and interior surfaces were then covered with a latex-based sealant. The effect of the clamping rods was to sandwich the several layers of cladding together, sealing the panels against moisture and water vapour.

Mark III buildings featured

 improved insulation, including double-glazed perspex box windows and heavy ‘cold room’ doors with clamp handles

 addition of coke-stove heating and exhaust fan ventilation, and purpose-designed furnishings

 improved access through a roof hatch

 reduced exterior maintenance by using aluminium sheeting to protect against snow blast

 a simple box appearance rather than pitched rooves and gabled ends.

The first building constructed at Mawson Station using this method was the Works Hut (later the Electricians’ Workshop, Figure 8).

Mark IV buildings

Mark IV building design began in 1955 and was structurally very similar to the Mark III design. As faults in the PTB series became evident, the preferred cladding material changed from bare plywood to aluminium, asbestos cement sheeting and finally zincanneal steel (used exclusively from 1959 on). The original stone base foundations gave way to timber sleepers, wooden chocks, steel scaffolding (from 1960 onwards) and eventually concrete. Structural details such as door and window types also changed over time, as did internal building materials. Onazote insulation, which was not fire retardant, was replaced by polystyrene foam and asbestos cement.

The PTB series was followed by a style known as the ‘Gimesey’ until deficiencies – primarily in the complex shipping and construction requirements, and failures of insulation – led to its abandonment.

By the late 1960s, more than 50 buildings had been erected at Mawson Station, with a closely spaced group of 27 core domestic and storage buildings (the central precinct).

##### Post-1970s: The Australian Antarctic Building System

From the late 1970s, a new generation of larger buildings with a modular design and construction was progressively introduced to Australia’s Antarctic stations.

Known as the Australian Antarctic Building System (AANBUS), the buildings are steel-framed and insulated steel panel-walled structures that sit on concrete foundations. They integrate multiple functions under one roof and are linked by sophisticated service systems. Whereas the earlier buildings were designed to be put together by unskilled expedition teams, the AANBUS buildings were prefabricated in Australia and erected by large teams of trades personnel in Antarctica.

The first AANBUS prototype was erected as a pump house in 1977, and remaining AANBUS buildings include the Red Shed (the living quarters, sleeping and medical quarters, and Australia’s first two-storey building in Antarctica), the Operations Building, Workshop, Emergency Vehicle Shelter, Tank House, Water Supply Building, Magnetic Variometer Building, Main Power House, Emergency Power House, Balloon Building, Waste Treatment Plant, Store, Aeronomy, Carpenters Workshop, ANARESAT Building (satellite equipment) and Ring Main Units (small service buildings). From 1977, this style of prefabrication ultimately led to the older buildings falling into disrepair.

More recently, the AANBUS buildings have been supplemented by two buildings assembled from modified 20 ft shipping containers mounted on concrete footings – the Field Store in 2007, and the Australian Radiation Protection and Nuclear Safety Agency (ARPANSA) Building in 2013 (which houses equipment to monitor international compliance with the Comprehensive Nuclear-Test-Ban Treaty).

#### 2.4 Historic structures located in the central precinct

###### Weddell (1950)

[image: Photograph of Weddell hut in the foreground, and Shackleton hut and a wind turbine in the background.]

Figure 5: Weddell (building in foreground)

Source: Rebecca Jeffcoat, 2022

Weddell is a 7.3 x 3.6 m pitched-roof PTB Mark I building originally erected on Heard Island in 1950 (Figure 5). It has four windows and built-in work benches. It was shipped to Mawson in 1955 for use as sleeping quarters. It sits on sleeper and bedrock foundations and is made of plywood-skinned panels attached to a pre-cut timber frame. The annex joining it to Biscoe once contained a laundry and an alcove in which timber was stored on racks. For many years husky pups were born and housed there. Weddell has also been used as a sleeping hut, recreation hut (from 1961) and hobby shop (from 1984). It is currently used as a hobby hut.

###### Biscoe (1954)

###### [image: Photograph of Biscoe hut in bright colours after repairs were completed.]

Figure 6: Biscoe (repairs completed)

Source: Mike Staples, 2008

Biscoe is a 7.9 x 7.9 m pitched-roof hut made by the Norsk Polar Institute (Figure 6). It was acquired by the Australian Government and shipped to Mawson Station in 1954. It is made of pre-cut timber, sheeted with external timber boards and lined with Masonite. It was later connected to Weddell by a 5 m annex which remains. Biscoe has also been used or known as the drying room, brewery, workshop, store, dog hospital, sewing room, laundry and carpenter’s shop. It is currently in use as a music room and recreation space.

###### Electrical Store (1954)

###### [image: Photograph of the yellow Electrical Store building situated on bedrock.]

Figure 7: Electrical Store

Source: Strategic Infrastructure Team, AAD, 2019

The Electrical Store is a 7.3 x 3.6 m plywood PTB Mark III building with a pitched-roof (Figure 7). It was constructed in 1954 and is the only remaining of a row of five huts originally running from the Radio Hut to the sea. The foundations comprise a stone base and timber sleepers. The building is painted yellow and orange. The Electrical Store has also been known or used as Stores Hut #1 and the medical store. It is currently not in use due to its poor condition, particularly the lack of sealing that could lead to blizzard ingress.

###### Electricians’ Workshop (1954)

###### [image: Photograph of the aluminium panelled Electricians' Workshop building situated on bedrock.]

Figure 8: Electricians’ Workshop

Source: Mike Staples, 2008

The Electricians’ Workshop was built in 1954. It is a 7.3 x 3.6 m aluminium panelled, flat-roofed PTB Mark III building constructed on railway sleepers on bedrock (Figure 8). The building has four small rooms including a cold porch (a double-door airlock), office, workshop and store. The building’s internal fixtures include wooden platforms along two walls and metal brackets for internal support. Its triple-glazed Perspex windows have been blocked from opening. The Electrician’s Workshop has also been used or known as the works hut, radio office, post office and storeroom. It is currently not in use due to its poor condition, particularly its lack of sealing.

###### Shackleton (1957)

###### [image: Photograph of Shackleton hut: a flat-roofed wooden frame building with insulated aluminium clad panels attached.]

### Chapter 3: Site Usage

#### 3.1 Historical Uses

##### Early exploration

Sir Douglas Mawson first sighted the coast, where Mawson Station is now located, during the first British, Australian and New Zealand Antarctic Expedition to the continent (BANZARE 1929–31), aboard the *HMS Discovery*. During this expedition, Mawson made a reconnaissance flight over the region and named the coast after Macpherson Robertson – a chocolate manufacturer who was also a keen patron of Australian Antarctic exploration.

Mawson’s extensive exploration of East Antarctica supported the formalisation in 1933 of the two sectors of the Australian Antarctic Territory (AAT). Mawson cited evidence of other nations’ interests in the Australian sector when convincing the Australian Government to establish a station on the continent. Initial Australian efforts to establish a permanent base on the continent failed due to the lack of a ship suited to heavy ice conditions. The first Australian National Antarctic Research Expedition (ANARE) in 1947 was successful in establishing stations in the sub-Antarctic on Heard Island and Macquarie Island. A station on the continent was still the goal and in 1953 Dr Phillip Law, the first Head of the AAD, used American photographs and Norwegian charts to propose Horseshoe Harbour as a priority site to assess and establish a year-round station on the bedrock.

##### Station foundation

Dr Law obtained approval to mount a small expedition to East Antarctica in early 1954 aboard the Danish ice-strengthened ship *MV Kista Dan*. The ship collected men and dogs from Heard Island, and on arrival confirmed that the Horseshoe Harbour site was suitable for a station. On 13 February 1954, a party led by Law arrived with aircraft, supplies and six huts and raised the Australian flag. In a formal ceremony, in the name of Queen Elizabeth II and the Government of the Commonwealth of Australia, Law named the fledgling station in honour of Sir Douglas Mawson. Australia became the fourth nation to have a permanent presence on the continent after Britain, Argentina and Chile.

The expeditioners shared two ‘barge caravans’ (Figure 19) until the living quarters were erected. The caravans were mounted on steel-sheeted runners and designed to float. They were designed by the first officer-in-charge (OIC)/station leader Bob Dovers, and were based on sleds used by the French in Adélie Land – France’s territorial claim ‘wedged’ between the two sectors of the AAT. These structures are no longer represented at the station.

[image: Black and white photograph from 1954 of expeditioners and barge caravans used during the establishment of Mawson Station.]

Figure 19: Barge caravans used during the establishment of Mawson Station

Source: Hugh Thompson, 1954

The first three permanent buildings – the Electricians’ Workshop (used by the OIC, the meteorologist and radio operator), the Electrical Store, and the Field Store (now removed) – were finished in the 10 days prior to the ship’s departure. These Australian-designed aluminium-clad panel buildings could be erected in a day and were to become the preferred construction method throughout Antarctica.

The first wintering party of 10 members erected three other buildings. The living quarters, or Biscoe hut, is the only building which remains and is still in use today. Biscoe was initially manufactured in Norway for the Norwegian-Swedish-British Antarctic Expedition (1949–52). However, when it was not able to be transported to their Antarctic base, Maudheim, it was left in Cape Town where it was subsequently acquired for use at Mawson Station. Biscoe is the only timber frame and timber board clad structure to be erected on an Australian station.

During this first season, makeshift maintenance was constantly required, equipment was trialled with varying degrees of success, and the long winter was punctuated by arduous field excursions. As Australia’s interests in Antarctica matured, the station came to be viewed as permanent, rather than an annual prospect. Ten more huts were erected during the hand-over to the OIC of the 1955 Mawson party, John Béchervaise.

From 1957, the station hub centred on ‘Coronation St’ comprised six buildings: Weddell hut, Shackleton hut, the Mess, Wilkins hut, Ross hut and Law hut. Within five years the station began to exhibit a village-like footprint with ‘Coronation Street’ representing the central village ‘hub’ (Figure 20).

#### 3.2 Current uses

Since its establishment in 1954, Mawson Station has supported research in the fields of cosmic ray physics, space physics, atmospheric physics, geophysics, glaciology, meteorology, ecology of seabirds and marine mammals, and polar medicine. It remains the western-most focal point for Australian Antarctic science. Activities conducted at Mawson Station – including by Geoscience Australia (GA), the Bureau of Meteorology (BOM), and the ARPANSA – also enabled Australia to contribute to international obligations under the Conservation of Antarctic Marine Living Resources (CAMLR) Convention Environmental Monitoring Program (CEMP), the Agreement on the Conservation of Albatrosses and Petrels (ACAP) and Comprehensive Nuclear-Test-Ban Treaty (CTBTO).

Cosmic ray data collected at Mawson Station dates back to 1955 and represents one of Australia’s longest standing scientific endeavours. Underground and surface cosmic ray telescopes provide information on the influence of the interplanetary and geomagnetic fields on the propagation of galactic and solar cosmic rays, as well as the occurrence of large solar events that potentially pose a radiation hazard to aviation and space operations.

Several space physics research and monitoring instruments, including an ionosonde and riometer, are operated at Mawson Station (Figure 21). The continuous automated measurements of geomagnetic variations ionospheric opacity and structure are used in various operational and research activities that support better detection and understanding of the space environment. Such information also contributes to GA’s observation system.

[image: Photograph of the Cosray laboratory wood-clad stairwell lit with ambient light.]

Figure 21: Cosray laboratory stairwell

Source: Wayne Papps, 1999

Meteorological measurements made at Mawson Station provide one of the longest continuous records of weather and climate in East Antarctica and contribute to BOM’s forecasting and monitoring. In addition to daily surface and upper air sounds, clean air samples are periodically obtained for trace gas analysis. An underground seismometer and a radionuclide atmospheric sampler contribute to monitoring associated with CTBTO and ARPANSA.

Two long-running investigations on populations and demographics of penguins are supported from Mawson Station. An Emperor Penguin colony at Taylor Glacier has been studied intermittently from 1959 to 1987, and continuously since 1988. An Adélie Penguin population at Bèchervaise Island has been systematically studied since 1987 and Adelie penguin populations in the broader Holme Bay region have also been studied since 2005. In recent years, additional studies on some of the flying seabird species (Cape Petrels, Snow Petrels, Wilson’s Storm-petrels, and Southern Giant Petrels) have been conducted in the region and contribute to ACAP monitoring efforts. This program has also contributed to the work carried out in the area to inform CEMP.

More recently, research at Mawson Station has focussed on the impacts of climate change, monitoring the southern atmosphere, as well as sustainable fisheries. Social science projects including team cohesion, collaboration and effectiveness have also been conducted.

Despite adaptions to the historic buildings as necessitated by station operations, the central precinct continues to represent the heritage values as identified.

#### 3.3 Future uses

Management of Mawson Station’s heritage values should complement the operational objectives of the Department. To this end, it is desirable for the historic buildings and structures to play an important role in the operational life of the contemporary station where possible, including continued use, on-site conservation, effective record-keeping and development of a heritage interpretation program.

The provisions in this management plan seek to provide for the protection of Mawson Station’s Commonwealth Heritage values while providing for the ongoing support of science in Antarctica within the context of the station’s remoteness, and the environmental, financial, operational, and logistic constraints that the remoteness entails.

The inclusion of the historic buildings and structures in the active life of the station, whether through continued use or adaptive re-use, will be encouraged. The re-use and adaptation of buildings is in line with past practices whereby individual buildings have been relocated and refitted as expeditioner numbers, science programs and similar instances have dictated a need.

Like other Antarctic national programs, Australia is in the process of assessing its existing station designs to understand how facilities might be modernised and remediated to meet current operational and environmental standards. Such activities are integral to ensuring that Australia’s stations remain safe for expeditioners, have minimal impact on the environment, and are essential to enhancing Australia’s ability to lead world‐class science and environmental stewardship across East Antarctica. A key consideration will be to ensure that the aspects of these stations that make them historically significant are recognised and valued, and in the case of Mawson Station, where Commonwealth Heritage listed elements are present, these are taken into account as the station continues evolve.

Commonwealth Heritage is protected under the EPBC Act (Section 26 – Actions on Commonwealth Land). As such, any actions that could lead to a significant impact on the environment, including heritage, cannot be undertaken without written approval from the Minister for the Environment and must be referred to the Department for assessment (see Chapter 4 for relevant legislation). To determine whether a proposed action requires assessment and approval under the EPBC Act, a self-assessment, and potentially a Heritage Impact Assessment should be undertaken. Chapter 10 provides further details on the assessment processes for determining whether an action is likely to have a significant impact on a Commonwealth Heritage place.

The Department’s Significant Impact Guidelines 1.2 provide further guidance on determining significant impacts on the environment. More information about the referral process can be found on the Department’s EPBC Act Environment Assessment and Approvals webpage.

### Chapter 4: Management Frameworks

#### 4.1 Site governance

Responsibility for Mawson Station is vested in the Department, on behalf of the Australian Government. This responsibility includes ensuring that the heritage values of the site are preserved, and that these values are interpreted and presented to the Australian community. In practice, the AAD is responsible for the development of priorities, policies and procedures associated with managing Australia’s Antarctic heritage, for implementation by the appropriate personnel.

The Australian Heritage Council advises the Minister responsible for heritage matters on identification, assessment, conservation and monitoring of heritage. In particular, the Council provides comment to the Minister on plans of management for listed sites.

Three key pieces of Australian legislation apply to the management of Mawson Station’s heritage values – the EPBC Act and EPBC Regulations, the *Antarctic Treaty (Environment Protection) Act 1980* (ATEP Act) and the *Protection of Movable Cultural Heritage Act 1986*.

#### 4.2 Environment Protection and Biodiversity Conservation Act 1999 and Environment Protection and Biodiversity Conservation Regulations 2000

The EPBC Act requires the responsible government agency to ‘make a plan’ to protect and manage the Commonwealth Heritage values of places that it owns or controls. Plans are binding on the Commonwealth and Commonwealth agencies. Mawson Station is listed on the Commonwealth Heritage List, and this plan is prepared under Section 341S of the EPBC Act.

The EPBC Regulations specify the *Commonwealth Heritage criteria* (10.03A); instructions for preparing *Management plans for Commonwealth Heritage places* (Schedule 7A); and the *Commonwealth Heritage management principles* (Schedule 7B). The EPBC Regulations should be consulted when preparing a management plan for a Commonwealth Heritage place. Appendix A identifies the parts of this plan that comply with Schedule 7A.

If any action on Commonwealth land is likely to have a significant impact on the environment, it must be referred to the Department for approval. Notably, the EPBC Act definition of ‘environment’ includes ‘the heritage values of places’ (Section 528). Accordingly, any actions that are likely to cause a significant impact on the heritage values at Mawson Station will need to be referred to the Minister for the provision of advice on, and approval of, on-site activities under the EPBC Act. This decision-making authority does not sit within the AAD.

It is important to note that section 341ZC of the EPBC Act requires adverse impacts on heritage values to be minimised. It prescribes that a Commonwealth Agency ‘must not take an action that has, will have or is likely to have an adverse impact on the National Heritage values of a National Heritage place or the Commonwealth Heritage values of a Commonwealth Heritage place, unless:

there is no feasible and prudent alternative to taking the action; and

all measures that can reasonably be taken to mitigate the impact of the action on those values are taken.’

#### 4.3 Antarctic Treaty (Environmental Protection) Act 1980

As a signatory to the *Antarctic Treaty 1959* and the *Protocol on Environmental Protection to the Antarctic Treaty 1991* (Environmental Protocol), Australia is bound by provisions on cultural and natural heritage under these instruments. These are given effect through the ATEP Act.

The ATEP Act requires that Australian activities in the Antarctic undergo prior environmental impact assessment. If the Minister considers that the environmental impact is likely to be no more than minor or transitory, the Minister must grant written authorisation to carry on an activity. The Minister may choose to impose conditions to protect the environment and take into account other relevant considerations. More detailed assessments (Initial Environmental Evaluation (IEE) or Comprehensive Environmental Evaluation (CEE)) are required for activities likely to involve higher levels of impact.

The Minister is responsible for the provision of advice on, and approval of, on-site activities under the ATEP Act.

#### 4.4 Protection of Movable Cultural Heritage Act 1986

The *Protection of Movable Cultural Heritage Act 1986* regulates the export of Australia's significant cultural heritage objects. This Act may cover artefacts that are at, or are associated with, the Mawson Station site.

### Chapter 5: Heritage Values

#### 5.1 Assessment of the Commonwealth Heritage values

Prior to its inclusion in the Commonwealth Heritage List in 2004, Mawson Station was assessed on two separate occasions for its heritage value.

Between 1992 and 1993, the heritage significance of the station was formally assessed by the Queen Victoria Museum and Art Gallery, Tasmania (Wishart and Clarke 1993). They concluded that the historical buildings preserved the visual character of the old station and reflected a diversity of building styles and functions, and that the surrounding landscape and station as a whole were also culturally significant.

Between 1995 and 1996, heritage specialists prepared a series of reports for the AAD which reaffirmed the presence of heritage values (Rando and Davies 1995; Rando 1996). Their primary conservation recommendations encouraged retention of the core area of the central precinct, otherwise known as the old station ‘village’, for its ‘picturesque nature’, and preservation of the range of construction technologies and building materials that the structures exhibited.

These assessments formed the basis of Mawson Station’s listings on the Register of the National Estate in 2001 (now a non-statutory archive) and the Commonwealth Heritage List in 2004 as they determined that the site was a place of *significant heritage value* (Division 10.5, s 10.03A, EPBC Regulations). A place’s heritage value is defined by legislation as ‘*including the place’s natural and cultural environment having aesthetic, historic, scientific, or social significance or other significance, for current and future generations of Australians’* (Division 2, s 528, EPBC Act). Overall, the Commonwealth Heritage listing provides the foundation for conservation policies to be developed (according to *The Burra Charter: The Australia ICOMOS Charter for Places of Cultural Significance*) and for heritage management to be undertaken at Mawson Station.

A review of the heritage values associated with Mawson Station confirmed that the values are still extant. A comprehensive review should be undertaken during the life of this plan to ensure any changes to the identified values are captured accordingly.

#### 5.2 Description of the Commonwealth Heritage values

In 2004, the Australian Heritage Council found that the site’s heritage values met six of nine Commonwealth Heritage criteria. Based on this advice, the responsible Minister made a decision to include the site on the Commonwealth Heritage List. The full statement is at Appendix B.

The reasons for listing are summarised in the entry’s *Summary Statement of Significance*:

* Mawson Station, established in 1954, is the oldest continuously occupied scientific station in Antarctica and the first permanent station south of the Antarctic Circle. It was Australia's first continental station, reflecting the post-World War Two revival of Australia's scientific research and territorial interests in Antarctica (Criterion A.4).*

* The establishment and early operations of the station were closely associated with several people of significance in Australia's Antarctic history, whose associations are reflected in planning and design decisions preserved in the surviving buildings. These include Phillip Law, John Bechervaise and Robert Dovers. The Station was named by Law after Sir Douglas Mawson (Criterion H.1).*

* Mawson Station retains the most complete surviving collection of buildings demonstrating the evolution of Antarctic building design used by Australian National Antarctic Research Expeditions (ANARE). These buildings demonstrate experimentation with different construction methods, cladding materials and foundation systems and innovation to deal with polar conditions. These designs are now entirely superseded by the modern Australian Antarctic Building System (AANBUS) modular system (Criterion F.1).*

* The Mawson Station building collection, a remnant of its former village layout, is an intact representation of the characteristics of Antarctic stations and the way of life of expeditioners, as they existed for the first forty years of Australia's permanent involvement on the continent (Criterion D.2).*

* The modern stations are entirely different in their scale, planning and construction and the lifestyle of expeditioners has changed dramatically. The original station design and lifestyle are no longer practiced and the demonstration of them to the degree seen in the Mawson buildings and landscape is now very rare (Criterion B.2).*

 *The village arrangement of Mawson Station's old buildings backed by the newer station and the more distant views of ice cap and mountains is highly regarded as a striking and unusual landscape (Criterion E.1).*

#### 5.3 Condition of the Commonwealth Heritage values

By nature of its Antarctic location, the historic fabric of the Mawson Station buildings is subject to extreme weather conditions. Without a high degree of regular maintenance, they will deteriorate rapidly. Maintenance of these historic structures has focussed on preserving the heritage values of those still serving an operational role. While obvious damage to vacated buildings has been repaired, work on these structures has been directed at ensuring they are maintained at a safe standard.

A summary of the condition of the historic fabric of the historic buildings and structures is provided at Table 3, and the impact that the condition of these physical attributes has on the integrity of the Official Values is demonstrated in Table 4.

The external cladding of the historic buildings is in various stages of disrepair, and some contain asbestos which poses a varying degree of risk based on disturbance potential and stability. Abrasion from wind, seasonal freezing and thawing has caused the external skin to delaminate, paint to flake, and tie rods to rust and corrode, resulting in a loss of external integrity of some PTB buildings.

The internal spaces and fixtures of many of the buildings and structures have been altered to accommodate new functions, while preserving the heritage value they embody. The degree of internal modification varies from little or no internal alteration, to a comprehensive refit to accommodate a different purpose. As such, the primary focus of this plan is on the external structures and their layout given their demonstration of the Commonwealth Heritage criteria. This is in keeping with the 1995 heritage assessment which identified that few historic structures were entirely unaltered, and that the changing, replacing or rearranging of building parts was a regular activity of station operations. Acknowledging this, the assessment recommended that ‘*there is no management imperative to restore buildings to their original conditions … [but that] damaged or worn building components should be replaced with similar materials if possible*’ (Rando and Davies 1995, 117).

### Chapter 6: Pressures

The Commonwealth Heritage values at Mawson Station will be subject to multiple pressures in the coming decade. While some of these can be forecast, there are a range of complexities associated with the ongoing maintenance and management of the site that compound the predictability and subsequent response to pressures in the region. These pressures can be broadly defined as operational, environmental, financial, procedural and inadvertent.

#### 6.1 Operational

Mawson Station is approximately 5,000 km from many of the services typically available to other Australian communities, and of the three Australian Antarctic stations, it is the furthest station from mainland Australia. This complicates the movement of people and resources for tasks such as a heritage monitoring and maintenance.

Mawson Station is also a working and aging station that requires infrastructure upgrades to maintain operational safety and efficiency standards. As highlighted in Chapter 3, the AAD is in the process of modernising and remediating its research stations. Accordingly, any such actions that could have a significant impact on the heritage values of Mawson Station would be referred under the EPBC Act prior to commencement (see Chapter 4 for relevant legislation, and Chapter 10 for the associated decision-making process).

#### 6.2 Environmental

The Antarctic environment is extremely harsh on buildings and other structures.

##### Climate change

The risks to heritage sites across the Antarctic from the effects of a changing climate are numerous and increasing in intensity. They include, but are not limited to, the following environmental factors which have been identified by Antarctic Treaty Consultative Parties (Information Paper no.88, ATCM 46, 2024:

\- changes in moisture levels that increases the exposure of historic fabrics to humidity, precipitation, wetting and flooding events

\- decreased sea ice extent and greater coastal erosion that can encroach on historic fabrics

\- warmer weather that increases the frequency of ice-free conditions and changes freeze-thaw cycles of historic fabrics

\- increased storm activity that can increase affects such as damaging wind abrasion to historic fabrics

\- increased marine acidification that causes adverse chemical reactions with historic fabrics;

\- increased UV-levels that deteriorate historic fabrics

\- increased microbial activity that affects organic historic fabrics.

All of these factors apply to the Mawson Station, and are likely to change how the environmental pressures described below affect the site in the coming decade.

##### Wind

The winds at Mawson are particularly challenging. They can gust between 90 – 180 km per hour and cause abrasion from wind-driven snow and ice (‘corrosion’), presenting a significant threat to wooden structures.

Furthermore, the winds drive snow drift up and into buildings, causing minute particles to penetrate weaknesses in the skin of buildings, and gradually freezing the walls and interior. Freezing and thawing processes have significant impacts including de-lamination of the skins of buildings, and internal damage to paintwork and fittings. Ice build-up underneath buildings can also cause floors to warp and lift.

Strong wind can also delay work on buildings for days through to an entire summer season. Exposure to wind chill work can be physically difficult for expeditioners which presents a challenge for the planning and tasking of heritage-related maintenance projects on-site.

##### Snow

Over the winter months, access to many buildings in the central precinct is blocked by snow drift, which presents challenges for their continued year-round monitoring and maintenance.

##### Atmosphere

The dry environment also creates fire risks, to which timber buildings are especially vulnerable.

#### 6.3 Financial

Australia has a large footprint in the Antarctic and sub-Antarctic regions. Maintenance, modernisation and remediation of Antarctic stations is becoming increasingly costly. The Department is required to balance the needs of supporting and conducting scientific research, with its obligations to preserve the heritage values of Mawson station, as well as other sites of significance including Mawson’s Huts Historic Site at Commonwealth Bay.

#### 6.4 Procedural

The modifications needed to facilitate the compliance of historic structures with contemporary work health and safety standards and procedures may have significant and unavoidable impacts on their fabric. For example, the removal of interior and exterior asbestos cement sheeting may be required to be removed for safety reasons, and action would need to be referred to the Department for assessment.

Notably, where historic fabrics pose a threat to safety – and where no alternative course of action will ensure safety – modification or removal will be permissible consistent with Section 341ZC of the EPBC Act.

#### 6.5 Inadvertent

Emergency interventions may pose risks to other fabric or the visual integrity of the site. These actions may include moving snow or diverting melt water to prevent accumulation under and around buildings; maintenance to address structural deficiencies; and conservation works needed to, for example, reconstruct fittings broken by ice loads.

Conservation work and the removal of snow and ice from the buildings and structures may also displace objects of potential heritage value from their context.

The principles and policies set out in Chapter 7 provide further guidance for such activities.

### Chapter 7: Principles and Policies

#### 7.1 Principles

The Commonwealth Heritage management principles, defined by Schedule 7B of the EPBC Regulations, outline the overarching objective for managing Commonwealth Heritage places, that is: ‘*to identify, protect, conserve, present and transmit, to all generations, their Commonwealth Heritage values’*. These principles also describe the approach to management: taking into account the best available knowledge; ensuring its use is consistent with the conservation of the identified heritage values; making provisions for community involvement; and providing for regular monitoring, review and reporting on the identified heritage values.

The policies and guidance within this management plan acknowledge and incorporate this objective and approach.

#### 7.2 Policies

Schedule 7A of the EPBC Regulations require Heritage Management Plans to include policies and guidance on the protection of heritage values and proposed activities on site.

This management plan sets out four policy areas that support the protection of Mawson Station’s heritage values:

Management and Conservation

### Chapter 8: Monitoring and Review

#### 8.1 Monitoring

Monitoring of the condition of the heritage values of Mawson Station will occur throughout the life of this plan with routine monitoring and reporting incorporated into station operational plans, including taskings, where appropriate, for works to be undertaken. The Implementation Plan will be reviewed on an annual basis to ensure that the policies and overarching objectives of this plan are being fulfilled. For further detail on monitoring efforts, refer to the Implementation Plan (Chapter 9).

#### 8.2 Review

This Heritage Management Plan will be reviewed every five years in accordance with Section 341X

(Commonwealth Heritage places) of the EPBC Act.

A review will also be undertaken if any of the following occur:

 the Commonwealth Heritage values of the place change

 important previously unforeseen management issues present a potential impact on the heritage values of the place

 or the policies contained in the plan do not achieve the stated management objectives.

The plan will remain in force until such time as a new plan is adopted or it is subject to sunsetting.

In the preparation of this plan, a review of the previous Heritage Management Plan, *Mawson Station Heritage Management Plan 2014-2019*, was conducted. The review found that although the previous plan was consistent with the Commonwealth Heritage Management Principles and provided a framework for the protection and conservation of the site's Commonwealth Heritage values, there was a need to better:

 align some actions with relevant legislative requirements;

 structure the plan to reflect a best practice approach; and

 commit to more effective implementation.

These findings have been considered and are reflected in this plan.

**Summary Statement of Significance**\
Mawson Station, established in 1954, is the oldest continuously occupied scientific station in Antarctica and the first permanent station south of the Antarctic Circle. It was Australia's first continental station, reflecting the post-World War Two revival of Australia's scientific research and territorial interests in Antarctica (Criterion A.4).\
The establishment and early operations of the station were closely associated with several people of significance in Australia's Antarctic history, whose associations are reflected in planning and design decisions preserved in the surviving buildings. These include Phillip Law, John Bechervaise and Robert Dovers. The Station was named by Law after Sir Douglas Mawson (Criterion H.1).\
Mawson Station retains the most complete surviving collection of buildings demonstrating the evolution of Antarctic building design used by Australian National Antarctic Research Expeditions (ANARE). These buildings demonstrate experimentation with different construction methods, cladding materials and foundation systems and innovation to deal with polar conditions. These designs are now entirely superseded by the modern Australian Antarctic Building System (AANBUS) modular system (Criterion F.1).\
The Mawson Station building collection, a remnant of its former village layout, is an intact representation of the characteristics of Antarctic stations and the way of life of expeditioners, as they existed for the first forty years of Australia's permanent involvement on the continent (Criterion D.2).\
The modern stations are entirely different in their scale, planning and construction and the lifestyle of expeditioners has changed dramatically. The original station design and lifestyle are no longer practiced and the demonstration of them to the degree seen in the Mawson buildings and landscape is now very rare (Criterion B.2).\
The village arrangement of Mawson Station's old buildings backed by the newer station and the more distant views of ice cap and mountains is highly regarded as a striking and unusual landscape (Criterion E.1).\

**Description history**\
Following World War Two, Australia renewed its interest in the Antarctic, continuing its territorial claims and the tradition of scientific, geographic and hydrographic research established by Mawson's Australasian Antarctic Expedition (AAE 1911–14) and the British, Australian, New Zealand Antarctic Research Expedition (BANZARE 1929–31). In 1947 three expeditions were organised by the Department of External Affairs, under the control of the newly created Australian National Antarctic Expedition (ANARE). The three expeditions were to establish research stations on Heard and Macquarie Islands and to seek out a base for a permanent station on the Antarctic continent, on King George V Land. Each station was to be staffed by fourteen people. The stations at Heard Island and Macquarie Island were successfully established in the summer of 1947–48, with transport being provided by the tank landing ship LST 3501 (later HMAS Labuan). The attempt to find a continental base from HMAS Wyatt Earp, however, was unsuccessful due to the inadequacy of the vessel in heavy ice conditions.\
The operation of the two island stations absorbed the efforts of the Antarctic Division, established in 1949 and the continental base was not established until 1954 when a suitable ice strengthened ship was found. Discussion over the location of the continental base lead to the decision proposed by Phillip Law, then Director of the Australian Antarctic Division, to seek a site in Mac Robertson Land rather than King George V Land, the area of Mawson's earlier AAE expedition. Mac Robertson Land was better situated in relation to the Auroral zone and the supply routes established to maintain Heard Island Station. The site for Mawson Station, on Horseshoe Harbour, was identified from a United States air-photographic survey of the region and from Norwegian maps. The expedition led by Phillip Law left Melbourne on the Kista Dan on 4 January 1954, they arrived at the chosen site and named the station Mawson and raised the Australian flag on 13 February 1954. A 10-man party, lead by Robert Dovers, was to winter on the site and establish the nucleus of the station to support a larger party the following year.\
The Kista Dan departed on 23 February 1954 by which time three huts had been completed. The first hut erected was a works hut now known as the Electricians’ Workshop (1) and initially used by the Officer in Charge, the meteorologist and radio operator. The two other buildings were for storage and are now referred to as the Electrical Store (2) and Field Store (now removed) (3). One other existing building was constructed in 1954, the Biscoe Hut (4). It was manufactured in Norway for Norwegian, British, Swedish Antarctic Expedition (1950–52) for use at the Maudheim Station. It was subsequently purchased by the Australian Antarctic Division for use at Mawson Station for the living quarters. It is a unique hut of timber frame and weatherboard construction.\
Mawson Station continued to expand over the years with buildings being erected in many years. Three of the buildings assessed for listing in the Register of the National Estate were built in 1954 (out of five completed that year), two were built in 1955, one in 1956 and two in 1957. Of the twenty-six buildings erected by the end of the 1957 season, eight survivors are listed.\
Mawson was the first permanent station established outside the Antarctic Peninsula, where Britain, Chile and Argentina were operating in support of their conflicting territorial claims. With the exception of a weather station on the South Orkney Islands, all Antarctic bases before World War Two had been occupied for only a few years, in support of exploration expeditions.\
From the outset the building designs used at Mawson Station differed from those used at other Antarctic stations. At earlier stations timber frame and weatherboard construction was common, as noted the Biscoe Hut (4) is one of this type. In contrast the buildings designed for ANARE and erected at Mawson Station were built with prefabricated panels, a system later widely adopted by other nations in the Antarctic. Mawson Station has examples of nearly all of the building types developed by Australia for its Antarctic stations from 1954 to the 1960s and beyond. Mawson Station, being the earliest established Australian station by default, experienced climatic extremes and was a test site for most of the new versions of building types. As a result, almost all building types developed by Australia for use in Antarctica before the current rebuilding programme are represented there, except the bio lab at Davis Stations.\
The prefabricated panel system evolved over time. The main system, the Post-tensioned Box construction (PTB), is represented by four main versions, termed PTB Mark I to IV. In these the design of the basic structures evolved and the cladding material changed from bare plywood to aluminium sheeting and finally to zinc anneal sheeting and asbestos cement sheeting. At the same time foundation systems evolved from stone bases with timber sleepers to wooden chocks, then to steel scaffolding and eventually to concrete. Structural details such as door and window types also changed over time. This evolution reflected increasing familiarity with Antarctic conditions and innovative responses to it.\
By 1968, the station core had expanded to a closely spaced huddle of twenty seven buildings, most of which served domestic functions or were used for storage. The station rebuilding program, commenced in 1978 and was generally completed in 1993. It introduced a new generation of design to the Antarctic, with large buildings of modular construction integrating a number of functions under one roof and linked by sophisticated services systems.\
The use of sledge dogs was introduced at Mawson Station in the first season (1954) and continued there until the last of Australia's dogs were removed in 1993, making Mawson the Australian station with the longest history of dog sledge operations. The use of dog sledges, now a practice excluded from Antarctica by the Environmental Protocol, was a major factor in the exploration and research of Antarctica.\
Mawson Station provided the first permanent foothold on the Antarctic continent for Australian Scientists. It saw a dramatic interest in scientific research in Antarctica.\
Australian Antarctic stations: The first station was established on Macquarie Island (RNE 11923) in 1911 by the AAE of 1911–14 led by Mawson. The same expedition constructed temporary bases at Cape Denision (Mawsons Huts RNE 13655) and on the Shackleton Ice Shelf. Heard Island 1947–55 (RNE 13656); Mawson Station 1954 onwards; Davis 1957 (RNE 18776) onwards (closed 1965–69); Wilkes 1957 - (transferred to Australia in 1959) closed 1969 (RNE Indicative 16100); Casey 1969 onwards, replacement station opened in 1989) (RNE Indicative 18616).\
Mawson Station is the oldest continuously occupied scientific station on the continent. The evolution of post war Antarctic design is well demonstrated. The two major building types not represented are the 14 sided huts (Alaskan Huts) and the US RAAF Borden Huts.\
The activities of some of most prominent individuals in Australia's Antarctic history are directly associated with the design features and layout of the station. Past and present Mawson expeditioners hold the station in high esteem because of the associations it has with their experiences in Antarctica. This association is strengthened at Mawson Station by the survival of so many buildings which have direct personal associations going back to 1954.\
The importance of Mawson Station in representing a large part of Australia's history in Antarctica is heightened by the extent to which the other continental stations, Davis and Casey, have been impacted by the rebuilding program. Old Casey Station has been almost entirely removed (save for one representative donga) and at Davis the few surviving buildings are a distinct group within the modern station buildings.\
**Description**\
Mawson Station was established on the shore of Horseshoe Harbour, in Holme Bay, Mac Robertson Land. Horseshoe Harbour was chosen because it is a safe anchorage which is ice free in summer. The station was built on the sloping land behind the harbour, the buildings being arranged in a series of roughly parallel curved rows up the slope. This arrangement, together with the topography of the site and its backdrop of ice cap and protruding mountains, gave Mawson Station the appearance of a village. The new station buildings are generally above or to the side of the village and do not substantially diminish the appreciation of the townscape qualities and integrity of the original station. Many of the buildings in this nomination are key components of the village landscape.\
