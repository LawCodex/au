## Executive summary

This national Recovery Plan for the Golden Sun Moth (*Synemon plana*) is made under the *Environment Protection and Biodiversity Conservation Act 1999* (EPBC Act). The plan seeks the persistence of the golden sun moth through protection of habitat, increased habitat quality and connectivity, and reduction in fragmentation, as well as threat management and reduction.

The golden sun moth is a medium sized day-flying moth that occurs in native grasslands in the Australian Capital Territory (ACT), New South Wales (NSW) and Victoria. The species is generally observed during its breeding season (mid-October to January), when moths emerge on warm dry sunny days. Upon emergence male golden sun moths fly in search of the largely stationary females. Adults have non-functional mouth parts and do not feed, but larvae feed on the roots of grasses and therefore grassy habitats are essential to support the species.

Since the golden sun moth was first listed under the EPBC Act as Critically Endangered, substantial survey work has found the species occurs over a broad but fragmented distribution, including in areas with degraded habitat containing non-native grasses, leading to a re-assessment of the species as Vulnerable in 2021. Although native grasslands within the range of the golden sun moth have been lost, degraded and/or fragmented, the golden sun moth occurs within several EPBC Act listed threatened ecological communities and also co-occurs with other listed species that are protected as matters of national environmental significance under the EPBC Act.

The objectives of this plan are as follows:

**Objective 1:** The distribution and habitat occupancy of the golden sun moth is better understood across the full extent of its range.

**Objective 2:** Maximise the number of golden sun moth metapopulations that are represented in secure conservation reserves or protected by conservation mechanisms.

**Objective 3:** Golden sun moth habitat requirements (both native and non-native) are better understood, quality is improved, fragmentation is reduced, and connectivity is increased.

**Objective 4:** Threats to golden sun moth are appropriately managed to reduce their impacts on golden sun moth metapopulations and habitat.

The plan includes recovery actions that are grouped according to four strategies. These actions aim to meet the objectives, as measured against performance criteria.

## 1 Background

### 1.1 Introduction

This National Recovery Plan for the Golden Sun Moth (*Synemon plana*) is made in accordance with Part 13 of the Commonwealth *Environment Protection and Biodiversity Conservation Act 1999* (EPBC Act). This plan considers the conservation requirements of the species across its range and identifies the research and management actions necessary to halt decline and support recovery of the golden sun moth, so that its chances of long-term survival in nature are maximised.

### 1.2 Conservation status

The golden sun moth is listed as Vulnerable under the Commonwealth *Environment Protection and Biodiversity Conservation Act 1999* (EPBC Act), under Criterion 2 (B2ab(ii,iii,iv,v)).

The species was previously listed as Critically Endangered in 2002. Increased survey effort since then has led to a substantial increase in the known area of occupancy (AOO) from approximately 9 km2 to at least 1596 km2, resulting in the species being re-assessed as Vulnerable in 2021.

#### Survey and research focus since listing

Substantial work has gone into surveys and research on the golden sun moth since it was first listed as Critically Endangered under the EPBC Act in 2002. An increase in known AOO has led to a revised listing as Vulnerable under the EPBC Act and an understanding that the species can occur in (and may feed on) areas of native grassland habitat containing invasive weeds such as Chilean needlegrass (*Nassella neesiana*) and serrated tussock (*Nassella trichotoma*), i.e., areas in which portions of habitat are non-native (DEWHA 2009; New 2012; Richter et al. 2013b; EPSDD 2017). Genetic studies indicate the species may be at risk of genetic bottlenecks caused by fragmentation of habitat (Clarke & O'Dwyer 2000; Clarke & Whyte 2003), highlighting the need to address fragmentation and re-establish habitat connectivity to enable subpopulations to persist into the future. Research into the population structure of the golden sun moth has shown that subpopulations shift at the landscape scale over relatively short timeframes and that subpopulations within close proximity of each other may act as metapopulations that gain and loose connectivity over time (Kutt et al. 2015, 2016).

### 1.4 Cultural significance

The cultural, customary and spiritual significance of species and the ecological communities they form are diverse and varied for First Nations peoples. Such knowledge may be held by First Nations peoples who are the custodians of this knowledge and have the rights to decide how this knowledge is shared and used.

First Nations peoples have a long and profound history of occupation and management of local native grassland habitats, some of which provide habitat for the golden sun moth. In the Bangerang language, the species is known as Yurrnga Ganbina (TfN 2024). Clan members of the Bangerang Nation with a Yurrnga Ganbina totem were responsible for looking after its grassland habitat by burning at the right time, in the right place and in the right way (TfN 2024). For the Bangerang People, the emergence of adult golden sun moths is an indicator of the changing of seasons from werra (spring) to wanala (summer) which could signal that care needs to be taken with burning due to the warmer weather (Bangerang Aboriginal Corporation 2025 pers comm 7 July).

Given the species has a broad geographic distribution, the lands on which the species historically and currently occurs belong to First Nations peoples. Traditional Owner groups with Country where the species occurs include, but are not limited to: Wiradjuri, Dharug and Gundungurra in NSW, Ngunnawal and Ngarigo in the ACT, Bangerang and Yorta Yorta in both NSW and Victoria, and Boonwurrung (Bunurong), Taungurung, Wurundjeri, Dja Dja Wurrung, Wadawurrung, Eastern Maar, Gunditjmara, as well as Wotjobaluk, Jaadwa, Jadawadjali, Wergaia and Jupagalk Nations in Victoria (AIATSIS 1996; SGV 2025).

## 2 Species information

### 2.1 Taxonomy

Conventionally accepted as *Synemon plana* Walker (1854).

### 2.2 Description

The golden sun moth, a member of the family Castniidae, is a medium-sized day-flying (diurnal) moth (NSW DCCEEW 2022). Adults have green eyes, clubbed antennae (i.e. antennae that are enlarged at the tip), no functional mouthparts, and females have a long extensible ovipositor (Clarke & Spier-Ashcroft 2003; ACT Government 2017a). Males have a wingspan of approximately 34 mm with the upper side of the forewing dark brown with pale grey patterning, and the hind wing a dark bronze-brown with dark brown patches. Females have a wingspan of approximately 31 mm with the upper side of the forewing dark grey with pale grey patterning, and the hind wing bright orange with black submarginal spots (Clarke & Spier-Ashcroft 2003). The sexes can be distinguished by their wing colours, with only females having bright orange hind wings. Additionally, male moths having a larger wingspan than the females is unique in the Australian Castniidae family (ACT Government 2017a).

Golden sun moth eggs are just over 2 mm long, and the larvae develop and pupate underground. Larvae are cream in colour, and late instars have a red-brown head capsule. At emergence, the empty red-brown pupal case is left protruding from the ground, usually at the base of, or close to a grass tussock. The pupal cases of female moths are larger than those of males, reflecting the larger abdomen size of the gravid female (Richter 2010 cited in ACT Government 2017a).

### 2.3 Distribution

Historically, the golden sun moth was widespread in south-eastern Australia and occurred relatively continuously throughout its range. The species extended from Winburndale near Bathurst in central NSW, through the NSW Southern Tablelands (including large areas of the ACT), down to central and western Victoria and into Bordertown in eastern South Australia (SA) (Edwards 1991 cited in O'Dwyer & Attiwill 1999; NSW DCCEEW 2022). The species’ distribution shows a close correspondence to that of native grasslands and grassy woodlands of low biomass dominated by *Rytidosperma* spp. (formerly *Austrodanthonia, Danthonia*) (wallaby grasses) and *Austrostipa* spp. (speargrass) that were historically maintained by native herbivore grazing and periodic intense fire (Edwards 1993 cited in O'Dwyer & Attiwill 1999; Dorrough et al. 2004; Richter et al. 2013a; Mata et al. 2017). Grasslands and grassy woodlands once covered over 2,000,000 hectares (ha) of south-eastern Australia, and it is probable that the species occurred wherever there were high densities of wallaby grasses (Edwards 1993 cited in DEWHA 2009).

Over the last 200 years, the golden sun moth’s overall extent of occurrence has been reduced and its distribution fragmented, with likely less than 10% of historically occupied areas remaining as suitable habitat. While the species continues to occur across a broad distribution (extent of occurrence (EOO) is estimated to be 145,322 km2), only approximately 1% of this area is estimated to be occupied (AOO is estimated to be 1,596 km2; DAWE 2021). However, while there has been considerable additional survey work since its first listing under the EPBC Act, this has been concentrated where development occurs and the presence or absence of the species is unknown over large areas, particularly the grassland areas west of the Great Dividing Range from approximately west of Goulburn and Canberra, down to Albury, and over the Victorian border to Wangaratta and Shepparton, and south-west to Bendigo. Contemporary data shows the golden sun moth is widespread but locally uncommon, mainly limited to areas where wallaby grasses occur, which are often small and fragmented. However, the species has also been shown to occur in some areas of non-native and degraded habitat with the invasive weed species Chilean needlegrass and serrated tussock (see section 2.5 Habitat; DEWHA 2009; New 2012; Richter et al. 2013b; EPSDD 2017). In both Victoria (Gilmore et al. 2008) and the ACT (Braby & Dunford 2006; Richter et al. 2013), subpopulations have been recorded breeding in grasslands comprised almost entirely of Chilean needlegrass.

Golden sun moth distribution is currently known from sites in the ACT, NSW and Victoria (OEH 2012; Gibbons & Reid 2013; ACT Government 2017a). No extant sites are known from SA, where the species is likely to be locally extinct (Edwards 1994 cited in DEWHA 2009). The known NSW and ACT subpopulations generally occur at elevations between 480 m and 720 m above sea level (asl) and Victorian subpopulations generally occur at lower elevations, between 95 m and 406 m asl (DAWE 2021).

The golden sun moth occurs across a range of land tenures, primarily private land but also Commonwealth land (airports, defence), state and territory land (including conservation reserves and state parks) and national parks. It is estimated that only 3.41% of the distribution occurs within the national reserve system, while the majority (88.77%) is on private land (DCCEEW 2025). The species is not known to occur within any dedicated Indigenous Protected Areas (IPAs).

### 2.4 Population structure

The biology of the golden sun moth creates difficulties in quantitative population assessments and comparisons of subpopulations[1] (Gibson & New 2007). Flying adult males are the only life stage and sex that are readily detected and counted, but they are short-lived and emerge across a season of many weeks (ACT Government 2017a). There is no robust estimate of the national population size for the species. The population estimate for Victoria is 13,500 mature individuals (DELWP 2020), and there is an estimate of 17,250 for the ACT (DAWE 2021; based on Mulvaney 2012). There are no population estimates for NSW, though the species is known from 61 subpopulations in that state (Gibbons & Reid 2013; NSW TSSC 2022).

Since European settlement, native temperate grasslands and grassy woodlands, and therefore areas of habitat for the golden sun moth which feeds on some native grass species, have been heavily reduced and fragmented (Clarke & O’Dwyer 2000). Many of the known subpopulations are confined to small grassland remnants of fewer than five ha, although some sites are larger (more than 300 ha) (Gibson & New 2007; Richter et al. 2013b). A considerable portion of known subpopulations of golden sun moth overlap with urban growth areas or already occur within a matrix of housing and industrial development (Gilmore et al. 2008; Mata et al. 2017). It is possible that the species’ ability to persist in some degraded and exotic grasslands may have mitigated some of the population declines previously inferred as associated with the decline in native temperate grassland habitat across its range (DAWE 2021), although overall decline is expected unless survey data demonstrates otherwise.

Within regions, genetic differentiation among subpopulations is correlated with geographic distance, i.e., subpopulations show isolation by distance (Clarke & O'Dwyer 2000). Males are capable of active and prolonged flight but are unlikely to disperse far (&gt;100 m) from areas of suitable habitat. As such, it is estimated that subpopulations separated by more than 200 m of unsuitable habitat are likely to be effectively isolated sites and may be considered separate subpopulations (ACT Government 2017a). The isolation by distance model describes increasing genetic differentiation correlated with increasing geographic distance (Jenkins et al. 2010). For mobile organisms, lack of significant genetic differentiation over relatively short distances is primarily due to the migration of individuals between subpopulations maintaining genetic diversity. However, in the case of the golden sun moth, given the species’ limited dispersal ability, the lack of genetic differentiation between closely located subpopulations may indicate recent fragmentation of historically connected subpopulations (Clarke & O'Dwyer 2000).

Low genetic differentiation may also indicate temporal variation in the distribution and connectivity of subpopulations (Kutt et al. 2015, 2016). Kutt et al. (2015) found that long-term weather patterns and landscape configuration (aspect, wet or dry position) interact to significantly affect local distribution and abundance over time (from large, connected subpopulations to small patches). This suggests that golden sun moths may form metapopulations, where over multiple years there is changing dispersal and survivorship based on available habitat and extrinsic factors (i.e., climate, landscape position, native vegetation composition), but there is a substantial degree of interaction between local subpopulations over time (Menéndez & Thomas 2000; Kutt et al. 2015, 2016). These metapopulations tend to have a core habitat node with multiple peripheral areas around it (Kutt et al. 2015). Peripheral subpopulations may not necessarily be individually viable across longer timespans but are sustained via dynamic movement through connected landscapes. Thus, the value and viability of any individual subpopulation should be viewed in the metapopulation and landscape context in which is sits.

Genetic studies have suggested that the Victorian subpopulations are evolutionarily distinct from the NSW/ACT subpopulations, representing different evolutionarily significant units (Clarke & O'Dwyer 2000; Clarke & Whyte 2003).

#### Important populations

In this section, the word ‘population’ is used to refer to a subpopulation, in keeping with the terminology used in the EPBC Act and state/territory environmental legislation.

The definition of important population is outlined in the Significant Impact Guidelines 1.1: an ‘important population’ is a population that is necessary for a species’ long-term survival and recovery.

As described above, recent understanding of golden sun moth is that it operates in a metapopulation context. That is, sun moth occurrence and/or density may vary considerably across the landscape according to local environmental conditions, and overall viability is maintained via the connectedness of subpopulations. Thus, important populations for this species Include those that maintain this connectedness and viability. These may be in larger habitat patches that are likely to act as a core node (i.e. reliably occupied across extended periods and diverse conditions), or that facilitate connectivity between adjacent nodes. Core nodes of metapopulations of golden sun moth tend to occur on a gentle north-facing slope or saddle between rock knolls, affording stable, reliable protection from inundation, where larval food plants tend to remain locally dominant, independent of weather or biomass management (Kutt et al. 2015, 2016). In assessing the value of an individual subpopulation, regard should be had to the overall extent of the metapopulation, the distance between subpopulations and the nature of the environment between them to determine likely connectedness pathways.

Additional important populations include those of sufficient size as to be expected to be viable in isolation, and those at the edges of the species’ range.

### 2.5 Habitat

Golden sun moth habitat includes areas containing native grassland, open grassy woodlands and derived grasslands (created by clearing trees from grassy woodlands), that retain a component of larval food species, particularly key wallaby grass species (see Larval food plants below). This includes degraded habitats that retain a component of native larval food species or have been invaded by exotic invasive species (e.g., Chilean needlegrass) which the golden sun moth is known to feed on (see 2.7.2 Introduced flora; DEWHA 2009).

Sites where the golden sun moth is found are generally flat or gently sloping (&lt;5°) and exposed to full sun for much of the day (north facing). This allows stable, reliable protection from inundation and tends to allow larval food plants to remain locally dominant. Kutt et al. (2015) found that there was a reduction in abundance and a spatial shift in subpopulations from nominally wet habitat to nominally dry habitat due to a shift from drought to above average rainfall. However, this shift may not represent actual movement, but rather changed survivorship of pupae, reduction in oviposition sites and the emergence of larvae (Kutt et al. 2015).

Most habitat in the ACT (88%) occurs in areas without trees or in very sparse woodland (Mulvaney 2012; OEH 2012; ACT Government 2017a). It has been suggested that subpopulations of the species occurring in open woodland and secondary native grassland habitat may be the result of the species spreading outside of its preferred habitat (native grassland) into adjacent woodlands following tree clearing (Hogg 2010 cited in ACT Government 2017a; TREC Land Services 2025). Open woodland and secondary native grassland habitat generally support fewer moths than native grasslands (ACT Government 2017a).

Important structural features of habitat appear to be grass tussocks for shelter, egg-laying and larval development and inter-tussock areas for basking to increase body temperature and for displaying females to attract males (ACT Government 2017a). Habitat for the species is usually comprised of a moderate abundance of larval food plants with low to moderate grass height and moderate to high grass cover with inter-tussock space (ACT Government 2017a). Field observations at some sites indicate that where both closed and open grassland occur in close proximity, dense swards of grasses appeared to be actively avoided, with male moths showing a preference for relatively open areas with reduced biomass (Gilmore et al. 2008).

*Larval food plants*

Larvae are underground feeders, found in silk-lined burrows closely associated with the grass roots that they feed upon. Early research suggested that the golden sun moth may be restricted to using wallaby grasses and speargrasses as larval food plants, based on cast pupal cases and tunnels that had been found within grass tussocks of these species (DEWHA 2009). It has been suggested that preferred habitat is comprised of 40% or higher cover of wallaby grass, on sites where soils are low in phosphorus (O'Dwyer & Attiwill 1999).

Within the ACT, sites containing a relatively large number of the species do tend to have a larger percentage cover of wallaby grasses (Mulvaney 2012; ACT Government 2017a). In Victoria, Brown et al. (2011) found a positive relationship between cover of wallaby grass and number of golden sun moths sighted across 46 site surveys within the Victorian Volcanic Plains (VVP). A study undertaken at the Canberra Airport found that the species of wallaby grass and/or the size of grass tussocks may also have importance in larval habitat quality. This evidence was found in an assessment of two sites with the same mean percentage basal cover of wallaby grass but that were comprised of different species of different tussock size (*Rytidosperma carphoides* and *R. caespitosum*). A higher number of both pupal cases and flying males were observed at the site with the larger but fewer tussocks (*R. caespitosum*). This suggests that tussocks with a large root volume may be beneficial in providing for a larva throughout its entire cycle and therefore removing the need to move through the soil to find another tussock and avoiding the energy cost associated with this (Rowell 2009 in ACT Government 2017a).

Golden sun moth larvae are known to feed on two species of exotic grasses (Chilean needlegrass and serrated tussock), with observations suggesting increased body size of larvae developing on Chilean needlegrass (Richter et al. 2013b; ACT Government 2017a).

#### Habitat critical to survival

Habitat critical to the survival of the golden sun moth has not been identified.

As the species has specialised habitat requirements within a fragmented distribution, all occupied native habitat is important for the breeding activity of the associated subpopulation and the recovery of the species. Metapopulations occurring in high quality *native* habitat are important for the long-term maintenance of the species, including maintenance of genetic diversity and long-term evolutionary development.

High quality habitat is defined as medium to large sites containing native grassland with an abundant component of native larval food species (i.e. *Rytidosperma* spp. and/or *Austrostipa* spp.), inter-tussock spaces, and land-use/ management that is consistent with the ecological values of the site. Medium or large are deliberately relativistic terms that have not been defined because they will vary between areas, depending upon the extent of grassland fragmentation, i.e. how intact and connected the landscape is, the quality of the habitat for golden sun moth and the threats impacting specific sites. Sites occurring at or toward the limit of the species’ range, or sites that are a long distance from other known subpopulations (i.e. are unlikely to have genetic exchange with other subpopulations) are also likely to be important for conserving the full range of genetic diversity.

While the species is known to be able to persist in areas dominated by the invasive Chilean needlegrass (see s2.7.2), more research is needed to determine the long-term viability of these habitat patches. Furthermore, Chilean needlegrass is declared and listed as a Weed of National Significance (WoNS), which creates a potential for tension between management of WoNS and golden sun moth. Such patches thus cannot be considered as high quality habitat or habitat critical to survival, and Chilean needlegrass is considered a threat to native biodiversity more broadly, but nevertheless may have value as described above under important populations and consideration given to rehabilitation via careful removal of Chilean needlegrass while seeking to reinstate native grass species that support golden sun moths.

No Critical Habitat as defined under section 207A of the EPBC Act has been identified or included in the Register of Critical Habitat.

### 2.6 Ecology

The adult life stage is the only life stage in which the species is readily detectable, predominately the males, which can be observed flying while female moths remain fairly stationary. Adult moths emerge from the ground during the breeding season (also referred to as the flying season) that falls between mid-October and early January (O'Dwyer & Attiwill 2000). The breeding season typically lasts from six to eight weeks and timing varies slightly between localities across the species’ range (NSW DCCEEW 2022). Adults emerge on warm, dry, sunny days (typically at least two days since rain) during the breeding season, and as they lack functional mouthparts, are unable to feed or drink and live for only a few days (1-4 days) (O'Dwyer & Attiwill 2000; Gibson & New 2007; DEWHA 2009; ACT Government 2017a). Emergence rates and timing differ from day to day and from year to year, being highly influenced by climatic conditions (Mulvaney 2012). The sex ratio of the species at emergence has previously been studied by Richter et al (2013b) through the identification and analysis of pupal cases at 11 survey locations in and near to Canberra. A distinct male-biased sex ratio was found across the two years of the study (a mean sex ratio of 1.9 males to each female). This ratio doesn’t differ significantly between native grassland and exotic (Chilean needlegrass dominated) grassland habitat. The factors that are likely to maintain a male bias in the species are unknown at this stage. It is acknowledged that males and females do differ considerably in their morphology and behaviour that affects their predation risk, catchability, and visibility. These factors would influence any evaluation of the adult sex ratio within subpopulations (Richter et al. 2013b).

After emergence, male moths spend their few short days patrolling habitat for females by flying in low (about 1 m above the ground), rapid, short bursts over grassland during late morning and early afternoon in warm (above about 20°C), sunny conditions with low-moderate wind speeds. Females have been observed to be active later into the afternoons (Gibson & New 2007; Richter et al. 2013b; ACT Government 2017a). Females are semi-flightless, tending to display themselves from sedentary positions, flashing their brightly coloured hind wings (Harwood et al. 1995 cited in O'Dwyer & Attiwill 2000; OEH 2012). After mating, females spend their time laying eggs within the base of grass tussocks of wallaby grasses, speargrasses and Chilean needlegrass (Richter et al. 2013a). Richter et al. (2013a) found in the dissection of 71 females, a mean of 74 eggs per female, with a range of 31 to 148. Males are capable of active and prolonged flight but are unlikely to disperse far (&gt;100 m) from areas of suitable habitat. As such, it is estimated that subpopulations separated by distances of greater than 200 m of unsuitable habitat are likely to be isolated sites and may be considered separate subpopulations (see Population structure, above; ACT Government 2017a). This restricted dispersal ability means sites from which the moth has gone extinct, or vacant patches of habitat more than 200 m from occupied sites, are considered unlikely to be (re)colonised (Clarke & O'Dwyer 2000).

The underground life stages (egg, larva and pupa) are not well understood. At the end of the larval period, which is likely two to three years, the larva prepares a tunnel to the surface and pupation occurs underground in spring. Once adult features have developed within the pupal case, the pupa rises to the surface and the adult emerges. The empty pupal case is left protruding from the soil (Edwards 1994 cited in DEWHA 2009; Richter et al. 2013a). Insect larvae living in temperate regions often face a pathway decision between continued growth and development to the adult stage or delaying emergence until the next season; this is often dependent on temperature, day length and quality of host plants (Gotthard 2008, Danilevskii 1965, and Friberg & Wiklung 2010, all cited in Richter et al. 2013a). High temperature, long days and quality host plants typically support continued development, while opposite conditions at the beginning of colder seasons trigger entry into diapause (Friberg et al. 2012, cited in Richter et al. 2013a).

The drivers of larval development in the golden sun moth are unknown, but distinct larval cohorts of three different size classes were found by Richter et al. (2013). Further research is required to better understand the species’ larval biology, population performance on native versus exotic food plants, relationship to ecological factors, and factors that may limit this part of the life cycle.

#### Survey methods

Certain aspects of the life cycle and behaviour of the species affect its detectability and therefore create challenges for assessing subpopulations. In addition, the lack of tested monitoring methods exacerbates comparative analysis (Richter et al. 2013b). Trialled methods for subpopulation counts and detecting presence/absence include spot counts, belt transects, line transects and mark-release-recapture (Gibson & New 2007; ACT Government 2017a). These methods are all constrained by challenges associated with the species’ detectability and as such, require surveys to be undertaken in suitable conditions during the flying season (DEWHA 2009). Richter et al. (2013b) trialled supplementation of subpopulation monitoring through the counting and sexing of pupal cases. This method has potential to be used as a valuable additional tool for monitoring local subpopulations, particularly when weather conditions are not favourable (i.e., wet and overcast), although it is more time intensive and may be more suited to small, fragmented sites. There are also limitations to this approach, as there can be difficulties in finding and counting pupal cases in differing conditions (i.e., in non-drought conditions where grass may be taller and pupa cases deteriorate faster in wet weather). Furthermore, predation or scavenging of pupa and cases by birds, such as *Gymnorhina tibicen* (Australian magpie) and *Corvus* spp. (ravens, crows), may occur unevenly across years or between sites.

During the breeding and flying season (mid-October to January), it is recommended that surveys be undertaken with suitable weather conditions for a minimum of four days at weekly intervals until flight is detected, with reduced intervals of three days when conditions are favourable and strong flight patterns are observed in nearby reference subpopulations, noting that this period is likely to vary depending upon the location of the survey area and the weather for that season. For this reason, reference sites with known golden sun moth presence may assist to indicate when moths are active. Survey methods may vary depending upon the focus of the particular survey, e.g., establishing presence, estimating abundance, recording pupal cases, etc (see DEWHA 2009; Richter et at. 2013b; SWIFFT 2025 for more information concerning survey methods).

Standardised survey methods have been developed for the closely related Western Australian (WA) species, *Synemon gratiosa* (graceful sun moth) (Bishop et al. 2010a,b). Given the ecology and behaviour of the graceful sun moth is very similar to that of the golden sun moth, these standardised methods may be applicable, noting and taking into account the difference between the species’ emergence and breeding times.

Landscape predictors of occurrence, such as aspect, landscape position, larval food plant species richness, and local climate conditions may also be effective to identify suitable locations for surveys of subpopulations (Kutt et al. 2016). This should be considered alongside the survey methodology outlined above and may benefit from aerial surveys to identify areas of suitable habitat that have not been adequately surveyed on foot.

### 2.7 Threats

The golden sun moth is under threat from habitat loss, fragmentation and degradation, primarily from human disturbance (urban development, agricultural activities) and weed invasion. Further, the effects of climate change may be significant, and inappropriate fire regimes are likely to be impacting on the species.

#### 2.7.1 Habitat loss, degradation and fragmentation

The golden sun moth was once widespread in temperate south eastern Australia (Clarke & O’Dwyer 2000). The native grassland habitat of the species has been significantly reduced and fragmented by agriculture and urban expansion, with less than 1% of the approximately 2 million ha existing prior to European settlement remaining (Clarke & O’Dwyer 2000). The sites at which the golden sun moth still occurs are generally small and isolated, due to the species’ highly fragmented distribution and limited dispersal ability, and are not secure from future development (DEWHA 2009; ACT Government 2017a). The species is subject to further habitat loss through both direct and indirect impacts of urban and agricultural development.

Some native grassland sites supporting the species have been listed as Threatened Ecological Communities under the EPBC Act (see 1.3 Legislative, policy and planning context, above) as well as relevant states (noting that state/territory listed ecological communities may not be equivalent to those listed under the EPBC Act). This provides additional protection for golden sun moth habitat.

##### 2.7.1.1 Soil disturbance

Physical soil disturbance can be destructive to golden sun moth habitat. Soil disturbance occurs through activities such as ploughing and cultivation, ripping rabbit burrows, laying infrastructure, driving vehicles and machinery on wet soil, and grazing by hooved animals (ACT Government 2017b). Soil disturbance destroys the subterranean early stages of the species’ life cycle and also kills host plants (Douglas 2004).

##### 2.7.1.2 Lack of biomass removal

The absence of biomass removal (e.g., from grazing, mowing, slashing, fire) results in denser, closed groundcover from tall perennial grasses. One way this can happen is when areas historically used for livestock grazing are converted to cropping land or abandoned due to being on urban fringes. Similarly, in times of high biomass there may not be enough grazing activity occurring to maintain the low biomass required by the species. Low growing wallaby grasses can become shaded and eventually choked out where this occurs (van Praagh 2004), resulting in areas becoming unsuitable for golden sun moth. Periods of prolonged biomass accumulation are likely to significantly impact subpopulations, given the estimated 2–3-year lifespan of the species. Additionally, grass cuttings left onsite following slashing/mowing may act as mulch (ACT Government 2017b), adding nitrates to the soil and promoting weed growth. This can change the structure of grassland sites, limiting areas of bare ground for basking and potentially making them unsuitable for the golden sun moth.

##### 2.7.1.3 Commercial grazing and/or mowing (in urban areas)

Excessive biomass removal through overgrazing by domestic stock or close mowing in urban areas or for hay production can cause soil compaction and reduce the health and root volume of native grasses. This can lower habitat quality and possibly expose eggs and/or larvae to excessive soil temperatures (ACT Government 2017a). Grazing and mowing also has the potential to introduce weeds and can prevent plant flowering and seed production if undertaken too frequently or at the wrong time of year (Barlow 1998).

Conversely, undergrazing can result in habitat degradation due to overgrowth of surrounding vegetation. Moderate, managed grazing has been shown to be beneficial to the species (Gibbons & Reid 2013). Grazing (or mowing) regimes are useful to maintain short swards of grass and open spaces between tussocks, thereby preventing habitat degradation and maintaining habitat suitability for golden sun moth.

##### 2.7.1.4 Application of chemicals such as herbicides, pesticides and fertilisers

Glyphosate and flupropanate have been shown to have a detrimental effect on established wallaby grass and are therefore not recommended for use at sites dominated by wallaby grass. Use of glyphosate and flupropanate within or adjacent to golden sun moth habitat could therefore have negative impacts on the integrity of habitat through off-target spraying and spray drift. Established wallaby grasses have demonstrated tolerance to other herbicides (e.g., diclofop-methyl, simazine, fenoxaprop-ethyl, and diuron which target broad-leaf weeds and annual grasses) and these are therefore preferable for use (Lodge & McMillan 1994). These specific herbicides to which wallaby grass is tolerant should be used when spraying for weeds or annual grasses in golden sun moth habitat. All available herbicides that kill perennial grasses impact on wallaby grass, so targeted, careful management, such as spot spraying or hand removal, needs to be utilised. Care needs to be exercised when undertaking spot spraying to prevent spray drift.

The addition or run-off of fertilisers into golden sun moth habitat is likely to favour exotic groundcover species over native larval food plants for the species. Specifically, phosphorus fertilisers have been shown to inhibit growth of wallaby grasses (Lodge & McMillan 1994; Lodge et al. 1994). Fertilisers therefore have the potential to change the species composition of habitat in a manner that is unfavourable for the golden sun moth (ACT Government 2017a).

Additional to the impacts of chemicals on habitat, pesticide (insecticide) use may also cause direct mortality to individuals or subpopulations of the golden sun moth.

##### 2.7.1.5 Planting and/or regeneration of shrubs/trees

Planting or regeneration of trees and/or shrubs can have a number of effects on golden sun moth habitat such as reducing the density of native grasses, shading, changing the species composition and habitat structure, and increasing the number of predatory animals by encouraging them to breed or forage within the site by providing perches and nesting opportunities (OEH 2012). The consequences of such actions vary, depending on the density of plantings/regeneration, with higher density plantings resulting in more serious consequences.

Furthermore, planting or regeneration of trees and/or shrubs will inherently increase the biomass of a given habitat and may lead to the conversion of grassland into a woodland, which will likely result in areas becoming unfavourable for the golden sun moth.

#### 2.7.2 Introduced flora

Native grasslands are under threat from various introduced pasture grasses and clovers which readily out-compete the native wallaby grasses and speargrasses that are characteristic of golden sun moth habitat (DEWHA 2009). As well as reduction or loss of larval host plants without which the species cannot persist, the invasion of weeds changes the structure, vegetation density, microclimate, surface and subsurface soil conditions and nutrient regimes of grasslands, which may also result in loss of habitat through the change from a patchy sward of short to medium height to a dense tall sward (ACT Government 2017a). Weeds posing significant threat to golden sun moth habitat across parts of its range include Phalaris (*Phalaris aquatica*), African lovegrass (*Eragrostis curvula*), paspalum (*Paspalum dilatatum*), oats (*Avena* spp.), St John’s wort (*Hypericum perforatum*), and various thistle species (ACT Government 2017a; NSW DCCEEW 2022; Agriculture Victoria 2025).

Little is known about whether golden sun moth utilise serrated tussock but large subpopulations of golden sun moth have been reported on sites with high infestations of Chilean needlegrass in the ACT and Victoria, including sites comprised completely of this exotic grass (New 2012; Richter et al. 2013b). Chilean needlegrass was introduced from South and Central America and is a distant relative of the native *Austrostipa* spp. (speargrasses) (DEWHA 2009). All golden sun moth sites in the ACT that are dominated by Chilean needlegrass are adjacent to native grasslands (Richter et al. 2011 cited in ACT Government 2017a), suggesting that the species has been able to persist in native grasslands that have suffered infestation of this weed or have been able to disperse into this habitat, from adjacent native habitats, following the establishment of the weed. It is also possible that these are peripheral subpopulations in relation to a larger metapopulation with a core node in the adjacent native grasslands; however, more research is needed to understand how golden sun moth utilise sites comprised completely of exotic grass.

Chilean needlegrass and serrated tussock are declared and listed as a WoNS by the Australian Government and landowners and land managers are responsible for managing them as per state and territory legislation/regulation. This results in a tension between management of WoNS and the possible presence of golden sun moth larvae that may feed on these weeds. Eradication of Chilean needlegrass and serrated tussock may be a management objective at some sites to achieve an overall ecological benefit, but this may result in some detriment to golden sun moth. Such activities should be undertaken in such a way as to minimise the negative effects on the moth, through approaches such as incremental removal of Chilean needlegrass and serrated tussock over time or ensuring the protection and viability of adjacent subpopulations in the metapopulation context. There has been little research into how best to do this and should be a focus for future recovery efforts for the golden sun moth.

#### 2.7.3 Habitat degradation by rabbits (Oryctolagus cuniculus)

Rabbits have the potential to impact on golden sun moth habitat through overgrazing and general damage to plants, preventing plant regeneration, altering ecological communities, and changing soil structure and nutrient cycling leading to significant erosion (DoEE 2016).

#### 2.7.4 Climate change

Climate change projections show that Australia’s climate will get hotter and drier. Time in drought is predicted to increase over southern Australia, with a greater frequency of severe droughts (Kirono et al. 2020), an increase in the number of dangerous fire weather days, and a longer fire season (CSIRO & BOM 2024). By 2050, the distribution of the golden sun moth is projected to experience an increase in average annual temperature (1.1 – 1.8 °C), an increase in the number of severe fire weather days (+0.2 – 1.2 days), an increase in dry months (up to 60%), a decrease in the number of cold nights per year (-16 – 32 days), a decrease in average annual rainfall (-8.7 – 13.9 %), and an increase in extreme rainfall events compared to a baseline of 1986–2005 for Victorian projections and a baseline of 1990–2009 for NSW and ACT projections (DEECA 2024; NSW DCCEEW 2024a,b).

The specialised life cycle and habitat preferences of the golden sun moth may indicate it is susceptible to the effects of climate change, such as increases in temperature and evaporation, changes in rainfall patterns, changes to hydrological flows, increased drought conditions, and impacts of more frequent grass fire. While there is little evidence available regarding the effect of long-term weather changes on the species, research has shown significant impacts caused by local weather conditions and extremes on golden sun moth distribution and abundance (Kutt et al. 2015, 2016). Surveys conducted in Victoria found there was a reduction in abundance and a spatial shift from nominally wet habitat to nominally dry habitat due to a shift from drought to above average rainfall (Kutt et al. 2015). This likely occurred due to a change in survivorship of pupae, reduction in oviposition sites and reduction in the emergence of larvae (Kutt et al. 2015). As the species prefers dry conditions, increased extreme rainfall events due to climate change may reduce the distribution and abundance of local subpopulations in flood prone habitats, despite the general drying trend.

Plants advantaged by climate change are likely to include C4 grasses (e.g., *Themeda triandra* (kangaroo grass)), which are not thought to be larval food plants. Some perennial grasses have C3 photosynthetic pathways, while others have C4 pathways. The C4 grasses have an additional pathway to capture carbon dioxide during photosynthesis and have higher temperature and light tolerance and lower moisture requirements in comparison to C3 grasses (Havrilla et al. 2022; DPIRD 2025). Additionally, weed species such as African lovegrass, which is highly competitive on the low-nutrient soils that are typical of drier native grasslands in the ACT, are likely to be at an advantage (Sharp 2011; ACT Government 2017a).

The impacts on the lifecycle of the species of temperature increases and altered environments as a result of climate change are not known and require further research.

#### 2.7.5 Inappropriate fire regimes

Little information is available on the impact of fire on the golden sun moth, though fire may impact grasses the larvae rely on for food. The species has been shown to withstand burning of its habitat at a site in Nhill, Victoria (Doughlas 2004; Biosis 2010b cited in ACT Government 2017a), and flying males were observed in higher numbers on a previously burned patch. It is unknown whether this was due to an attraction of males to areas of low herbage mass, larvae surviving the fire, or a reduction in the dominant kangaroo grass exposing or allowing growth of subdominant wallaby grasses (ACT Government 2016a). Depending on the nature of the fire and seed physiology, fire may help to reduce herbage mass and residual seed of some introduced grass and weed species. In the absence of a suitable grazing regime, low frequency fire likely poses a threat to the species’ persistence in areas of naturally higher biomass given their preference for low biomass areas. Patchy ecological burns are seen as a desirable method of herbage mass reduction in Victoria (Douglass 2004; Gibson 2007 cited in ACT Government 2017a), though sites in the ACT have been managed with grazing rather than fire (Edwards 1994 cited in ACT Government 2017a).

Fires at certain times of the year (primarily September to January) have a higher chance of killing adult golden sun moths and/or eggs, and smaller sites may risk local extirpation when faced with high severity fire during breeding times. Edwards (1994 cited in ACT Government 2017a) speculated that mobilisation of grass root reserves during resprouting following fire could create a larval food shortage.

The frequency and intensity of controlled burns needs to be planned, and the species and habitat monitored to better understand the effects of fire. Mosaic burns should be conducted outside the pupation and flight period (September to January), and may be most preferable in autumn, between March and April to facilitate recovery prior to emergence (ACT Government 2017a; EPSDD 2019). However, this may not always be feasible across the species’ distribution, and the risk of burning at inopportune times needs to be considered against the risk of not burning at all. Similarly, planned ecological or hazard reduction burns must consider the requirements of other EPBC Act listed species and broader landscape scale outcomes, which may result in trade-offs if there are conflicts with the most ideal burning regime for the golden sun moth. Some compromise may be achieved if mosaic burn patterns can be aligned with metapopulation structures, such that not all of the component populations are burnt within a given season.

#### 2.7.6 Increasing predation caused by installation of artificial structures

Predation of adult moths by predatory birds and insects may contribute significantly to adult mortality. Observations at one site showed as many as 30% of the moths observed were taken by predators (Clarke & O’Dwyer 2000). Predatory birds include the native *Rhipidura leucophyrs* (willie wagtail), *Hirundo neoxena* (welcome swallow) and *Grallina cyanoleuca* (magpie-lark), and the introduced common starling (*Sturnus vulgaris*) (Clarke & O’Dwyer 2000).

During monitoring undertaken at the Amberfield Grassland Reserve in Craigieburn, Victoria, willie wagtails were observed hunting in areas of the grassland where golden sun moths were most commonly sighted. The birds were seen to be using an internal farm-style fence to attack prey (which included golden sun moths) and then returning the fence, a behaviour defined as a ‘sally’ (Remsen Jnr & Robinson 1990 cited in Bainbridge & Longmore 2016). Willie wagtails were also observed using perimeter fences and other structures around the reserve as perches for hunting (Bainbridge & Longmore 2016). The common starling was also observed at this site apparently using perimeter structure to scrutinise their surroundings for prey, including the golden sun moth (Bainbridge & Longmore 2016). These observations support the idea that man-made structures could increase the level of predation at grassland sites by providing perches for hunting birds.

It should be noted that there is no baseline of predation on golden sun moths to compare this apparent increase caused by artificial structures and further research is required to determine if current rates are negatively impacting the species’ ability to persist.

## 3 Recovery planning

### 3.1 Conservation situation

The golden sun moth occurs across native grassland and grassy woodland areas which are under increasing development pressure, primarily from urban development and agricultural activities. While some of these grassland areas are in protected conservation reserves, most areas containing golden sun moth subpopulations are not. Some of the grasslands in which the golden sun moth occurs are part of Threatened Ecological Communities that are listed under the EPBC Act and/or state/territory legislation. The golden sun moth has a restricted flying period and is therefore not visible for much of its lifecycle, so that it may be overlooked when considering development proposals and grassland habitat retention and/or protection.

Surveys for golden sun moth and knowledge of golden sun moth habitat has increased substantially, primarily because of requirements to consider development impacts on the species, as well as the increasing impacts of invasive species, fire and climate change on grasslands. Where developments are likely to result in significant impacts on the golden sun moth, habitat areas are more likely to be set aside for protection, and there is an increasing interest in finding ways to enable co-existence between this species and agricultural activities, such as grazing.

Recovery of the golden sun moth requires acknowledgement of these pressures and impacts, and consideration of opportunities to protect habitat, improve habitat quality and connectivity, and manage and reduce threats.

### 3.2 Biodiversity benefits

As the majority of actions required to conserve and manage populations of the golden sun moth involve protection and management of native grasslands and grassy woodlands, they may also have considerable synergistic benefits for other species and communities (both flora and fauna) inhabiting these habitats. The extent of these benefits is likely to be determined by the type of management regime on sites, which ideally allow for a range of management approaches (i.e., mosaic approach, to provide appropriate habitat for multiple species). The modelled distribution of the golden sun moth overlaps with other listed threatened species[2] such as *Tympanocryptis pinguicolla* (Victorian grassland earless dragon) – Critically Endangered, *Tympanocryptis lineata* (Canberra grassland earless dragon) – Critically Endangered, *Delma impar* (striped legless lizard) - Vulnerable*, Aprasia parapulchella* (pink-tailed worm lizard) – Vulnerable, *Prasophyllum petilum* (Tarengo leek orchid) – Endangered, *Ammobium craspedioides* (Yass daisy) – Vulnerable and *Rutidosis leptorrhynchoides* (button wrinklewort) – Endangered.

Habitat for the golden sun moth occurs within five EPBC-listed Threatened Ecological Communities, all of which are either Critically Endangered or Endangered. Each of these Threatened Ecological Communities has conservation planning documents that include conservation actions that complement recovery actions for the golden sun moth:

*Approved Conservation Advice for the Grey Box* (Eucalyptus microcarpa) *Grassy Woodlands and Derived Native Grasslands of South-east Australia* (DEWHA 2010).

*Approved Conservation Advice for Natural Grasslands of the Murray Valley Plains ecological community* (DSEWPaC 2012).

*Approved Conservation Advice (including listing advice) for Natural Temperate Grassland of the South Eastern Highlands (EC 152)* (TSSC 2016).

*Approved Conservation Advice for the Natural Temperate Grassland of the Victorian Volcanic Plain* (DEWHA 2008).

*Conservation Advice for the White Box – Yellow Box – Blakely’s Red Gum Grassy Woodlands and Derived Native Grasslands* (DCCEEW 2023).

*National Recovery Plan for the White Box – Yellow Box – Blakely’s Red Gum Grassy Woodlands and Derived Native Grasslands* (NSW DECCW 2010).

### 3.3 Vision

Avoid any decline of the golden sun moth population with habitat maintained and increased to an extent that the species is no longer eligible for listing as threatened under the EPBC Act.

### 3.4 Objectives

Four objectives support progress towards the long-term vision. These objectives should be evaluated as implementation occurs, and particularly at five years and 10 years from beginning implementation, to consider the effectiveness of recovery actions, and the extent to which performance criteria are being met and objectives are being achieved.

**Objective 1:** The distribution and habitat occupancy of the golden sun moth are better understood.

**Objective 2:** Maximise the number of golden sun moth metapopulations that are represented in secure conservation reserves or protected by conservation mechanisms.

**Objective 3:** Golden sun moth habitat requirements (both native and non-native) are better understood, quality is improved, fragmentation is reduced, and connectivity is increased.

**Objective 4:** Threats to golden sun moth are appropriately managed to reduce their impact on golden sun moth metapopulations and habitat.

### 3.5 Performance criteria

Performance criterion 1: Within 10 years, surveys, monitoring and research activities provide a more complete understanding of golden sun moth distribution and habitat occupancy.

Performance criterion 2: Within 10 years, an increased number of golden sun moth metapopulations are represented in secure conservation reserves or protected by other conservation mechanisms.

Performance criterion 3a: Over the next 10 years, there is a net positive annual increase in connectivity between golden sun moth subpopulations.

Performance criterion 3b: Over the next 10 years, there is a net positive annual increase in habitat quality for golden sun moth metapopulations.

Performance criterion 4a: Over the next 10 years, there is a net positive annual increase in habitat quality and extent for golden sun moth across its national distribution.

Performance criterion 4b: Within 10 years, impacts of invasive species on golden sun moth habitat, including those the species has been shown to coexist with, are better understood, managed and reduced.

Given these factors and consideration, recovery actions (Table 3) in this Recovery Plan are presented as a ‘menu’ approach that aligns with the four main objectives of the Recovery Plan. The intention of this approach is to enable recovery actions to be applied flexibly and adaptively, according to relevant considerations and influences at each site or subpopulation of the species.
