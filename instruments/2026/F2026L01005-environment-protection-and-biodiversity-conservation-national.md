## 1. Introduction

This National Recovery Plan for the ghost bat (Macroderma gigas) has been prepared under Part 13, Division 5 of the *Environment Protection and Biodiversity Conservation Act 1999* (EPBC Act). This is the first national recovery plan for the ghost bat. It sets out research, protection and management actions to stop the decline and support recovery of this species.

At the time of listing this species under the EPBC Act in May 2016, a Conservation Advice was published to guide its protection and conservation. Research and management actions have been undertaken since, including the development of improved survey techniques, targeted surveys, genetic sampling and analysis, research on movement patterns, and implementation of tailored fire management programs. These activities have led to the identification of new sites where the species occurs and greater understanding of population sizes in some areas.

### 1.1 Conservation status

The ghost bat is listed as Vulnerable under the Commonwealth EPBC Act (TSSC 2016). It is also listed as Vulnerable on the IUCN Red List of Threatened Species and in Western Australia (*Biodiversity Conservation Act 2016*); Endangered in Queensland (*Nature Conservation Act 1992*) and South Australia (*National Parks and Wildlife Act 1972*); and Near Threatened in the Northern Territory (*Territory Parks and Wildlife Conservation Act 1976*) but under assessment for uplisting at the time of the making of this plan.

### 1.2 Recovery interests

Ghost bats are known to occur across a wide range of land tenures and uses including, but not limited to, agricultural lands and pastoral leases, Indigenous Protected Areas (IPAs) and other lands managed by First Nations peoples, private conservation reserves, mining and exploration leases and tenements, and national parks and other conservation reserves. For this reason, a collaborative approach between land managers, governments, First Nations people, researchers and conservation organisations is vital to progressing recovery actions for this species.

Key recovery partners include, but are not limited to:

 The Ghost Bat Recovery Team

 Ghost bat experts and researchers

 Landholders (including the mining, agricultural/horticultural, pastoral and forestry sectors)

 First Nations groups (including organisations, people and ranger groups)

 Land managers, including managers of national parks and other conservation reserves, private leases and Commonwealth lands (e.g. Parks Australia)

 Local governments

 Conservation organisations

 National Resource Management (NRM) groups

 Planners, managers and environmental regulators

 Ecological/environmental consultants

 Tourism operators

 Recreational visitors to caves

 Local community and the broader public

### 1.3 Cultural significance

The cultural, customary and spiritual significance of ghost bats to First Nations peoples are diverse and varied across the species’ range but not well documented. This section describes some published examples of the cultural significance of ghost bats.

In central arid areas (WA, NT, SA, Qld), the ghost bat has been documented in caves in the Rawlinson and Kathleen Ranges and has an important role in ceremonies (Churchill & Helman 1990). Local names for the species in this area include *Irlkintjiri* (Luritja), *Warramurrungka* (Manytjilytjarra), *Tjalku-tjalku* (Ngaatjatjarra and Pitjantjantjarra), *Warramurrungka* (Ngangamarta), *Tjalku-tjalku* (Pintupi) and *Warramurrungka* (Warnman) (Burbidge et al 1988). In the Top End, the ghost bat is central to the Wagiman Warlang (ghost bat) dreaming story (Murphy et al. 2025). Natural roosts used by ghost bats may also be culturally important to First Nations people.

First Nations people are vital partners in the recovery of the ghost bat. Large areas of ghost bat habitat, including many roosts, are found on lands managed and/or owned by Indigenous rangers and custodians. This includes a substantial network of Indigenous Protected Areas (35 individual IPAs as at 2026) across the modelled distribution of the ghost bat. Supporting engagement of First Nations peoples in the protection and recovery of this species is a priority action of this plan. This includes through:

 developing and implementing land management activities to support ghost bat recovery on land managed by, or associated with, First Nations peoples, including linkages to Healthy Country Plans;

 promoting opportunities to increase land management capacity through sharing of cultural and scientific knowledge;

 documenting new records of ghost bats;

 two-way science and knowledge sharing; and

 developing and implementing research projects on land managed by, or associated with, First Nations peoples.

## 2. Species information

### 2.1 Taxonomy

The species is accepted as *Macroderma gigas* (Dobson, 1880). The ghost bat is the only living representative of the genus *Macroderma* and the only extant member of the family Megadermatidae in Australasia (Hand 1996). Although endemic to Australia, close living relatives of the ghost bat occur in Asia and Africa (Miller 1906). The ghost bat was described by Dobson as *Megaderma gigas* from an individual captured at Mount Margaret Station, Wilson’s River, Queensland (Finlayson 1958), with the genus *Macroderma* subsequently erected for this species by Miller (1906). The fossil record shows that ancestral ghost bat species (*Macroderma)* have been part of Australian ecosystems for more than 25 million years (Hand 1996; Armstrong et al. 2020). Douglas (1962) recognised the Kimberley population as distinct and described it as a separate subspecies, *Macroderma gigas saturata*; however, the subspecies is currently treated as a junior synonym of *M. gigas* (*fide* Jackson & Groves 2015).

### 2.2 Description

The ghost bat, previously known as the Australian false vampire bat, is a large, facultative echolocating bat with adults typically weighing 100–172 g, measuring 10–13 cm (head-body length), and a wingspan of 64–76 cm (Armstrong *et al* 2023, Churchill 2008). Females are generally larger than males. The ghost bat is the third-largest echolocating bat in the world (Armstrong *et al.* 2023).

The species is readily recognised by its large ears, which are joined by a band of skin across the head, a forked tragus, large eyes, a long simple noseleaf, a prominent lower lip and no tail (Armstrong *et al* 2023). Fur colour ranges from light grey to light brown to rufous, with a paler underside (Hanrahan 2020; Figure 1).

### 2.3 Distribution

The ghost bat occurs in the Pilbara and Kimberley regions of Western Australia (WA), the Top End of the Northern Territory (NT), northwestern Queensland (Qld) and adjoining areas of the NT, and from Cape York to Rockhampton in eastern Qld (Figure 2). It is presumed to be extinct from the central arid areas (Churchill & Helman 1990), and from the Boodjamulla/Riversleigh area in Qld (White et al. 2016). The type specimen may have historically been erroneously attributed to Mount Margaret Station near Eromanga in southwestern Qld (Churchill & Helman 1990). Fossil records show the ghost bat’s distribution has contracted north over approximately 12,000 years (Hudson & Wilson 1986; Churchill & Helman 1990).

For the purpose of recovery and management, the ghost bat’s range has been split into six geographic regions. These regions comprise Eastern Queensland (Qld), Gulf-Inland Qld (NT-Qld), Top End (NT), Kimberley (WA), Pilbara (WA) and Central Arid (SA-WA-NT-Qld).

These broad regions share similarities in landscape attributes, threats, and recovery partners. Splitting the species’ distribution into regions improves the usability of this plan by providing a framework for communicating region-specific information (ecology, populations, roost sites, critical habitat), as well as variation in threat risk (as required under s270(2)(j) of the EPBC Act). The regions also help present regional variation in the actions required to halt the decline and support the recovery and long-term survival of the ghost bat. They may also guide the identification of the most appropriate sites for long-term monitoring across the species’ range.

To provide context to the location of roost sites and known populations, the regions are described below, noting that there may be overlap between some regions (e.g. Kimberley and the Top End). Due to remoteness and/or geological complexity across the ghost bat’s range, comprehensive surveys have not been undertaken across all regions. As actions in this plan are implemented, new roosts or populations may become known.

#### Pilbara (WA)

Following the IBRA bioregion boundary (Interim Biogeographic Regionalisation for Australia (IBRA) version 7, Cth DCCEEW 2025), the Pilbara region extends from the Indian Ocean coast, inland across the Hamersley and Chichester Ranges to the interior of WA east of Newman. The Pilbara is rugged and has a semi‑arid climate. It is characterised by low mountain ranges, notably the Hamersley and Chichester Ranges, which provide complex topography and a network of sites suitable for roosting. Extensive rocky terrain, including banded ironstone formations and granite rockpiles, often contain deep natural cave systems that have suitable microclimates for use by ghost bats. The vegetation generally comprises mulga woodlands, open low eucalypt woodlands, mixed *Acacia* shrublands and hummock (spinifex) grasslands that support foraging of ghost bats.

The Pilbara region contains an isolated population that contributes independently to the long-term persistence of the ghost bat. It is a genetically distinct population (DBCA unpublished data). The largest colonies were historically found at six gold and copper mines in the East Pilbara (Chichester); however, two of these sites no longer exist and the remaining four are at risk of collapsing (Cramer et al. 2022). No other large roosts are known in the region. Instead, colonies in natural roosts in the Chichester and the Hamersley Ranges are often small, with groups consisting of less than 10 individuals and with occupancy heavily fluctuating between years (Ottewell et al 2017). Drivers of occupancy in natural roosts are not well understood. The population size for the region is thought to be approximately 2,000 individuals, albeit this figure is yet to be confirmed by empirical data (TSSC 2016).

The Pilbara region is a priority area for economic development, including mining, pastoralism and renewable energy development. The majority of habitat that is used by the ghost bat for roosting and/or foraging is in areas that are being explored or developed for mining (Shaw et al. in prep). Additionally, knowledge of ghost bat distribution and ecology in the region is derived from research and monitoring efforts associated with mining activities, with most recorded roosts, particularly in the Hamersley Ranges, known from mining tenements. Only 0.6% of known ghost bat roosts are on the conservation estate. While habitat modelling indicates suitable roosting habitat is present in Karijini National Park, it is less likely to occur within the Millstream Chichester National Park (Shaw et al. in prep).

Ghost bats are also found in areas adjacent to the Pilbara bioregion, such as Karlamilyi National Park in the Little Sandy Desert bioregion, and near the Barlee Range Nature Reserve in the Ashburton bioregion.

#### Kimberley (WA)

The Kimberley region encompasses the area from the Mueller Ranges northwards to the north coast, through to Derby in the west and the WA-NT border in the east. The Kimberley region is characterised by a tropical-arid mix of habitats and cave‑forming geology that provides suitable microclimates for ghost bat roosting. It is characterised by wet-dry seasonal cycles, extensive sandstone and limestone ranges and plateaus, monsoon rainforest, vine thickets, and savanna woodlands. It also includes islands on which ghost bats have been recorded (McKenzie and Bullen 2012). The Kimberley differs from the nearby Pilbara region in that it is more humid, tropical, tidal and more vegetated.

The ghost bat has been detected across rocky landscapes and islands of the Kimberley region (McKenzie et al. 2019), but its distribution is still poorly known, population estimates are largely inferred, and threats are not well understood.

#### Top End (NT)

The Top End region in the NT encompasses the area north of Larrimah and up to the northern coast of the NT. Groote Eylandt is included in the Top End region due to potential interconnectivity with the Arnhem coast. No genetic analysis has been conducted on Groote Eylandt ghost bats and further studies may indicate the area as a distinct region.

The Top End region is characterised by rugged, geologically complex terrains such as sandstone escarpments and gorges (e.g. Arnhem Land, and Kakadu, Nitmiluk, Keep River, and Litchfield national parks) and karst systems (e.g. Tindall Limestone and Judbarra (Gregory) National Park) that support roost habitat. Historic mine workings in some areas provide roosting habitat for ghost bats. Savanna woodlands and riparian areas provide common foraging habitat for ghost bats (Tidemann *et al* 1985; Ruykys *et al* 2026).

The Top End region contributes significantly to the long-term persistence of the ghost bat, with relatively high abundance in some areas and likely connectivity across the region (Worthington Wilmer 1997; Hanrahan *et al* 2026). In the Pine Creek area, ghost bats are predominantly found in mine workings and the largest known maternity roost is in an unstable disused mine. Other large colonies are known from within Litchfield and Kakadu national parks. Elsewhere in the region, ghost bats roost in caves in sandstone escarpment and limestone systems.

#### Gulf-Inland Queensland (Qld)

The Gulf–Inland Qld region extends from the Gulf of Carpentaria in the Northern Territory, across the Qld border and south‑eastward to approximately 150 km south of Cloncurry. The landscape spans dry tropical to semi‑arid inland environments, forming a broad mosaic of habitat types across the region.

The area is characterised by tropical savanna, hot semi‑arid terrain and arid inland plains, with prominent geological features including sandstone escarpments, rugged gorges and extensive dolomitic limestone cave systems. These landforms occur across key sites in the region: the sandstone plateaus of Pungalina‑Seven Emu Sanctuary, the fossil‑rich limestone karst of Riversleigh, the Cambrian dolomitic cave systems of Camooweal and the naturally cavernous and mined terrains around Cloncurry and Mount Isa, which are dominated by weathered rocky outcrops and mineralised formations.

Vegetation communities across the region include eucalypt woodlands, riparian vegetation, Mitchell grass grasslands, littoral grasslands and freshwater wetlands. In northern coastal areas such as around the Gulf, the landscape also supports mangrove‑lined estuarine and coastal systems.

The status and distribution of the ghost bat in the Gulf-Inland Qld region is poorly understood. Infrequent monitoring has shown declines in some areas and likely extinction in the Riversleigh/Boodjamulla National Park area.

#### Eastern Queensland (Qld)

The Eastern Qld region spans from the McIlwraith Range on Cape York Peninsula south through the Wet Tropics, west to approximately Hughenden in the dry tropics, and further south to Rockhampton on the central Qld coast. It contains an exceptionally broad environmental gradient and roost diversity—sea caves, limestone and sandstone cave systems, granitic boulders, volcanic/lava‑tube formations and disused mines. The vegetation is also mixed and includes rainforests, semi-evergreen vine thickets, open woodlands and grasslands that support ghost bat populations that vary in size and security.

Within this region, Cape York contains some of Queensland’s largest and most secure colonies, including the state’s largest known colony at Alwal National Park (Cape York Peninsula Aboriginal Land). Most known roosts occur in remote rocky ranges with limited human disturbance and no subregion-wide decline is evident.

Confidence levels in data on ghost bat occurrence in this region varies considerably. Distribution, trends and connectivity are unresolved due to sparse, infrequent surveys across a vast landscape. Recent work has confirmed multiple large, genetically disjunct colonies and newly documented roosts but major data gaps persist—particularly around Mitchell Palmer, the McIlwraith Range and Cape Melville, where some colonies are known only from incidental records.

Ghost bat colonies in north-eastern Qld (wet and dry tropics from Mt Carbine south to Hervey Range, and west to approximately Hughenden) are poorly known. The landscape is rugged, often well-vegetated and consequently under-surveyed. There are no confirmed large colonies equivalent to those in Cape York and most historical records are scattered and unvalidated. Potential roosting structures are widespread (granite/volcanic ranges, small sandstone outcrops, lava tubes, caves and old mines) but their presence, persistence, and connectivity remain largely unknown.

On the central Qld coast, two small, isolated populations persist at Mt Etna and Cape Hillsborough, with no recent connectivity between the sites or to populations further north. Mt Etna declined and then stabilised at very low numbers (&lt;50 individuals); Cape Hillsborough also appears small but stable. Both require ongoing protection and targeted recovery given their size, isolation and exposure to local pressures. Fossil records from the Mt Etna-Capricorn Caves complex indicate occupation of the area by rainforest‑associated faunas from at least ~500,000 years ago followed by shifts to more arid‑adapted communities as the climate dried during the late Pleistocene (Laurikainen Gaete et al. 2025); these deposits include ghost bats throughout the entire period where much of the other fauna transitioned from New Guinea‑like rainforest biotas (e.g., cassowaries, striped possums) to more xeric assemblages (e.g., greater bilbies, hopping mice).

#### Central Arid (WA, NT, SA, Qld)

The Central Arid region includes the Mt Margaret area west of Quilpie in Qld—the type locality for the species—as well as parts of the Central Desert in the NT. The NT subregion runs from the Dulcie Range National Park through Tjoritja/West MacDonnell National Park, to Uluru and extends approximately 50 km into SA and west to Warburton (Milyirrtjarra) in WA. The environment is arid to semi-arid, dominated by rocky ranges and desert sand and gibber plains, with scattered limestone and sandstone cave systems, abandoned mine shafts and deep crevices in ranges that historically provided roosting and foraging habitat.

Ghost bats have not been detected at Mt Margaret since the 1960s or from the Central Arid region since the 1940 to 1970s (Churchill & Helman 1990), noting these dates do not incorporate knowledge held by First Nations peoples. There are records from the mid-1800s to the mid-1900s across the southern part of the NT, the far north-west of SA and close to the NT borders with WA and Qld. Undertaking an extinction probability assessment (IUCN 2024) is worthwhile, because the species has not been detected in the Central Arid region in at least 40 years. No other recovery actions specific to the region are included in this plan.

### 2.4 Important populations

Woinarski et al. (2014) estimated the national population size of the ghost bat to be fewer than 10,000 mature individuals. While there are significant knowledge gaps in relation to distribution across the species’ range, ghost bats are listed as threatened due to declines in multiple populations in the Pilbara and Kimberley (WA), Kakadu National Park (NT) and Mt Etna, Cape Hillsborough, Camooweal and Kings Plains (Qld) (TSSC 2016). Consequently, all known populations of ghost bat are considered important.

Objectives and actions outlined in this plan seek to improve knowledge of ghost bat distribution as well as to better understand the species’ population demographics. This information will help inform recovery actions so that ghost bat populations stabilise or increase in the long term.

## 3. Biology and ecology

### 3.1 Diet

The ghost bat feeds on a wide variety of prey, including large insects, birds, small mammals (including other bat species), reptiles and frogs (Douglas 1967; Tidemann et al. 1985; Toop 1985; Pettigrew et al. 1986; Schulz 1986; Boles 1999; Johnston et al. 2015; Arteaga Claramunt et al. 2019; Start et al. 2019). While the composition of the ghost bat’s diet differs amongst studies (principally ratio of invertebrate to vertebrate prey), most studies do not include assessment of seasonal or demographic differences in diet. It is therefore not possible to make meaningful comparisons on diet variability between regions (Ghost Bat Recovery Team pers. comm. 17 March 2026). The exception to this is Toop (1985) who assessed diet seasonality, but whose work was restricted to Mt Etna, Qld.

The diet of the ghost bat is likely to vary over time and across its distribution, in line with the variability in distribution and abundance of prey species, as well as with the species’ seasonal nutritional needs. For example, Toop (1985) reported that the main prey of the Mt Etna population (in the Eastern Qld region) during warmer months was large insects, whereas in cooler months the prey were mostly birds (e.g. owlet nightjar, little shrike-thrush, Lewin’s honeyeater) and mammals (e.g. house mouse, common sheath-tailed bat).

### 3.2 Foraging behaviour

The ghost bat's main foraging strategy is perch-and-ambush, with the species often ambushing prey whilst hanging from trees (Tidemann et al. 1985; Toop 1985; Bat Call WA 2021). Individuals locate prey while remaining stationary at a vantage point, restricting flight activity to short flights in order to capture prey and/or to change vantage point (Tidemann et al. 1985). Individuals move to another vantage point if the previous one was unproductive. The ghost bat may also search for prey by flying low to the ground (1–2 m) (McKenzie & Bullen 2012; Augusteyn et al. 2018). Prey is captured on the ground, in vegetation or while in flight, then carried to a high point in a tree, rock overhang or inside a roost to be eaten (Douglas 1967; Tidemann et al. 1985; Kulzer et al. 1984; Armstrong et al. 2023). Tracking data (VHF, GPS and PIT tag) also shows that they do not necessarily forage throughout the night; instead, they may forage in bouts. In the middle of the night, individuals often perch in trees or rock overhangs for prolonged periods or return to the roost, leaving the roost again to forage close to dawn (Tidemann et al. 1985; Ryan Ellis pers. comm. 17 March 2026; Nicola Hanrahan pers. comm. 17 March 2026).

### 3.3 Sensory perception and sociality

The ghost bat can use both echolocation and vision to navigate but does not always echolocate (Douglas 1967; Guppy et al. 1985). The species has excellent night vision and owl-like hearing (Douglas 1967; Guppy & Coles 1988) that are attuned to detecting movement and noise respectively (Kulzer et al. 1984). Individuals communicate through at least 12 distinct types of social calls (chirp-trills, squabbles, grumbles, whistles, ‘ultrasonic social’ calls etc.), often at 5–15 kHz but also up to 70 kHz (Guppy et al. 1985; McKenzie & Bullen 2012; Hanrahan 2020). Social calls are made when feeding close to each other; in roosts, particularly during peak activity times around sunset and sunrise; during mating and when raising pups; by males when defending territory; and when fighting or in distress (Douglas 1967; Tidemann et al. 1985; Hanrahan et al. 2021; 2022).

The ghost bat usually roosts communally but bats roosting singly have been observed, particularly during the dispersal period (outside of the mating period). Colony size is highly variable with most colonies, excluding regionally important roosts in the Pilbara, numbering 1–10 individuals (Ryan Ellis pers. comm. 22 March 2026); in the Top End 5–100 individuals is common (Nicola Hanrahan pers. comm. 22 March 2026). On occasion in certain roosts, colonies can comprise several hundred to more than 1,000 individuals (Armstrong et al. 2023).

### 3.4 Reproductive ecology

The ghost bat is an obligate cave-breeder with a slow reproductive rate. According to Hoyle et al. (2001), 40% of females breed in their second year (therefore, at one year old), but the majority of females (&gt;90% breeding) breed in their third year (therefore, two years old).

Males and females congregate in mating roosts during the mating season (Hanrahan et al. 2021) and females are pregnant for a minimum of 11–12 weeks (Churchill 2008), with a nearly 6-month gestation at the Mt Etna population (Eastern Queensland) recorded by Toop (1985). A study of reproductive hormones in captive pregnant bats (in Perth Zoo) reported a gestation length of 120–172 days (17.1–24.5 weeks), measured from the time the breeding male was removed from the enclosure until the females gave birth (Keeley et al. 2023). Keeley et al. (2023) suggest that this may indicate gestation length is more variable than previously reported and that ghost bats may use adaptive reproductive strategies (e.g., sperm storage, delayed implantation or embryonic diapause) to extend gestation when conditions are unfavourable.

Pregnant females congregate in maternity roosts (Toop 1985; Hall et al. 1997), which is often the same roost used for mating, with each female typically giving birth to a single pup. For the first approximately three-four weeks, she carries the pup while foraging at night but later leaves it in a crèche in a maternity roost (Toop 1985; Armstrong et al. 2023). The mother may move her pup to a crèche in a different roost during its development (Toop 1985). Juvenile ghost bats fly independently at approximately seven weeks after birth (Toop 1985) but continue suckling milk and remain with their mothers for another two months (Toop 1985).

The timing of breeding is poorly understood and varies across the distribution of the ghost bat (Table 2) with animals in the northern parts of the range breeding earlier in the year than those in southern parts (Ghost Bat Recovery Team pers. comm. 17 March 2026).

### 3.5 Longevity

Wild individuals have been redetected from genotyping of scats after 10 years (Ryan Ellis, unpublished data) and longevity in captivity has been recorded up to 22 years (Weigl 2005). Woinarski et al. (2014) infer that the generation time is approximately 8 years.

### 3.6 Activity

The ghost bat is active year-round, foraging most nights and roosting each day. Individuals may rest during the night between feeding bouts (Tidemann et al. 1985). The ghost bat usually emerges from its roost within a few hours after dark and seeks to roost again sometime before sunrise (Douglas 1967; Tidemann et al. 1985; Augusteyn et al. 2018; Bullen et al. 2023). However, this is variable, with some individuals not departing until midnight and others returning before midnight and remaining in the roost (Bullen et al. 2023; Ghost Bat Recovery Team pers. comm. 2 October 2025). Foraging individuals may also return to a roost during the night before departing to forage again (Augusteyn et al. 2018; Bullen et al. 2023).

### 3.7 Movement and dispersal

The ghost bat is physically capable of high mobility but individuals tend to have high fidelity to both their roost and foraging area (Bullen et al. 2023; Ruykys et al. 2026). Although ghost bats can fly long distances and quickly within a short period of time, individuals generally use the same roost or small number of roosts repeatedly and use the same foraging area over multiple nights. This means that an individual ghost bat is reliant on a particular area providing it with sufficient resources for both roosting and foraging.

The movement and dispersal of ghost bats is poorly understood. This is complicated by movement patterns that vary across the species’ range, including seasonally in response to roost humidity, temperature and water levels, the reproductive cycle of the ghost bat, and probably also the availability and abundance of prey. For example, the Mt Etna population (in the Central Qld Coast region) converges on a cave for warmth during the cool season and females remain aggregated to give birth as the weather begins to warm. This population also converges on a dry cave during the later part of the wet season. At other times, individuals (predominantly males) can disperse up to 150 km from the colony during winter (Toop 1985).

Regarding roost fidelity, the most comprehensive information available is for the Pilbara region. Here, repeat detections of individuals via scat samples from across the region over multiple years suggest individual ghost bats generally switch between 1–4 roosts that are in proximity (usually within 1–10 km), with many recaptures occurring at the same roost (Prada et al. 2024). Recaptures based on genomic analyses of scats have also occurred at longer ranges (&gt;20 km) but are less common, albeit detections are constrained by sampling, as it occurs in relation to mining tenements (Prada et al. 2024).

Broader patterns derived from analyses of genetic structure show that females show fidelity to their colony and (especially natal) area, with gene flow mainly facilitated by dispersal of males (Worthington Wilmer et al. 1994; Umbrello et al. 2025). This is supported by spatial autocorrelation analyses showing that females within 30 km are more genetically similar than males within the same distance (Umbrello et al. 2025).

Regarding nightly foraging, individuals are physically capable of travelling far and fast but tend not to do so. The currently recorded commuting flight speed for GPS-tracked wild ghost bats is approximately 17–22 km/h (Bullen et al. 2023; Ruykys et al. 2026). This accords with data from two captive individuals that had a ‘maximum cruise speed’ (the maximum steady-state speed that could be sustained for medium to long periods without appreciable acceleration or slowing) of 32 km/h and a ‘maximum spurt speed’ (the maximum speed that can be maintained for short periods) of 38.9 km/h (Bullen et al. 2016). Another study in Qld also reported a maximum commuting speed of 31.9 km/h (E. Williams pers. comm. 25 February 2026). However, such commuting speeds do not necessarily represent ‘standard’ movements: a study in Katherine in the Top End region found the mean distance travelled per hour was 1.2 km (Ruykys et al. 2026). This is consistent with the species’ perch-and-ambush foraging strategy, in which individuals stay in one location for extended periods of time.

There are very few records of the average or range of flight heights for the ghost bat in the wild. Some data for the Mt Etna population recorded flight height as being below 45 m, mostly below 10 m, and frequently less than 2 m (Augusteyn et al. 2018).

The distances flown by individual ghost bats from their roost site to their foraging area and the average size of foraging areas are not well understood. This is compounded by factors such as individual variation, nightly conditions, seasonality, reproduction, sex, prey abundance and landscape factors that can influence movement patterns. There are also limitations in tracking ghost bats with GPS tags (nocturnal activity so no solar recharge, tag size, cave-roosting, grooming off tags; Ruykys et al. 2026) so that the distances currently recorded are almost certainly underestimates.

The limited data available indicate that individuals often return to the same foraging area on consecutive nights (Tidemann et al. 1985; Augusteyn et al. 2018; Bullen et al. 2023; Ruykys et al. 2026). However, tracking has also shown individuals sometimes foraging in one area and then moving to other areas over successive nights (R. Ellis pers. comm. 25 February 2026). Data from the Top End (Ruykys et al. 2026), Pilbara (Biologic 2019; 2020; Ryan Ellis, *unpublished data*; Bullen et al. 2023) and Qld (Augusteyn et al. 2018) indicate a mean distance of ≈12–20 km travelled per individual per night. However, there are documented instances of individuals travelling up to 50 km in one night (Bullen et al. 2023; Ruykys et al. 2026, E. Williams pers. comm. 25 February 2026).

Data on the radial (i.e., one-way) distance travelled by individuals from their roost to their foraging area(s) are also limited but suggest averages from 3–8.5 km, with recorded maximums of ≈12–25 km (Augusteyn et al. 2018; Bullen et al. 2023; Ruykys et al. 2026, E. Williams pers. comm. 25 February 2026). These ranges depend on resource availability and abundance, which is often influenced by rainfall during the preceding wet season.

The home ranges of ghost bats are also poorly documented and greatly influenced by GPS/VHF tag limitations (number of fixes, number of bats tracked and short tracking duration). Home ranges are likely to vary with habitat quality intra- and inter-annually, as well as across colonies/populations. Inconsistencies in data collection and analysis also limits meaningful comparisons across studies. For example, Tidemann *et al.* (1985) used the minimum range method (Mohr 1947) to calculate a mean foraging area of 60.8 ha (range 28–120 ha) for five ghost bats with ≥10 VHF-tracked fixes. In contrast, Bullen *et al.* (2023) present data on the sizes of ‘typical foraging areas’ in the Pilbara. These were determined per individual by placing polygons over areas that had multiple GPS fixes at consecutive times. Bullen *et al.* (2023) identified a mean ‘foraging area’ of 105 ha (maximum of 450 ha). In contrast, Ruykys et al. (2026) calculated short-term core and home ranges for individuals with a sufficient number of GPS fixes and, where this was insufficient, populations. They identified that the size of short-term home and core ranges varied by sex and reproductive season, with core and home ranges ≈6–15 ha smaller in the mating than the maternity seasons, with the scale of this difference differing by sex and colony.

### 3.8 Hydration

The ghost bat has not been observed drinking water, including in captivity. The species appears to be capable of meeting its water requirements without drinking free water, relying instead on hydration obtained from prey. Cave humidity conditions of 70–90% appear to be important for maintaining water balance and good skin health (Toop 1985; Baudinette et al. 2000).

### 3.9 Habitat

The ghost bat occurs in a wide range of ecosystems: caves, rocky areas, dry grassy savanna and woodland, dry shrubland, tropical monsoon woodland, tropical monsoon forest and rainforest, riparian forests, mangroves, and agricultural land that has particular features (Duncan et al. 1999; Augusteyn et al. 2018; Armstrong et al. 2021).

Habitat of the ghost bat comprises three components:

 **Roosts** for thermoregulation and protection when resting and breeding

 **Foraging areas** for energy and nutrients (i.e., prey)

 **Areas** among roosts and foraging areas that are used for movement

These components are described in detail below. Characteristics of these components determine where the ghost bat can occur and persist. The three components are not spatially independent: ghost bats require productive foraging areas an accessible distance from suitable roosts.

#### Roosts

Access to suitable roosts is of critical importance to the ghost bat. All individuals require access to a roost with suitable conditions for protection from physiological stress and predators. A variety of roosts are used, ranging from shallow overhangs and crevices to deep underground caves and mines that are well insulated from weather conditions. Some roosts are used rarely, whereas others are permanently occupied. Roosts can accommodate a few individuals (Umbrello et al. 2025) up to more than 1,000 animals (Tidemann et al. 1985).

There have been several attempts to classify ghost bat roosts in terms of their relative importance to the species or within a region. Local assessment, based on good understanding of occupancy patterns and movements of local ghost bat populations, is necessary to classify or assign values to roosts in any area. Sites where ghost bats have not been detected but have appropriate structural and biophysical properties may also represent prospective roosting habitat (non-detection may be a result of climatic conditions, population fluctuations, movements associated with reproduction and/or insufficient surveys).

Maternity roosts, permanent roosts and regular roosts (see Glossary) are essential for the persistence and recovery of local ghost bat populations. These are not mutually exclusive – maternity roosts are either permanent or regular roosts. Occasional and opportunistic roosts (see Glossary) can vary from unimportant to essential for persistence, recovery and connectivity, depending on the local context.

Roosts are usually in natural rock structures or disused underground mines (Toop 1985; Hall et al. 1997). Natural roosts for the ghost bat have been reported in weathered and exposed rock formations, including – but not limited to – limestone karst, dissected sandstone plateau, sandstone bluff and escarpment, banded iron formation, boulder field and basalt lava tube (Douglas 1967; Kavanagh 1999; Armstrong & Anstee 2000; McKenzie et al. 2019; Augusteyn et al. 2023; SET 2024; Shaw et al. in prep.). Disused underground mines may be in different geological formations.

When occupied, these roosts have a stable temperature (usually 23–28oC) and moderate to high humidity (50–90%) (Churchill & Helman 1990). Higher humidity is likely to be most important at maternal roosts, helping reduce water loss in females and their young (Ryan Ellis pers. comm. 25 February 2026). Ghost bats will also seek out caverns or other dome-like structures in caves where warmer air is trapped (Toop 1985).

Ghost bats occupy roosts at night before and after foraging activity. They may also use roosts during the night to consume prey. Older pups occupy crèches in roosts both day and night (see Reproductive Ecology). For ghost bats to persist at roosting sites it is necessary for sufficient, suitable and accessible prey to be available in the surrounding landscape.

Ghost bats have also been recorded roosting during the day in rail culverts (Ryan Ellis pers. comm. 25 February 2026) and rock crevices (Bat Call WA 2021). More rarely, they have been observed roosting in large trees that are structurally complex (e.g., figs) or hollow (e.g., boab) (Armstrong & Anstee 2000), as well as disused buildings, and mining and other infrastructure (AWC unpublished data; Nicola Hanrahan pers. comm. 25 February 2025; Ryan Ellis pers. comm. 25 February 2026). These may be transient roosts and are likely being used for short-term refuge or by individuals that are dispersing (Armstrong and Anstee 2000). They may also be important for supporting population connectivity or access to foraging areas that are far from natural or mine roosts.

Suitable roosts for different purposes (e.g., breeding, connectivity) are limited across landscapes and regions. Loss of access to roosts can substantially interfere with the persistence and recovery of the species.

Some of the largest remaining ghost bat colonies occupy disused mine adits and shafts; for example, Kohinoor, Spring Hill and Lalla Rookh (R. Ellis pers. comm. 19 March 2026, N. Hanrahan pers. comm*.* 19 March 2026). Consequently, preservation of these roosts is equally important to protection of natural roost sites for ghost bat recovery.

Ghost bats are known to occupy artificial roosts under some circumstances; however, purpose-built artificial structures are not a substitute for natural roosts or mine roosts. Purpose-built artificial roosts may have a role in supplementing habitats for ghost bats, particularly where existing disused mines roosts are unstable and at risk of collapse, but they are not a replacement for existing roosting habitat. Research is underway to characterise the roosting characteristics of natural roosts to help inform specifications for potential artificial roosts, but further research is required to determine any potential for purpose-built artificial structures to enhance recovery of the species.

#### Foraging areas

Selection of foraging habitat by ghost bats and the characteristics of foraging habitat across the species’ distribution are poorly understood. Local information about ghost bat movement patterns and diet is necessary to identify and define foraging areas.

The limited information that is available indicates that ghost bats forage in a variety of ecosystems, including areas of intact native vegetation and agricultural land (Augusteyn et al. 2018). Foraging areas are likely to change in response to environmental conditions, e.g. fire regimes. The size of foraging areas and how they are used also fluctuates over time and space, due at least in part to changes in prey abundance, which is influenced by seasonal conditions.

Prey abundance is often influenced by water availability. Alluvial plains, stony plains and other habitats can be rich with prey after good rainfall events (J. Augusteyn pers. comm. 19 March 2026). Waterways were often visited by VHF/GPS-tracked ghost bats from the Mt Etna and Kings Plains populations (Augusteyn et al. 2018; Williams 2025), most likely because they provided the optimum vegetation structure for hunting, including perching trees and shrubs and low-moderate ground cover (N. Hanrahan pers. comm. 19 March 2026). Permanent and ephemeral wetlands are also areas of high productivity (Queensland Museum 2022) that can provide abundant food resources for the ghost bat (Kingston 2005; Woinarski et al. 2008; Shaw et al. in review).

Foraging areas can be near roosts or significantly further away (see Activity, and Movement and dispersal). Application of research methods that can detect ghost bats outside their roosts (such as GPS tracking and the landscape-scale acoustic lure method - Ruykys et al. 2024) will help to improve understanding of foraging areas.

The ghost bat has been recorded foraging in a variety of ecosystems and vegetation types, including:

 Pilbara region:

 GPS-tracked foraging across plains, low hills, rocky ridgelines and drainage lines with open grassy woodlands of low scattered mulga (*Acacia* spp.) or eucalypt over shrubs and patchy grasses, typically with areas of clear ground (typically 30–70% clear of ground cover) (Bullen et al. 2023).

 Tracking data have shown that there is a preference for more sparsely vegetated habitats with reasonable visibility/access to the ground and little shrub or tree cover, with perching opportunities provided by occasional larger shrubs and trees (R. Ellis pers. comm. 23 March 2026).

 Foraging habitat includes alluvial plains dominated by tussock grasses and sparse eucalypts, stony and sandy plains dominated by hummock grasses, to mulga woodlands (grove – inter-grove form generally) with sparse to no ground cover between groves and drainage lines (R. Ellis. pers. comm. 23 March 2026).

 Kimberley region: No studies on ghost bat foraging have been conducted in this region. Anecdotal observations are from wooded areas.

 Top End region:

 Recorded in savanna woodland, shrubland, riparian vegetation and plantations (e.g., sandalwood, mango), including in pastoral lands with retained native vegetation and in remnant vegetation adjacent to horticultural crops (Ruykys et al. 2024).

 Pine Creek: VHF-tracked foraging in grassy woodland/open woodlands dominated by eucalypts over Cymbopogon bombycinus (lemon grass, silky oilgrass), Aristida latifolia (feathertop wiregrass), Themeda triandra (kangaroo grass), Trioda spp. (spinifex) and/or Eriachne spp. (wanderrie grass) (Tidemann et al. 1985).

 Eastern Queensland region:

 Kings Plains: Observed foraging at the edge of rainforest and open grassy woodland in a hilly granite boulderfield landscape (SET 2024).

 Mt Etna: Tracked in woodlands and ephemeral watercourses with *Eucalyptus tereticornis* (blue gum), *E. platyphylla* (poplar gum) and/or other *Eucalyptus* spp. and *Corymbia* spp. on alluvial plains within a matrix of simplified agricultural areas (Augusteyn et al. 2018). Edges were frequently used in these modified landscapes.

 Gulf-Inland Queensland and Central Arid regions: No studies on ghost bat foraging have been conducted in these regions.

### 3.10 Habitat critical to the survival of ghost bats

Habitat critical to the survival of a species refers to areas necessary for the species’ continued persistence and recovery. As outlined in the *Environment Protection and Biodiversity Conservation Regulations 2025* (Reg 7.09), in determining whether an area constitutes habitat critical to the survival of a species, consideration should be given to the extent to which it is:

 used during periods of stress – such as flood, drought, fire,

 used to meet essential life cycle requirements such as foraging, breeding, nesting, roosting or social behaviour,

 used by important populations,

 necessary to maintain genetic diversity and long-term evolutionary development,

 necessary for use as corridors to allow the species to move freely between sites that are used to meet essential life cycle requirements,

 necessary to ensure the long-term future of the species through reintroduction or re-colonisation, and

 any other way in which habitat may be critical to the survival of a listed threatened species.

#### Identifying habitat critical to the survival of the ghost bat

Any action undertaken within the range of the ghost bat must avoid ‘*adverse impacts to habitats critical to the survival of the ghost bat’* (MNES 1.1 guidelines).

Identification of habitat critical to the survival of a species is based on the species’ life cycle requirements and habitat attributes required to support long-term survival and evolutionary development. For the ghost bat, it is necessary to consider its habitat requirements across its range to ensure that all habitat attributes required to support its life cycle requirements are met. Refer to the Distribution and Biology and ecology sections for more detail on the species’ life cycle requirements and the attributes of critical habitat.

Areas of habitat critical to the survival of the ghost bat comprise the following attributes:

 Roost sites

 being a network of roosts centred around a permanent and/or regular roost(s) (which may include occasional and/or opportunistic roosts) used for mating and maternity activities, feeding and transiting.

 Foraging areas

 of adequate spatial extent and with habitat attributes suitable for foraging, i.e. an appropriate range and density of suitable prey species and vegetation structure for perching and hunting. These support viable populations and population growth through seasonal and stochastic environmental variability over the long-term.

 Connectivity

 amongst roost sites, as well as between roost sites and foraging areas. This facilitates movement and gene flow, enables population growth, and supports re-colonisation following disturbance and in response to environmental change.

Areas of critical habitat for the ghost bat are unlikely to be small or isolated and may not be easily demarcated. Based on regional and local variation in habitat, home range and movement, critical habitat for the ghost bat will vary in size and composition across the species’ range.

Historically, permanent and/or regularly occupied roosts have been the primary focus of management. This approach has led to the development of a roost classification scheme applied within the Pilbara region (Bat Call WA 2021). Roost sites are a fundamental attribute of critical habitat for ghost bats and protection of permanent and regular roosts is vital for ghost bat recovery. However, roost sites are not the sole component of critical habitat for the species. Rather, foraging habitat (free of, and/or managed for threats) is also essential to ghost bat persistence and recovery. Current understanding of ghost bat nightly movements indicates flights from 3 km to in excess of 25 km radial distances from the roost (Augusteyn et al. 2018; Bullen et al. 2023; Ruykys et al. 2026; E. Williams pers. comm. 25 February 2026). Individual bats may utilise multiple roosts within a regional network, with documented routine movements in one study at Mt Etna indicating up to 30 km from a day roost and occasional dispersal movements exceeding 50 km (Toop 1985). Population structure is, therefore, best understood as a network organised around roost complexes rather than discrete isolated sites (Tidemann et al. 1985; Toop 1985; Bullen et al. 2023).

Noting routine foraging distances in excess of 25 km (Augusteyn et al. 2018; Bullen et al. 2023; Ruykys et al. 2026; E. Williams pers. comm. 25 February 2026), any potential impacts to ghost bats within a minimum 25 km radius of permanent and regular ghost bat roosts must be considered. This is the area in which the species is most vulnerable to threats, and where recovery action should be focused.

Habitats that are not critical to survival of the ghost bat may also be important for recovery (e.g., for restoration and species re-introduction) and should be considered as part of conservation planning for the species.

#### Information requirements

Determining areas of habitat critical to the survival of the ghost bat requires access to information on the ghost bat within the broader landscape. This includes but is not limited to:

 valid species records, including roost types and locations and their condition, trajectory and any identified threats,

 information on foraging,

 any available telemetry/movement data for the relevant region,

 state/ territory-based (or other) vegetation and habitat condition mapping,

 landscape mapping (including geological and hydrological features and mine features),

 mapping of land use of the area (modification, disturbances and historical uses),

 aerial photography to assess habitat condition, fragmentation and likely barriers,

 surveys to identify and assess use of foraging habitat and/or to indicate hotspots for targeted searches for roosting habitat, and

 targeted surveys of roosts to determine location and extent within structures, assess occupancy and seasonal usage (i.e. whether roosts are permanent and/or maternity roost or other), and determine connectivity amongst roosts and between roosts and foraging areas.

## 4. Threats and impacts

### 4.1 Current known and suspected threatening processes

The type, extent and impacts of threats on the ghost bat vary across the species’ broad distribution, reflecting differences in landscape characteristics, land use and activities. Threats that cause mortality or reduce fitness and reproduction are likely to be contributing to population decline and inhibiting recovery. Cumulative impacts of multiple threats may also be putting ghost bat populations under pressure.

The most significant threats to ghost bats are loss and degradation of natural roosts and mine roosts (Armstrong et al. 2021) and of foraging habitat (primarily due to land clearing, habitat modification and fragmentation). Degradation of roosting habitat can include changing microclimate conditions as well as environmental disturbance such as noise, vibration, dust and artificial light. Degradation of foraging habitat primarily reduces availability and diversity of prey and thus the foraging efficacy of the ghost bat. Declines in prey lead to nutritional stress, reduce individual fitness and decrease reproductive rates. In all cases, the cumulative loss or degradation of habitat across regions must be considered.

#### Loss of natural roosts

Natural roosts that provide conditions suitable for ghost bats are created through thousands to millions of years of erosion that forms cave systems (Young 1987; Grimes et al. 2003; Woodhouse et al. 2022) that cannot be replaced. The loss of one or more roosts – especially a maternity, permanent or regular roost – causes local population displacement or loss, particularly where alternative roosts are scarce or unavailable. Physical loss of natural roosts is primarily – but not exclusively – due to activities associated with industry, including mining/quarrying (including use of ordnance), construction, energy generation/development and infrastructure (e.g., Cramer et al. 2022). Associated activities involve vegetation clearing and the construction of infrastructure and extensive networks of linear features such as roads, transmission corridors, pipelines, substations and service tracks (Laurance et al. 2009; Northrup & Wittemyer 2013). Collectively, these result in cleared, fragmented and modified landscapes, reducing connectivity and foraging habitat.

**Regional considerations**: Loss of natural roosts is a threat across the entire distribution of the ghost bat. It is particularly acute in the Pilbara region where many suitable natural roosts occur in regolith over banded iron formations that is targeted for mining activities (see Northover et al. 2023). In the Gulf-Inland Queensland region, loss of natural roosts is also occurring due to mining activities, often exacerbated by there being limited information on the species’ distribution and roosting requirements (Ghost Bat Recovery Team pers. comm. 21 March 2026).

#### Loss of roosts in mines and infrastructure

Significant ghost bat populations inhabit disused underground mines, many of which are structurally unstable and have an inherent risk of collapse without ongoing maintenance. Loss of mine roosts can also occur due to mine reworking, flooding (from mining and natural events) or closure due to perceived risk to humans, as well as other industrial activities.

**Regional considerations**: Loss of mine roosts is a major threat to ghost bats in the Pilbara (particularly areas that are prospective sites for gold mining in the Chichester subregion) and Top End regions. Loss of mine roosts has also been recorded in the Eastern Qld region. Disused underground mines are common in the Gulf-Inland and Eastern Queensland regions (Ghost Bat Recovery Team pers. comm. 21 March 2026).

#### Loss of foraging habitat

The removal and fragmentation of ghost bat foraging habitat can reduce the capacity of landscapes to support the species, even when roosts remain available. Ghost bats require access to foraging habitat of sufficient extent, complexity and connectivity to support their nutritional needs and movement across the landscape.

Loss of foraging habitat occurs discontinuously throughout the species’ range and at varying scales, primarily as a result of land clearing, modification and fragmentation of native vegetation associated with agriculture, mining, renewable energy developments (particularly solar farms), urbanisation and other infrastructure. Impacts include the direct removal of habitat that supports the ghost bat’s range of prey and removes vegetation that is required for the ghost bat’s perch-and-ambush hunting strategy. The removal of foraging habitat may also act as a barrier if ghost bats are reluctant to cross cleared areas. This may affect movement between roosts (Ghost Bat Recovery Team pers. comm. 21 March 2026).

Evidence indicates ghost bats have fidelity to foraging areas, with individuals visiting the same sites across multiple nights (Tidemann et al. 1985; Ruykys et al. 2026; E. Williams, unpublished data). The loss of foraging habitat may directly remove sites used by certain individuals. While several ghost bats have been observed to hunt in the same foraging areas at the same time (Tidemann et al. 1985), the carrying capacity of foraging areas and the relationship between individuals that routinely hunt in proximity are poorly understood. Foraging habitat requirements of ghost bat colonies likely differs based on roost type, season and colony size.

The nature of impacts on ghost bat roosting colonies of removal and fragmentation of foraging habitat is poorly understood. Levels of impact are likely determined by factors such as the types of roosts present, colony size, seasonal use, extent of cleared/fragmented area, and cumulative loss and fragmentation of foraging habitat in the region.

**Regional considerations**: Simplification and removal of foraging habitat caused by agriculture and other industries is recorded to some degree across the ghost bat’s range (Ghost Bat Recovery Team pers. comm. 19 March 2026).

#### Entanglement in barbed wire

Entanglement in barbed wire fences is a significant cause of mortality for the ghost bat throughout its range and likely contributing to ghost bat population declines at a local scale, particularly from new fences and those near permanent and regular roosts and in good foraging habitat close to suitable roosts (Armstrong & Anstee 2000; Chris Clague pers. comm. 24 November 2025). The ghost bat’s low flight height, intermittent use of echolocation in flight and a reliance on observing movement and passive listening to detect prey mean it is particularly susceptible to entanglement in barbed wire (Armstrong and Anstee 2000; Cramer et al. 2022).

**Regional considerations**: Ghost bat mortality from entanglement in barbed wire has been reported in the Pilbara region (Armstrong and Anstee 2000; McKenzie & Bullen 2009; Bradley et al. 2023) and observed in the Kimberley (AWC & DBCA observations), Top End (N. Hanrahan pers. comm. 2 October 2025) and Eastern Queensland (SET unpublished data) regions. It is likely to be a risk across most of the range of the ghost bat (Ghost Bat Recovery Team pers. comm. 18 March 2026).

#### Habitat degradation due to grazing by cattle

Cattle grazing has modified and degraded landscapes that provide habitat for the ghost bat. Habitat has also been cleared to increase areas available for grazing. Cattle grazing – particularly in conjunction with inappropriate fire regimes – simplifies vegetation structure and composition with flow-on effects on the availability and accessibility of prey for the ghost bat (Woinarski et al. 1999; Woinarski & Ash 2002; Woinarski et al. 2011). This is particularly problematic along waterways, where high cattle grazing pressure leads to complex and productive riparian ecosystems transitioning to simpler, more homogeneous ecosystems. Cattle grazing also introduces and spreads transformer weeds (e.g. buffel grass) that alter fire regimes and reduces water quality (Mihailou & Massaro 2021). Reduced vegetation cover or reduced vegetation complexity modifies the suitability of habitat for a range of ghost bat prey.

**Regional considerations**: Cattle grazing is the primary land use across northern Australia (ABARES 2016; 2025), ranging from intensively managed grazing to seasonal or occasional mustering. Most of the distribution of the ghost bat is subject to cattle grazing, but direct threats to ghost bats from cattle grazing are unlikely in:

 Conservation reserves and protected areas where cattle are actively removed or excluded

 Areas where pastoral operations are managed to have a low ecological impact

 Areas that are too rocky, steep or rough for cattle and/or contain less palatable vegetation

In Cape York (Eastern Qld region), overgrazing has significantly affected quality and prey availability in foraging habitat (Ghost Bat Recovery Team pers. comm. 19 March 2026). Simplification/degradation of foraging habitat caused by grazing has been recorded in the parts of the Pilbara, Eastern Qld and Gulf-Inland Qld regions (Ghost Bat Recovery Team pers. comm. 19 March 2026). In many areas of the Pilbara where cattle numbers are low, feral donkey and wild horse populations are impacting landscapes that provide habitat for the ghost bat. This is particularly the case in the eastern Hamersley Range and northeast parts of the Chichester subregion (S. van Leeuwen, pers. comm. 17 April 2026).

#### Disturbance at roosts

Ghost bats are sensitive to disturbance at their roosts (Douglas 1967). Disturbance can be caused by entry of humans, and by noise, vibration, light and dust. Disturbance can result in mortality, roost abandonment, sensory disruption and reduced fitness.

Disturbance by human activity can occur due to tourism, exploration, fossicking or research activities. Disturbance from human entry has the greatest impact during the day. If disturbed, ghost bats may exit a roost during daylight when they are at an increased risk of heat stress and predation (Woinarski et al. 2014). Bats may be able to utilise alternative sites but may still be impacted energetically.

Seasonal impacts are greatest during overwintering, pregnancy and lactation. During lactation, entry to maternity roosts has caused pups to be dropped by mothers or to be dislodged from walls during their mothers’ rapid take-off (Toop 1985). While disturbance may not always cause the immediate abandonment of a roost, metabolic stress levels may increase to a point of individuals abandoning roosts in the medium- to longer-term or, in extreme cases, death.

Disturbance from noise, vibration, light and dust is generally associated with activities related to construction, drilling, blasting, processing, vehicle movement, earthmoving and general operations in relation to mines, quarries and renewable energy developments (Bat Call WA 2021), including installation of transmission lines. The level of impact is site-specific and is dependent on many factors, such as the proximity to the roost; the nature and scale of the disturbance e.g. amplitude, frequency, timing, schedule; landscape factors e.g., geology, orientation and complexity of roost; and colony size and season.

Disturbance may also occur due to low-flying (including military) aircraft, but this is poorly understood. Research is needed to understand the potential impacts of low-flying helicopters on ghost bat behaviour and population dynamics.

Electromagnetic fields are known to affect bats due to sensory disruption. For example, bat activity is reduced in areas where radars emit constant electromagnetic fields of more than 20 V/m (Nicholls & Racey 2009), and as low as 2 V/m (Nicholls & Racey 2007). Although the impacts of sensory disruption on the ghost bat are poorly understood, impacts by electromagnetic fields may be while animals are foraging or roosting. Linear electromagnetic fields (e.g., high voltage transmission lines) may restrict movement due to the ghost bat generally flying low (see Foraging behaviour).

**Regional considerations**: Disturbance of roosts is occurring in the Eastern Qld region due to mining, recreation and infrastructure activities. Disturbance of roosts due to mining activities is also occurring in the Pilbara region. Expansion of renewable energy may be an emerging threat. Cave tourism occurs in some areas of the Top End region and disturbance of mine roosts by tourists and fossickers is likely common near human-populated areas. Disturbance by tourists at Dimalurru/Tunnel Creek (Kimberley region) may be a risk to that colony (Ghost Bat Recovery Team pers. comm. 18 March 2026).

#### Habitat modification due to altered hydrology

Changes in hydrology that lower humidity and/or cause thermally challenging conditions are inferred to lead to ghost bat roosts having a lower carrying capacity (L. Ruykys pers. comm. 9 February 2025). Such changes can also reduce ghost bat reproduction/recruitment and lead to abandonment of roosts. Changes to hydrology are often associated with mining activities (e.g., groundwater drawdown), irrigated agriculture, infrastructure projects and urbanisation.

Changes in hydrology can also alter the hydrodynamics of areas that are used by the ghost bat for foraging. Such changes may be caused by intermittent or permanent flooding or desiccation. Altered hydrology, such as groundwater drawdown or the rerouting of surface water bodies and surface overland flows, may affect ghost bats by changing the structure and productivity of foraging habitats and reducing prey resources. Reduced water availability can alter riparian and other water-influenced vegetation, disrupt local prey concentrations, and lower the abundance or availability of both insect and vertebrate prey. Changes to hydrology may have local impacts throughout the species’ distribution.

**Regional considerations**: Altered hydrology is a threat in the Pilbara, Top End, Eastern Qld and Gulf-Inland Qld regions (Ghost Bat Recovery Team pers. comm. 18 March 2026).

#### Predation by and competition with feral cats

Predation by feral cats (*Felis catus*) is a key threatening process listed under the EPBC Act (Cth DCCEEW 2024). While ghost bats have several native predators, including northern quolls, pythons and birds of prey, feral cats are the greatest predatory threat to ghost bats and could have the greatest potential to cause ghost bat population declines (L. Ruykys pers. comm. 2 February 2025). Cats can prey on ghost bats at entrances to roosts and are agile enough to capture individuals roosting or flying within roosts, where confined spaces are likely to be advantageous for cats (R. Ellis, pers. comm. 23 March 2026). The remains of ghost bats have been found at the entrances of natural roosts, on the floor of mine passages leading to roosts and at the bases of trees with signs of cat predation (Bat Call WA 2021; R. Ellis, pers. comm. 23 March 2026). Feral cats have been observed preying on ghost bats at roosts in the Pilbara region and the frequency of their visitation indicates that they have the potential to cause significant declines of ghost bat colonies, particularly where few individuals are recorded regularly roosting (R. Ellis, pers. comm. 23 March 2026).

Feral cats may compete directly with ghost bats for prey, particularly for small native mammals. In addition to predation, feral cats are reservoirs for *Toxoplasma gondii*, a zoonotic parasite capable of reducing rodent populations and potentially altering prey availability for ghost bats. It remains unknown whether toxoplasmosis affects ghost bats directly.

**Regional considerations**: Predation by and competition with feral cats is a threat across the range of the ghost bat; however, predation by feral cats is likely to be more impactful in the Pilbara region due to the generally smaller colony sizes. There are no direct observations in other parts of the ghost bat’s range and the level of impact is unknown but may be high (Ghost Bat Recovery Team pers. comm. 19 March 2026).

#### Impacts of wind turbines

The expansion of the renewable energy industry has led to the rapid expansion of wind farms. As well as loss of foraging habitat, potential impacts of wind farms on ghost bats include sensory disruption, noise disturbance associated with the construction and operation of wind farms and direct injury or mortality when ghost bats collide with the moving blades of wind turbines (Reid and Baker 2025). A recent ecological risk assessment (Reid and Baker 2025) showed the ghost bat to be one of the species most at risk from direct and indirect impacts of wind turbines.

**Regional considerations**: Collision with wind turbines is a significant potential threat in the Pilbara and Eastern Queensland regions, where renewable energy projects are planned or underway (Ghost Bat Recovery Team pers. comm. 27 March 2026).

#### Fire regimes that cause declines in biodiversity

Fire regimes that cause declines in biodiversity is a key threatening process listed under the EPBC Act (Cth DAWE 2022). Fire regimes across much of Australia have changed over time due to a variety of factors, including the clearing and fragmentation of vegetation, the use of fire-suppression and management techniques, an increase in the number of ignition sources, a warming and drying climate and a decline in traditional burning practices by First Nations communities (Cth DAWE 2022).

High frequency or high severity burning is a driver of large-scale degradation of ghost bat habitat. Frequent and/or severe fires simplify landscapes and reduce the abundance and availability of ghost bat prey, particularly small mammals (Woinarski et al. 2015; Ziembicki et al. 2015; Radford et al. 2021; Einoder et al. 2023) and some birds (Reside et al. 2011). Inappropriate fire regimes may also impact invertebrate, frog and reptile prey populations (Cth DAWE 2022). High severity fire may also threaten roosting colonies, particularly maternity roosts when females are lactating and caring for juveniles.

**Regional considerations**: Frequent and extensive fire is a threat across most of the ghost bat’s range, except some wetter areas of coastal Northeast Queensland and Central Queensland Coast regions. In Cape York (Eastern Qld region), changes to fire regimes have caused vegetation simplification, prey shifts, and reduced integrity in rocky landscapes (Ghost Bat Recovery Team pers. comm. 19 March 2026). There are likely to be interactions between bushfires and grazing pressure in the Eastern Qld and Kimberley regions. Simplification or degradation of foraging habitat caused by changes to fire regimes is recorded in the Pilbara, Top End, Eastern Qld and Gulf-Inland Qld regions (Ghost Bat Recovery Team pers. comm. 19 March 2026).

#### Climate change

Climate change is increasing the intensity of rainfall events across northern Australia and the amount of rainfall from Cape York to the Pilbara (CSIRO & BOM 2024). In arid areas, climate change is predicted to increase temperatures, heatwaves, evapotranspiration, rainfall variability, and the severity of drought, while also increasing the intensity of heavy rainfall events when they occur (CSIRO & BOM 2024). Severe rainfall events may cause mortality from inundation and temporarily obstruct access to roosts. For example, ghost bat roosts in the Camooweal area, and elsewhere in the Gulf-Mt Isa region, are likely to have been inundated during a flooding event in March 2023 (J. Augusteyn pers. comm. 2025). In the Top End, some ghost bat roosts were inundated during rainfall events in March 2026 (N. Hanrahan pers comm. 19 March 2026). Beyond causing short-term evacuation of the roost, these types of events may increase erosion and reduce the stability of some roosts. Intense rainfall events and increased seasonal rainfall can also reduce the suitability of roosts due to seepage (Toop 1985) or other changes to hydrology.

More intense rainfall events across the species’ range, together with increased aridity (particularly in the southern parts of the range), are likely to affect ghost bat foraging habitat and prey availability. In southern areas, increased aridity may also reduce the suitability of some roost sites by lowering humidity levels, particularly in less complex roosts that rely more on external conditions to maintain suitable temperature and humidity. Where climate change is increasing day and night temperatures (CSIRO & BOM 2024), there may be increased risk of ghost bats experiencing heat stress.

**Regional considerations**: Extreme events (flooding, extreme heat) caused by climate change may be a particular threat in the Gulf-Inland Qld region (and potentially other regions), particularly where they affect ghost bat prey (Ghost Bat Recovery Team pers. comm. 19 March 2026).

#### Habitat modification due to introduced weeds

Habitat of the ghost bat is degraded by weeds that include buffel grass (Cenchrus ciliaris, C. setiger), grader grass (Themeda quadrivalvis), gamba grass (Andropogon gayanus), rubber vine (Cryptostegia grandiflora), leucaena (*Leucaena leucocephala*), Madeira vine (*Andreda cordifolia*) and lantana (Lantana camara). These weeds can significantly add to fuel loads and increase the severity and extent of fires within ghost bat habitat. This simplification inhibits ghost bat foraging strategies by altering the density of the ground layer, decreasing the suitability for some prey and increasing the suitability for other prey (e.g. rodents).

This threat is recognised under the Novel biota and their impact on biodiversity key threatening process listed under the EPBC Act (TSSC 2013). Invasion of northern Australia by gamba grass and other introduced grasses is also a key threatening process listed under the EPBC Act (Cth DSEWPC 2012).

**Regional considerations**: Weed infestations occur patchily across the distribution of the ghost bat. Lantana and rubber vine can form dense thickets across cave entrances, restricting access at Mt Etna (John Augusteyn, pers. comm. 24 March 2026). The Kimberley is the least impacted region (Ghost bat recovery team pers. comm. 18 March 2026).

#### Direct and indirect impact of cane toads

The biological effects, including lethal toxic ingestion, caused by cane toads is a key threatening process listed under the EPBC Act (Cth DSEWPC 2011). There is some evidence that poisoning by cane toads (*Rhinella marina*) has impacted ghost bats; however, the extent and severity of the threat to the species is unclear. Ghost bats are known to consume cane toads, as demonstrated by the remains of toads in ghost bat droppings, and in Kakadu National Park, a deceased ghost bat was found with the decomposed remains of a small cane toad in its oesophagus (White et al. 2016). The ghost bat lacks immunity to bufotoxin secreted by the cane toad (Shine et al. 2016). However, cane toads are widespread across northern Australia, where ghost bat populations and colonies persist. For these reasons, the impact of ingestion of cane toads on ghost bats at the population level is also unclear.

Secondarily, the arrival of cane toads has caused dramatic declines in large reptilian predators such as goannas and large elapid snakes (Doody et al. 2017; Ward-Fear et al. 2024). The loss of these apex predators can trigger mesopredator release, allowing mid-sized snakes—including pythons—to increase in abundance due to reduced predation and competition (Brown et al. 2013). These snakes may further compete with ghost bats for diminishing small vertebrate prey and, in some cases, may predate roosting or juvenile ghost bats. Collectively, these interacting pressures—introduced carnivores, and trophic restructuring following feral carnivore and cane toad invasion—may exacerbate other threats for the ghost bat.

**Regional considerations**: Cane toads may be having impacts in the Eastern Qld region and possibly in the Top End and Kimberley regions; further investigation is required to clarify the extent of the impact. Infrequent monitoring has shown declines in ghost bat numbers in some areas of the Gulf-Inland Qld region and likely extinction in the Riversleigh/Boodjamulla National Park. The latter has been attributed to the arrival of the cane toad in the Gulf-Inland Qld region during the 1980s and 1990s (Ghost Bat Recovery Team pers. comm. 18 March 2026.) Canes toads are not present in the Pilbara region but are predicted to arrive between 2035 and 2055 (Dunlop et al*.* 2025).

#### Persecution

Intentional (illegal) killing of ghost bats has occurred historically (McKean & Price 1967) and is likely to be an ongoing threat at a local scale. Intentional or inadvertent disturbance through noise, light, smoke and unwarranted visitation also occurs. Persecution can cause direct mortality, injury, roost abandonment and stress. The highest risk is at roosts.

**Regional considerations**: Ghost bats are recorded as having been intentionally killed in the Eastern Qld region (e.g. McKean & Price 1967), and intentional disturbance is known from the Eastern Qld (Toop 1979) and Top End regions (Ghost Bat Recovery Team pers. comm. 18 March 2026).

#### Direct and indirect impacts of other introduced fauna

Predation by the European red fox (*Vulpes vulpes*) is a key threatening process listed under the EPBC Act (Cth DEWHA 2008). Predation by the European red fox may be a threat to ghost bats in some locations as, in these locations, foxes have been detected at cave entrances (John Augusteyn pers. comm. 2025). Ghost bats and foxes co-occur at the Mt Etna, Cape Hillsborough and Hervey Range colonies, as well as in the Pilbara region. Foxes are expanding their range and there are now sightings north of Cairns (Qld) and in Broome (WA) (Atlas of Living Australia 2026).

In northern Australia, large groups of pigs (Sus scrofa) take shelter inside caves during the heat of the day (Nicola Hanrahan pers. obs.). Pigs become active shortly after sunset, coinciding with the fly-out time for ghost bats. The noise, movement and smell of pigs in ghost bat roosts may affect use of these caves and the subsequent fitness of resident ghost bats. Pigs may also predate on ghost bat pups that are dislodged from the cave wall because of disturbance (L. Ruykys pers. comm. 10 February 2026).

**Regional considerations**: Predation by foxes may be a threat where the range of the fox and the ghost bat overlaps. Feral pigs may impact ghost bats at roosts in northern parts of the ghost bat’s range, including the Kimberley, Top End, Gulf-Inland Queensland and Eastern Queensland regions (Ghost bat Recovery Team pers. comm. 18 March 2026).

## 5. Recovery objectives and actions

### 5.1 Vision

This national recovery plan guides conservation and recovery of the ghost bat at a national scale. It builds on work of numerous government agencies, researchers and non-government, community and First Nations organisations. The vision for this recovery plan is to:

determine the species' current national population size and distribution, and

increase or maintain the population size within each region to a level that is resilient and supports the species’ long-term persistence across its range.

### 5.2 Objectives

**Objective 1:** Population sizes are known, and trends are monitored and tracked effectively

**Objective 2:** The extent, quality, and connectivity of roosting and foraging habitat are maintained or increased

**Objective 3:** Key threats are managed effectively

**Objective 4:** Recovery actions are implemented with the participation of stakeholders

**Objective 5:** Key knowledge gaps are addressed, and information is used to improve decision-making, conservation and recovery

### 5.3 Recovery actions

Actions to guide the protection and recovery of ghost bats are detailed in Table 4–9 below. Each recovery action is prioritised (1, 2 or 3) depending on the degree of urgency.

**Priority 1:** Is considered an urgent action. Prompt action is needed to ensure effective coordination, to avoid or mitigate the most significant threats, or to provide crucial information for planning and management.

**Priority 2:** Is considered an essential action over the course of the plan. Action is necessary to avoid or mitigate threats, implement planning and management, undertake research, and develop tools towards long-term recovery.

**Priority 3:** Is considered highly beneficial. Action is desirable, and while not critical, will provide for longer-term survival and recovery.
