## Executive summary

The National Recovery Plan for Greater Gliders covers two species of greater glider listed under the EPBC Act, *Petauroides minor -* Greater Glider (northern) and *Petauroides volans -* Greater Glider (southern and central). Throughout this document the common name ‘greater gliders’ refers to the genus *Petauroides*. For brevity, scientific names (rather than common names) are used throughout this recovery plan.

Greater gliders are flagship species for forest ecosystems. These arboreal marsupials occur in eucalypt forests and woodlands along the Great Dividing Range from northern Queensland to southern Victoria (Lindenmayer et al. 2011), extending east into coastal forests with subtropical to temperate eucalypt communities. Well-connected and contiguous forest communities with old trees (&gt;100 years old) and stands of successive age classes that contain abundant and developing hollows are essential habitat attributes for greater glider reproduction and foraging. These intact ecosystems also play a crucial role in mitigating and adapting to a changing climate, providing species like the greater glider with refuge sites.

Greater gliders are sensitive to habitat loss and disturbance due to their specialised diet of eucalypt leaves and obligate dependence on mature trees with large hollows for shelter, making populations more vulnerable to decline and increased risk of mortality (Jackson 2023). Both listed *Petauroides* species are subject to similar threatening processes. The main drivers of population decline relate to fire events, and activities associated with primary industry, climate change and energy development.

In 2019-20 Australia experienced an unprecedented bush fire season (Black Summer bushfires), one of the worst in the nation’s history. It is estimated that 19 million hectares were burnt during a period of record-breaking temperatures and extremely low rainfall. This equated to approximately 28% of the greater gliders’ geographic range (Ward et al. 2020). Following this catastrophic event, greater gliders underwent substantial population declines due to a significant loss and fragmentation of habitat. This prompted a reassessment of the conservation status of these highly dependent forest dwellers.

This recovery plan establishes a national framework to secure the long-term survival of greater gliders by 2050. Its vision is for populations to be increasing, genetically diverse, and resilient, supported by healthy, connected, high-quality habitats that includes climate refugia. To achieve this, the plan focuses on securing and enhancing habitat by protecting critical foraging, denning, and dispersal areas, restoring degraded landscapes, and improving connectivity, while also establishing a monitoring program to track population trends and support measurable recovery.

The plan outlines research actions across the species’ range to guide recovery. These actions will address key knowledge gaps, inform conservation planning, and facilitate adaptive management, providing the best chance to protect and manage greater glider populations. Engagement with land managers and other relevant stakeholders operating within the greater gliders’ distribution will play a critical role in the species’ long-term recovery, including collaborations with Traditional Owners. Developing and implementing fire management practices that incorporate Traditional Owners knowledge of cultural burning could also support ecosystem health and species recovery.

## 2. Ecology and biology

### 2.1 Description

Greater gliders are hollow-dependant arboreal marsupials that are predominantly nocturnal, occurring in the *Eucalyptus* forests of eastern Australia (Norman and Mackey 2023). Greater gliders are cryptic and distinguished from other gliders by the fold of connecting skin (i.e., the patagium) that is attached at the elbow of the forearm to the outer digit of the tarsus (Harris and Maloney 2010). The long, furred tail, which is not prehensile, is used as a rudder (Nowak 2018). Pelage colour varies between individual greater gliders more than in any other marsupial. The very long, dense fur is typically brownish-black but can range from pure black with a creamy underside, to dusky browns and greys, cinnamon, red, yellow, and completely white (McKay 1989).

*Petauroides volans* is typically larger in size than *P. minor* with combined head and body lengths ranging from 35-46 cm, tails can be 45-60 cm long, and average weight is between 0.9-1.7 kg (Jackson 2023). Head and body lengths of *P. minor* range from 30-40 cm, tail lengths between

40-48 cm, and an average weight between 0.7-0.9 kg (Comport et al. 1996; Jackson 2023). *P. minor* has a more slender body shape with smaller ears and tail than *P. volans* (Comport et al. 1996). The dorsal pelage is typically a dusky brown, often with a darker mid-dorsal stripe, and lighter or whitish underbelly (Jackson 2023). ### 2.2 Cultural significance

The significance of greater gliders to First Nations peoples is not well documented. As the distribution of greater gliders is widespread, they are likely known by many different names. As recorded by Fleay (1947), the Yarra Yarra tribe (in Victoria) knew the greater glider as ‘Poong-goong’, while in the Lake Tyers region (Victoria) they were known as ‘Warnda’.

Kerle (2001) reported that possums and gliders play an extremely important part of the interwoven spiritual and physical lives of First Nations people. For example, some communities consider their “spirit” as being extremely curious and often mischievous, but also kind and very active (Kerle 2001). Greater gliders may feature in dreaming stories similar to the sugar glider, which are said to glide down from the sky with messages from the ancestors. English ornithologist John Gould (1804-81) described the value of greater gliders and other possum species as a source of protein, with hunters locating gliders by detecting hairs caught on bark around tree hollows or from bark scratchings (Kerle 2001). It is likely that they also provided other resources including clothing, fur, string and artifacts to local peoples.

### 2.3 Taxonomy and genetics

Greater gliders are the only extant members of the genus *Petauroides* (Thomas, 1888). Historically, the genus was considered to contain a single species, *P. volans* (Kerr, 1792) with two subspecies, *P. volans volans* ranging from Victoria to around Rockhampton (Qld), and *P. volans minor* (Collett, 1887) found north of Rockhampton (ABRS 2021). Recent genetic analysis has provided evidence to support three operational taxonomic units (OTUs) representing northern, central and southern groups, which are supported by morphological differences (Jackson and Groves 2015; McGregor et al. 2020; McGregor et al. 2023). However, more work is needed to determine whether there are two or three taxa and whether they should be treated as distinct species or subspecies. Research is currently underway to resolve the problem. The Australian Faunal Directory (AFD) treats *P. volans* and *P. minor* as distinct species but retains *P. volans armillatus* as a junior synonym of *P. volans*. By contrast, the Australasian Mammal Taxonomy Consortium’s Australian Mammal Species List recognises only the two subspecies *P. volans volans* and *P. volans minor* (AMTC 2025). This recovery plan follows the AFD in referring to the greater gliders as two distinct species as listed under the EPBC Act. Additional information on the classification and redescription of *Petauroides* is provided in the conservation advice for each species (DCCEEW 2022a; b).

Broad scale population and genetic surveys are required to clarify the distinguishing characteristics for each of the OTUs, particularly in parts of Australia occupied by what is known as *P. volans*. Baseline genetic information gathered by Knipler et al. (2023) from samples of *P. volans* collected in southern NSW found a pattern of low effective population size and a high genetic structure, suggesting there was limited dispersal ability and isolation of some populations of this species in this region. This study also implies that there is a putative adaptation to temperature. Such research could be expanded to other regions to determine whether other populations contain functional genes that may facilitate adaptation to climate change. Determining the genetic health of greater glider populations across their range is required to better understand gene flow, the effects of genetic drift and dispersal pathways.

Maintaining evolutionary processes and safeguarding genetic diversity that help greater gliders adapt to change is important to the recovery of both species. Retaining genetic diversity can be achieved by retaining contiguous or connected habitat, or through managed interventions such as translocations for more isolated populations.

### 2.4 Habitat

The tree species and vegetation characteristics of greater glider habitat varies widely across the species’ distribution. In Victoria and New South Wales this includes a variety of eucalypt species (e.g., *Eucalyptus fastigata, E. radiata, E. dives, E. elata, E. obliqua, E. sieberi, E. regnans*, *E. viminalis, E. ovata, E. dalrympleana, E. pilularis, E. rubida, E. cypellocarpa, E. maiden, E. nitens and E. delegatensis, as well as Corymbia gummifera,*) (Norton 1988; Kavanagh and Wheeler 2004; Smith 2019). In Queensland greater gliders are commonly found in habitat dominated by *Corymbia* species*, E. longirostrata*, *E. crebra*, *E. tereticornis*, *E. exserta, E. latisinensis, E. fibrosa, E. moluccana, E. portuensis, and E. siderophloia* (Eyre 2006; Eyre et al. 2022).

Greater gliders occur across an elevational gradient of 0-1400 m (Lindenmayer et al. 2018a; Emerson et al. 2019) but are more likely to occur at higher elevations (Bennett et al. 1991; Kavanagh & Bamkin 1995; Kavanagh 2000; Smith and Smith 2020; Eyre et al. 2022; Ridley et al. 2024). They are generally recorded in dry and wet sclerophyll forests compared to open forest and woodlands with varying degrees of understory (Kavanagh 2000; 2004; Eyre et al. 2022). These habitats provide cooler, more stable microclimates, which are critical for greater gliders given their likely low thermal tolerance to high temperatures (Rubsamen et al. 1984; Beale et al. 2018; Wagner et al. 2020; Youngentob et al. 2021; Smith and Smith 2022). Such areas, even localised patches, support greater gliders during heatwaves and drought and are likely to be critical for the recovery of the species as climate change impacts worsen. Other habitats and areas that provide climate refugia include coastal lowland forests (due to the stabilising effect of the ocean on temperatures), areas with continuous or semi-continuous canopy cover, riparian zones, habitats sheltered from hot, dry westerly winds, forests with dense understorey and midstorey vegetation, and areas where hydrology or geology promotes water retention within the species’ range (Reside et al. 2014; 2019; Wagner et al. 2020; De Frenne et al. 2021; Smith and Smith 2022). Refuge sites generally have a history of low burn severity or no fires, and minimal changes in land use (e.g., land clearing, timber harvesting).

Greater gliders can be abundant in continuous habitat where there are stands of mature trees with large numbers of hollows, i.e., 2-4 per ha (Andrews et al. 1994; Kavanagh 2000; Eyre 2004; Kavanagh and Wheeler 2004; Van der Ree et al. 2004b; Vanderduys et al. 2012). Ambrose (1982) indicates that it takes between 40-80 years for trees to reach a diameter size of around 40 cm where hollows start to form. Greater gliders prefer forests with increased structural complexity, which helps to maintain or create heterogeneous microclimates at fine spatiotemporal scales (De Frenne et al. 2021). The level of landscape disturbance, fire frequency, intensity and severity (including extent of the fire scar), along with the concentration of nutrients (nitrogen, phosphorus and potassium) in the foliage in which gliders feed collectively influence their patchy distribution and abundance throughout their range (Kavanagh 1984; Kavanagh and Lambert 1990; Wagner et al. 2021b).

While larger contiguous habitat contributes to sustained population viability, both glider species have been recorded in fragmented landscapes with smaller habitat patches (McCarthy and Lindenmayer 1999a; Pope et al. 2004; Youngentob et al. 2013). Fragmentated habitats impose multiple constraints on populations with these being particularly pronounced for canopy dependent species (Keinath et al. 2017). McCarthy and Lindenmayer (1999a) reported that the quality of suitable habitat and the spatial arrangement of these remnant patches in highly modified landscapes must be complementary (i.e., both are needed to support the species) to have some conservation value. A strategy of connecting habitat patches through wildlife corridors is vital for building a biodiverse landscape and for reconnecting more isolated patches. Gracanin and Mikac (2023) evaluated corridor pathways and the connectedness of habitat in the Berry Bush Links (NSW) project. Greater gliders in this study were found to be the least connected of all targeted species. Although glider poles and rope bridges have been developed to facilitate movement in fragmented areas there is little evidence to suggest that greater gliders use these structures (Slapp et al. 2026).

In the Strathbogie Ranges (Victoria), Downes et al. (1997) also found that gliders were not present in habitat corridors with a minimum width of 32 m, even when occupying nearby forest patches. This indicates that greater gliders are a dispersal limited species and ideally require much larger swathes of habitat to facilitate movement (Taylor et al. 2007). Movements of only a few animals between patches may be sufficient for gene flow and may explain the shared genetic structure greater gliders have across parts of their range. However, further research is needed to determine the threshold of where roads and other gaps create barriers to recolonisation and genetic connectivity.

Habitat corridors facilitate movement of individuals, helping to maintain effective population sizes by allowing enough individuals to contribute to breeding across the landscape and reducing the risk of inbreeding and genetic drift (Lancaster et al. 2011; Frankham et al. 2014). Maintaining large old trees as keystone ecological structures within forest communities is crucial for providing both dens and forage, as well as movement pathways for greater gliders (Le Roux et al. 2014). Gibbons and Lindenmayer (2002) outlined that the partitioning of space between mature hollow bearing nesting trees varies depending on the forest community but generally ranges between 21-141 m for greater gliders. In areas where habitat has been maintained, greater gliders have been observed to follow exact routes through tree crowns indicating that olfactory cues may be important for movement pathways (Henry 1984).

Effective landscapes must address both the adequacy (enough habitat to persist) and the representativeness of habitat features to meet the requirements of greater gliders (e.g., tree hollows &gt;50 cm DBH, connected canopy cover and sufficient foraging trees &gt;30 cm DBH). Furthermore, as forests are dynamic environments, recruitment of hollow-bearing trees can be diminished due to direct removal of young trees, high-intensity grazing/browsing of young trees by introduced or native herbivores, competition with other plants including exotic species, and high frequency and/or severity bushfires (Lindenmayer et al. 2014). Ensuring an adequate ongoing supply of den trees will be important for the survival and recovery of all greater glider populations. A mix of native trees of different age classes should be retained in all areas where greater gliders occur to ensure ongoing recruitment, with rates of natural attrition of trees considered. The long-term viability of greater glider populations across landscapes is contingent on well-connected habitat that encompasses hollow bearing and foraging trees within a multi-aged, structurally diverse, forested community at scale.

### 2.5 Life history and breeding

Greater gliders are estimated to live for 15 years and have a generation length between six and eight years (Woinarski et al. 2014). Their mating system is typically polygamous but may shift to monogamy in response to reduced habitat patch size and limited food availability (Henry 1984). Members of the genus are polyoestrous with breeding restricted to between February and May (Harris and Maloney 2010; Ritchie 2024). For *P. volans,* males only produce sperm during that time. As a result, females that fail to breed or subsequently lose their young will not conceive again during that breeding year (Tyndale-Biscoe and Renfree 1987). Up to 50% of females do not breed in any given year (Tyndale-Biscoe and Smith 1969b). The relatively low reproductive rate may render small subpopulations in isolated remnants more prone to local extinction (Pope et al. 2004; Van der Ree et al. 2004b).

Sexual maturity in *P. volans* is reached at approximately two years of age with females giving birth to a single joey from March to June (Tyndale-Biscoe and Smith 1969a; b; McKay 2008). Young gliders usually spend four, sometimes up to six, months in the pouch and then another four months as a dependent nestling (Nowak 2018). There is limited evidence of paternal investment in young greater gliders and full independence from mothers occurs around 10-13 months of age (Lindenmayer and Lacy 1995; Nowak 2018). Little information is available on juvenile dispersal, but some juveniles are presumed to inherit a part of their mother’s range. Juvenile mortality of up to 16% has been reported (Smith 1965; Tyndale-Biscoe and Smith 1969a). Adult males can be antagonistic to their male offspring which may drive them away at an early age (Tyndale-Biscoe and Smith 1969b).

The life history of *P. minor* is not well documented and additional research is required although it is assumed to be similar to *P. volans*.

#### 2.5.1 Denning trees

Greater gliders are nocturnal and depend on the largest and oldest trees that have hollows, which they use for shelter and for rearing young (Lindenmayer et al. 2004; Lindenmayer et al. 2011). Both live and standing dead trees are used by greater gliders although they prefer live hollow-bearing trees as they maintain a lower thermal profile (Lindenmayer et al. 2017; Goldingay and Thomas 2021; Callan et al. 2023). Some tree species form hollows more readily than others (Gibbons and Lindenmayer 2002; Eyre et al. 2022) with greater glider dens being recorded in over 25 species (Goldingay 2012; Eyre et al. 2022). Disturbance that injures trees such as fire, insect damage or other physical damage may accelerate hollow formation (Gibbons et al. 2000; Adkins 2006; Lindenmayer et al. 2018a; Wagner et al. 2024). In Victoria, temperatures in dens of live trees can be up to 8oC cooler than outside ambient temperatures, which helps greater gliders remain cool on hot days (Rowland et al. 2017; Goldingay and Thomas 2021). During warm nights gliders may remain in their dens without feeding, which can lead to their energy and water stores being rapidly expended (Beale et al. 2018; Youngentob et al. 2021).

In Queensland, both glider species preferentially select eucalypts with a diameter at breast height (DBH) greater than 50 cm for denning, whilst utilising trees with a DBH greater than 30 cm for foraging (Eyre et al. 2022). The age of trees in which suitable denning hollows form varies depending on forest type but generally commence when eucalypts are aged between 150-250 years, representing one of the slowest ‘reproductive cycles’ for any organism (Gibbons and Lindenmayer 2002). *P. volans* has been shown to use between one and 20 denning hollows per individual within a home range (Norton 1988; Kavanagh and Wheeler 2004; Lindenmayer et al. 2004; Smith et al. 2007), while *P. minor* uses one to six dens within a home range (Comport et al. 1996; Starr et al. 2021; McGregor et al. 2023). Two studies have found that greater gliders were generally absent from sites supporting less than six hollow-bearing trees per hectare (Smith et al. 1994; Lamb et al. 1998).

Hofman et al. (2023) undertook a comprehensive study of den tree and hollow characteristics in the dry sclerophyll forest of the Seven Mile Beach National Park (SMBNP), Gerroa. Gliders preferred blackbutt (*E. pilularis*) hollows selecting those at ‘branch end’ with dead wood at the entrance that generally orientated to a northerly aspect (Figure 3). There was variability in both the depth of hollows ranging from 30 cm to 500 cm (mean 250 +10 cm), as well as cavity wall thickness (1-30 cm, mean 8.0 +0.7 cm) and shape suggesting that a diversity of hollows is utilised for not only breeding but also for short-term needs like predator avoidance.

Figure 3. Greater glider den hollows in *E. pilularis* identified at Seven Mile Beach National Park Source: Hofman et al. (2023)

Hollow height also varies depending on forest type ranging from 15-40 m off the ground and this was found to be correlated to tree height and distance of den below the canopy (Henry 1984; Kehl and Borsboom 1984; Lindenmayer et al. 1991; Hofman et al. 2023). Increased coverage from foliage at these heights is an important factor for den selection as it provides some protection from predators such as powerful owls and provides a staging point for gliding (Hofman et al. 2023). Hofman et al. (2023) conceptualised a ‘standard hollow’ from SMBNP (Figure 4) with average measurements representing (1) the minimum cavity wall thickness, (2) maximum diameter of the entrance to a hollow, (3) maximum cavity wall thickness at entrance; (4) minimum diameter of hollow entrances and; (5) hollow depth.

[image: A schematic diagram of a standard tree hollow and measurements]

**Figure 4.** Conceptual diagram from Hofman et al. (2023) representing a ‘standard hollow’ (n = 68)

The selection of denning trees is influenced by a wide range of factors including tree size and stage of senescence, aspect, canopy height, and total canopy cover (Comport et al. 1996; Stobo‐Wilson et al. 2021). Resident trees and movement pathways can occasionally be determined by the varying degrees of shredding or scratching of the bark where their claws have scored the trees surface when landing (Fleay 1947). Further research is needed to quantify the degree of variation in habitat structure and composition across different forest communities and what inﬂuences den-tree selection and hollow use.

The utility of nest boxes to replace dens is an area that is understudied. Nest boxes have been used to supplement the loss of hollows; however, greater gliders have only used them intermittently and breeding has not been documented (Menkhorst 1984; Lindenmayer et al. 2003; Goldingay et al. 2020; Brown 2025). Alterations to the construction of nest boxes and improvements to their thermal properties remain significant issues for greater glider habitation. Nest boxes designed by Greening Australia and Pro Con Wildlife for use in Victoria were tested for suitability and subsequently utilised by greater gliders shortly after deployment (Howard et al. 2022; Brad Blake, pers. comm. 2025). Gracanin et al. (2025) also investigated the uptake and use of greater glider nest boxes in the post fire environment of the wet sclerophyll forest in Tallaganda, NSW. Comparisons were made between nest box designs (plastic, plywood, with and without insulation) to address variability in internal temperatures and the gliders’ ability to thermoregulate. This study identified a rapid acceptance of nest boxes and offered insights into the role of nest boxes as supplementary habitat to assist population recovery following the loss of hollow-bearing trees. Further research into the use of nest boxes for greater glider recovery is needed at a landscape scale, across different climatic regions, with monitoring to occur over an extended period.

There also remains a limited understanding of effective nest box placement, with Hofman et al. (2023) suggesting that nest boxes should be installed above 65% of the tree height, or as high as feasible. McGregor et al. (2023) quantified the height and positioning of dens for *P. volans* indicating that trees selected for dens had a mean height of 31 m with the mean height of den entrances being at 17.34 m. In comparison, trees used for dens by *P. minor* had a mean height of 23 m with mean entrance height being at 15.15 m. This study was conducted at four locations spanning the latitudinal and longitudinal extremes of the genus *Petauroides* range (i.e., Bendoc SF, Blackbraes NP, Mount Zero–Taravale National Refuge and Wombat SF), encompassing open eucalypt forest and both dry and wet sclerophyll forest types. As tree height varies across greater glider habitat, the appropriate height for den placement in a tall eucalypt forest (e.g. *Eucalyptus regnans*) is likely to differ from that of a shorter woodland system such as coastal *E. racemosa* forest. Nest boxes could be a potential and effective tool for mitigating the short-term impacts of habitat loss and disturbances if appropriately deployed in some circumstances (e.g., immediately after fire events in some habitats) but retaining mature hollow-bearing trees in mixed aged forest communities is the highest priority for the species.

Mechanically carving hollows into tree trunks or branches to create artificial dens is a technique also gaining considerable attention (Griffiths et al. 2018; 2020; Best et al. 2022). This method has the potential to be superior to nest boxes as carved hollows may be more likely to be used, could last significantly longer (with appropriate maintenance) and may offer better internal thermal conditions (Jollie et al. 2025). Ensuring trees survive to an age to develop natural cavities is however the most viable option for population recruitment of greater gliders (Lindenmayer et al. 2003). Appropriate fire management to protect trees from early mortality or the loss of hollow bearing trees in mature forests due to more frequent and high severity fires is essential for long term glider occupancy.

#### 2.5.2 Diet and foraging

Greater gliders forage almost exclusively on *Eucalyptus* leaves, supplemented by buds and flowers (Comport et al. 1996). Despite their broad distribution in different forest types, greater gliders have similar dietary patterns across their geographic range (Henry 1985; Comport et al. 1996; Hume 1999). Eucalypt leaves are relatively low in nutrients and high in toxins, with the concentrations of both being highly variable between species and even within trees of the same species (Braithwaite et al. 1983; Kavanagh and Lambert 1990; Lawler et al. 2000; Moore et al. 2010; Wallis et al. 2010; Youngentob et al. 2011). Greater gliders prefer tree species and individual trees with higher foliar nitrogen content (Braithwaite et al. 1983; Youngentob et al. 2011). Young leaves generally contain higher protein, fewer toxins, and more water than older leaves from the same tree. Approximately 85% of the greater gliders’ water requirements are provided by the consumption of leaves (Foley et al. 1990). Water is presumably obtained from dew condensation on leaf surfaces or within canopy hollows rather than creeks or pools as gliders rarely come to ground (Rubsamen et al. 1984). Foliage nutrition depends on several factors including tree genetics, soil nutrients, CO2 levels, and weather conditions (Lawler et al. 1997; Wagner et al. 2021b). Many of the greater gliders’ favoured tree species are epicormic resprouters (Ashton 1976; Kavanagh and Lambert 1990; Cunningham et al. 2004; Smith and Smith 2025), suggesting that these eucalypts can play an essential role in supporting individuals after extreme events such as fire. Wagner et al. (2026) found that epicormic foliage generally has favourable leaf chemistry, inferring that regrowth after fire could be utilised by greater gliders if mature habitat features including hollows remain.

Greater gliders prefer to feed in tall trees or those with a DBH greater than 30 cm (Kavanagh and Lambert 1990; Smith et al. 2007; Youngentob et al. 2011; Eyre et al. 2022). Both species of greater glider exhibit seasonal and condition-based preferences for foraging tree species, with selection patterns capable of changing within a single night depending on palatability (Kehl and Borsboom 1984; Comport et al. 1996). It is believed that this feeding behaviour minimises the ingestion of toxins and helps to vary their nutrient intake according to their needs, e.g., ambient temperature, whether breeding or nursing young (Youngentob et al. 2011; Jensen et al. 2015).

The following references have extensive lists of food trees for *P. volans* (Kavanagh and Lambert 1990; Eyre 2006; Smith et al. 2007; Ferguson et al. 2018; Eyre et al. 2022) and *P. minor* (Comport et al. 1996; Vanderduys et al. 2012; Starr et al. 2021). There remain significant gaps in knowledge around the feeding ecology of greater gliders, particularly regarding tree species preferences and linking such information to critical habitat, habitat refugia (including after extreme events) and ranking the value of habitat for greater gliders. Like other arboreal folivore species, greater glider habitat is associated with mixed-age eucalypt forests that provide leaves of sufficient quality and abundance to support the nutritional requirements of a glider population immediately and over the longer term (Moore et al. 2004a; DeGabriel et al. 2009; Wallis et al. 2012; Stalenberg et al. 2014; Au et al. 2019; Kohane et al. 2025).

#### 2.5.3 Temperature sensitivity

Greater gliders have adapted to cool climate conditions and are sensitive to high temperatures, particularly when warm and dry conditions occur for an extended period of time (Rubsamen et al. 1984; Wagner et al. 2020). The acute consequences of extreme heat events on greater gliders arise from their physiological limits and dietary preferences. *P. volans* is unable to cool effectively at temperatures greater than 20oC (Rubsamen et al. 1984; Youngentob et al. 2021). During periods of heat stress, gliders decrease their food intake to reduce the additional burden of heat produced during foraging and digestion (Youngentob et al. 2021). This poses a risk as they survive on a diet with limited nutrition and obtain the majority of their water from the leaves they consume. At higher temperatures gliders cool themselves by panting and licking their fur, which requires an increased expenditure of water to stay cool (Rubsamen et al. 1984). Unlike many other mammals, greater gliders are likely to suffer higher mortalities during heatwaves or if nighttime temperatures consistently remain above 20oC they can become hyperthermic, preventing their ability to dissipate heat built up during the day (Rubsamen et al. 1984; Wagner et al. 2020).

There is little known about the thermal tolerance of *P. minor* but it is suggested that this species does not cope with ambient temperatures around 35oC for even acute periods, with body temperatures increasing dramatically over 38oC (Denise McGregor, pers. comm. 2019). High temperatures can lead to mortality, or during less severe heat events, may reduce milk production and growth in nursing young, and impact the nutritional intake and fitness of adult gliders. While direct empirical evidence for greater gliders is limited, these physiological responses are consistent with established patterns observed in other arboreal marsupials and mammalian folivores (Beale et al. 2018; Youngentob et al. 2021). Having access to appropriate hollows can be critical for thermoregulation, providing cooler, climate refugia on heat stress days. Certain tree species not commonly recognised as important food trees may also be essential for greater glider survival, particularly if they provide additional dietary water in their leaves.

Greater gliders are susceptible to heat stress, dehydration, and starvation during the following weather conditions (Foley et al. 1990; Kearney et al. 2010; Beale et al. 2018; Youngentob et al. 2021):

When temperatures are high, particularly if diurnal temperatures remain high for several consecutive days and when nocturnal temperatures are sustained (&gt;20oC)

When humidity is high and winds are low, limiting the effectiveness of evaporative cooling

During drought or dry seasons when tree leaves contain less water

When the humidity levels and nocturnal temperatures prevent dew forming on leaves.

#### 2.5.4 Home ranges and densities

Home range size for greater gliders can vary depending on the availability of resources to meet the needs of the individual, which can change according to season, age, gender, and reproductive status (Powell and Mitchell 2012). At the stand scale, the need for multiple tree hollows for denning and eucalypt species with higher foliar nutrient content shape the establishment and spatial configuration of individual home ranges (Wagner et al. 2021b).

Greater gliders have high site fidelity with home range size for *P. volans* varying from 1.2 – 4.4 ha (mean 2.7 ha), with males having a significantly larger home range than females. Eyre et al. (2022) provided a summary of studies relating to mean home range. The smallest recorded home range for this species was 0.8 ha in southeast NSW (Kavanagh and Wheeler 2004), while in the Brigalow belt bioregion, Queensland where there are open woodlands and less fertile soils, home range has been observed to be as large as 19.3 ha (Smith et al. 2007). For *P. minor* the home range size varies from 1.03-11.45 ha (Starr et al. 2021). Individuals of both sexes will have multiple dens (between 4-20) with the most frequently used trees being at the core of their range (Comport et al. 1996; Lindenmayer et al. 2004; Smith et al. 2007). Core home range areas for male *P. volans* are tightly maintained and do not typically overlap with those of other males. Home range size is therefore an essential requirement for both individuals and the population structure of *P. volans* (Comport et al. 1996; Kavanagh and Wheeler 2004; Pope et al. 2004; Smith et al. 2007; Eyre et al. 2022). Conversely, males of *P. minor* have some overlap in their home range but to a lesser extent than females (Comport et al. 1996; Starr et al. 2021).

#### 2.5.5 Dispersal

Greater gliders have limited dispersal ability, exhibiting a preference to stay within native forest habitats and utilising established corridors for movement (Woinarski et al. 2014). Both species traverse trees by gliding and climbing across interconnected canopy branches, usually gliding from the top of one tree to the lower trunk of another to maximise their foraging efficiency (Jackson 2023). McGregor et al. (2023) calculated the average minimum nightly distance travelled by *P. minor* as 740 m, being twice as much as *P. volans* with an average minimum nightly distance of 284 m. Greater gliders can effectively manoeuvre through the tree canopy changing direction mid-flight by up to 90 degrees. Where trees are not sufficiently close together greater gliders do occasionally come to ground. This is not their preferred method of movement as it leaves them exposed to predators (Eyre et al. 2022). Coming to ground can also increase potential for entanglement on barbed wire fences.

Greater gliders prefer habitat without steep slopes (Davey 1990; Stockwell et al. 1990; Wigham 2000) and can glide a maximum distance of 75 m (Taylor and Goldingay 2009). *P. volans* glides with an average angle of ≈45° and a glide ratio of ≈1 m horizontal per 1 m drop. This steeper trajectory suggests this species typically uses shorter, more direct crossings. The glide angle for the northern greater glider is currently unknown. The gap greater gliders can navigate depends on the height of the tree canopy and the distance measured between trunks of neighbouring trees (Table 3). Gaps between mature tree trunks of 1.1 times the canopy height or less and an assumed glide angle of 40 degrees allows individuals to land on trunks at least 2.5 m above ground (Taylor and Goldingay 2009). Females with young are likely to have shorter glide distances and will be less able to navigate gaps than conspecifics without young (Starr et al. 2021).

Unvegetated gaps wider than the effective glide distance of an individual form barriers to daily movements, dispersal and population connectivity; indicating that greater gliders are sensitive to disturbance tending to avoid forest edges and are less likely to cross man-made barriers such as roads (Dalton 30 April – 2 May 2018 ; Youngentob et al. 2012). Timber harvesting is known to adversely influence movement patterns with some greater gliders recorded moving distances of 0.4−2.8km along the ground, which increases their mortality risk (Tyndale-Biscoe and Smith 1969b; Kavanagh 2000). For long-distance movement it is also essential that greater gliders have access to eucalypt leaves to sustain their energy requirements (Foley et al. 1989) and hollows that serve as a refuge from predators.

Taylor et al. (2007) demonstrated the importance that patch connectivity has on the genetic diversity of *P. volans* when comparing remnant habitat patches (distances 1-7 km separated by pine plantations) and continuous native eucalypt forests. Despite there being some evidence of immigration, genetic diversity was lowest in patches that had been geographically isolated for longer. This indicates that, depending on patch size, eucalypt remnants may not support greater gliders for extended periods, but they remain important in providing refuge, foraging resources, or as a ‘stepping stone’ within fragmented landscapes. Youngentob et al. (2013) revisited remnant eucalypt sites and assessed the density and probability of occurrence of arboreal marsupials, finding that species recorded in the initial 1996 survey were still present in 2007, with no significant declines detected. The size of a remnant patch did influence the occurrence of *P. volans* at both time periods with much higher densities observed in the contiguous forest habitats in the second survey. Extensive areas of continuous forest habitats are essential to maintain links between populations and enable movement across the landscape. In general, dispersal behaviour for both greater glider species is poorly understood, thus requiring additional research on how far greater gliders can move at different stages of life in both continuous and modified landscapes.

#### 2.5.6 Habitat critical to survival

Habitat critical to the survival of a species refers to areas necessary for the species’ continued persistence and recovery. In determining whether an area constitutes habitat critical to survival, consideration should be given to the extent to which it:

Supports essential life cycle requirements, including foraging, breeding, social behaviour, home range maintenance, and facilitates dispersal

Is used during extreme environmental events, such as fire, drought or flood

Supports a significant proportion of the population or multiple populations

Contributes to the maintenance of genetic diversity and long-term evolutionary processes

Functions as a movement corridor between habitat patches

Supports long-term persistence through recolonisation or translocation

For greater gliders, the identification of critical habitat is based on the species’ functional ecology and the forest structural attributes required to support long-term population viability. Maintaining these attributes is essential to halt ongoing decline and enable recovery of both listed species.

Based on current information, the following attributes define habitat that is critical for greater glider persistence:

 Large, contiguous patches of mature eucalypt forest, with

• High densities of large (&gt;50 cm DBH) hollow bearing trees, (tree hollow density of &gt;0.8 hollows/ha)

• Preferred food tree species (&gt;30 cm DBH) of sufficient nutritional quality to support foraging, shelter and reproduction

 Forest structure and ecological processes that support regeneration, including recruitment of younger trees to ensure the ongoing development of future hollow bearing and food trees as older trees senesce

 Habitat in suitable climatic conditions, including areas that provide cool stable microclimates, and function as refugia from extreme heat, drought, and fire

 Sufficiently sized areas compromising the above habitat attributes to be able to support multiple viable populations over the long-term e.g., minimum effective population size of ≥500 individuals (see Frankham et al. 2014)

 Ensuring connectivity between sufficiently sized habitat areas, through corridors and buffer zones to facilitate movement, maintain gene flow and support recolonisation following disturbance

Refining what constitutes habitat critical to the survival of the greater glider is a priority action. All areas that contain one or more of the ecological attributes within the descriptions provided above have the potential to be habitat critical to the survival of the greater glider. Habitat that contains a higher number of the ecological attributes identified above, increases the likelihood that the habitat may be critical to the survival of the greater glider.

Further guidance in relation to the protection and management of greater glider habitat is outlined in Actions 2.4 and 2.6.

Additional research is required to better understand greater glider habitat requirements and support insights into species-specific behaviour and physiology, as well as the characteristics of foraging trees, including chemical composition and responses to environmental variation.

### 2.6 Distribution

Members of the genus *Petauroides* are widely distributed in eucalypt forests and woodland habitats along the Great Dividing Range from north of the Tropic of Capricorn to southern Victoria, at all elevations up to ~1400 m above sea level (Harris and Maloney 2010; Lindenmayer et al. 2011; Woinarski et al. 2014; Smith and Smith 2018; Emerson et al. 2019; McGregor et al. 2020; Starr et al. 2021; Jackson 2023). Their distribution also extends eastward into coastal forests, where there is a broad mix of subtropical to temperate eucalypt communities that constitute suitable habitat.

*P. volans* predominantly occurs on private native forests in NSW and Qld, and to a lesser extent leasehold land and combined nature reserves, whereas *P. minor* is distributed mainly on leasehold land (Table 4). Across both species’ distribution there are still large tracts of land that are not protected from future land use changes, increased disturbance, fragmentation and loss.

#### 2.6.1 Population size and trends

Although there is reasonable confidence in the broad distribution envelopes of both greater glider species, there are no precise or robust estimates of total population size for either species. Woinarski et al. (2014) provided historical population estimates for both greater glider species, being more than 10,000 individuals for the *P. minor*, and 100,000 individuals for *P. volans* with each species comprising of more than 10 subpopulations. Data from long-term monitoring sites have indicated localised and significant population declines (&gt;30%) over recent generations, i.e., 20 years (McGregor et al. 2020; DCCEEW 2022b; a), including in the Central Highlands of Victoria (Lindenmayer et al. 2011), as well as Booderee National Park (Lindenmayer et al. 2018b) and the Blue Mountains World Heritage Area, NSW (Smith and Smith 2018, 2025). There has been limited survey effort across both species’ distribution resulting in major knowledge gaps about their population status. Long-term monitoring is essential to improve the understanding of multiyear population dynamics (Goldingay et al. 2022; May-Stubbles et al. 2022). Alignment with established initiatives (e.g., the National Koala Monitoring Program) could provide opportunities for shared survey design, data collection, and reporting, creating efficiencies and strengthening outcomes for both species.

#### 2.6.2 Important populations

Important populations are those that are considered necessary for the ongoing survival and recovery of the species. Populations that are important for persistence of greater gliders are any:

That are sufficiently large in an area where key threats can be managed, with this likely to differ depending on the geographic location, land tenure and productivity of the landscape

That are important in maintaining connectivity in the metapopulation, or during extreme disturbance events e.g., fire

That have the potential to act as source populations to adjacent areas of suitable or potentially suitable habitat

Occur in areas of climatically suitable refugia during periods of environmental stress including droughts, heatwaves, and long-term climate change

That are genetically diverse or contain adaptive genes to current and future environmental stressors

That are geographical or environmental outliers

## 3. Threats and impacts

The main drivers of decline are land clearing, climate change, and the frequency, severity and extent of fire events. These threats may act in isolation or may interact with each other (McLean et al. 2018). Key impacts include habitat loss, fragmentation, and degradation, leading to reduced hollow availability and changes in forest structure, tree species composition, stand age and recruitment, density and distribution. Other threats that may have localised or regional impacts on some greater glider populations include excessive predation by native species in degraded habitats (e.g., owls and other raptors, quolls), injury and mortality due to introduced predators (e.g., cats and foxes), disease, inter-specific competition for tree hollows and entanglement in barbed wire fencing.

Research to date has predominantly focused on *P. volans* populations, with less understanding of the ecology and threats facing *P. minor*. Enhancing knowledge of these regional differences is crucial for effective recovery planning.

### 3.1 Habitat loss and fragmentation

Changes in land use practices causing the loss and fragmentation of habitat are a significant threat to greater glider populations (Pope et al. 2004; van der Ree et al. 2004a; DCCEEW 2022a; b; Wardell-Johnson and Robinson 2024; Cally et al. 2025). Key human-generated factors that contribute to habitat loss and fragmentation include agriculture and forestry, as well as the development of infrastructure for renewable energy, resource exploration and extraction (SLA 2014; Lindenmayer and Sato 2018; Ashman et al. 2022; Lindenmayer and Dickman 2022; Daly et al. 2024). Expansion of urban areas and transportation infrastructure, including upgrades to existing road and rail networks and new vehicle trails within forests, also contribute to habitat fragmentation and associated edge effects. Bushfires and climate change are also major drivers of habitat loss with greater gliders being particularly vulnerable due to their reliance on mature forest habitat and sensitivity to heat.

Habitat loss occurs at multiple spatial and temporal scales, with these factors directly influencing the severity of its impact on population persistence and ecosystem function. At a landscape scale, habitat loss not only reduces the amount of available habitat but also fragments landscapes. Fragmentation reduces the extent of and access to suitable habitat, increases exposure to predation and competition (Kavanagh 1988; Lindenmayer et al. 2013), limits movement pathways (Gracanin and Mikac 2023), and impairs gene flow between populations (Knipler et al. 2023). Artificial light at night may further reduce functional habitat connectivity by altering nocturnal behaviour and movement between habitat patches.

The low fecundity rate of greater gliders and their limited dispersal ability make them highly sensitive to fragmentation. Habitat fragmentation also amplifies edge effects, alters microclimates, and makes ecosystems more vulnerable to external pressures such as invasive species, bushfires, and climate change (Fischer and Lindenmayer 2007; Youngentob et al. 2012; De Frenne et al. 2021). Forest edges in fragmented landscapes are typically warmer and drier than contiguous forests, and more exposed to damaging winds, further diminishing their suitability as available habitat for greater gliders and making these areas more fire prone (Armenteras 2013; De Frenne et al. 2021).

Energy developments, including renewable energy and coal and gas projects, are an emerging and increasingly complex threat to greater gliders through direct and indirect landscape-scale modification. Energy development activities involve vegetation clearing and the construction of extensive networks of linear infrastructure such as access roads, transmission corridors, pipelines, substations and service tracks (Laurance et al. 2009; Northrup and Wittemyer 2013). Collectively, these developments fragment continuous forests, increase edge effects, and simplify landscape structure, reducing habitat connectivity and the effective area of suitable habitat. These disturbances are often extensive and long-lasting, removing mature forest and preventing hollow-bearing tree persistence and recruitment for many decades (Horner et al. 2010; Treby 2014). Breaks in canopy cover reduce glide pathways and increase fragmentation for arboreal mammals, even where total habitat loss is relatively localised (Forman and Alexander 1998; van der Ree et al. 2015).

Other impacts to wildlife resulting from energy developments include altered behaviour, reduced fecundity and breeding success, and changes in space use, such as movement patterns and home range (Northrup and Wittemyer 2013). Coal and gas extraction can also alter hydrology, soil structure, and local microclimates, indirectly affecting forest condition and the nutritional quality of foraging trees (Evans et al. 2012; Hovenden and Williams 2010). Where energy developments overlap with other land uses such as forestry, grazing, or fire-affected landscapes, cumulative impacts may exceed ecological thresholds for the persistence of greater glider populations across an entire region, particularly in regions with limited remaining mature forest (Lindenmayer et al. 2018; Cumming et al. 2020).

Native forestry encompasses a range of practices (e.g., selective harvesting, mosaic thinning, variable retention logging, salvage harvesting post fire, ABARES 2023), with similar landscape effects to those caused by energy developments. These effects vary in their potential impacts on habitat and greater glider populations, as well as across the species' geographic range. The degree of impact depends on forest type, harvesting intensity, rotation frequency, presence of any buffers and reserved areas, restocking techniques, landscape complementarity and the level of interaction with other disturbance events.

Greater glider populations can persist in harvested forests, though likely at lower densities than before timber harvesting, providing sufficient habitat quality and extent are maintained over space and time (Tyndale-Biscoe and Smith 1969b; Kavanagh 2000). Retention of at least 60% of the original basal area of eucalypts and a greater gliders home range has been identified as necessary to support occupancy and persistence within harvested landscapes (Environment East Gippsland Inc v VicForests (No 4) [2022] VSC 668). Larger population declines have been recorded in areas most heavily impacted by historical short rotation clear felling (Kavanagh 2000), although this activity is no longer undertaken. Population monitoring has generally been insufficient to determine the long-term impacts of less-intense silviculture practices on greater glider densities, especially where practices have been modified over time. Wagner et al. (2021b) investigated this and identified a higher threshold and level of importance in retaining aggregated habitat patches. Recovery of greater glider populations following harvesting is slow, with the species persisting at low densities or not at all within areas harvested for many years (Goldingay and Whelan 1997; Goldingay and Daly 1998; Kavanagh and Webb 1998).

In Queensland, important agricultural areas used for livestock grazing occur within, or adjacent to, remaining greater glider habitat that lies outside of formal conservation areas (Ferguson et al 2018; Norman and MacKay 2023; Queensland Government 2023). As a result, grazing practices can affect a substantial proportion of the species’ current and potential habitat. In addition to habitat loss through land clearing for agricultural practices, stock grazing and trampling of seedlings can suppress tree species recruitment, reducing the long-term sustainability of habitat. Over time, this can lead to aging forests with few replacement trees, exacerbating hollow shortages.

Extensive areas of connected mature eucalypt forests with hollow-bearing trees are critical for the survival of greater gliders (Lindenmayer et al. 1990). The loss of hollow bearing trees can directly impact an individual’s fitness and can reduce reproductive success, resulting in population decline and an increased risk of local extinction. Large hollow-bearing trees generally occur at low densities in forests formerly managed for timber production, such as the Central Highlands of Victoria (Lindenmayer and Sato 2018) and southern Queensland (Eyre et al. 2010), further limiting the distribution of greater gliders. Compounding this issue is the significant lag in the recruitment of new hollow-bearing trees, as tree hollows can take more than 150 years to form (Gibbons and Lindenmayer 2002). Greater gliders cannot persist in landscapes where the loss of hollow bearing trees exceeds their rate of recruitment (Tyndale-Biscoe and Smith 1969b; McCarthy and Lindenmayer 1999a; b; Eyre 2006; Taylor and Goldingay 2009).

Habitat loss fragments landscapes and can impede greater glider movement, increasing exposure to other threatening processes (Brooker et al 1999; Cogger et al. 2003; Bradshaw 2012). For greater gliders, key impacts that arise from the clearing and thinning of native vegetation is a reduced canopy cover, removal of hollow-bearing trees, and simplification in forest structure, directly diminishing the availability of den sites and foraging resources essential for persistence. Loss of surrounding vegetation types that help buffer glider habitat from threats can also potentially have a significant impact.

Habitat loss and fragmentation also impact the availability and accessibility of foraging trees. While research has investigated the minimum foraging habitat requirements in Victoria (Wagner et al. 2021a; Wagner et al. 2021b), these thresholds are likely to vary across the species’ distribution. Greater gliders are particularly vulnerable to the loss of access to high-quality foraging habitat. These pressures are compounded by the cumulative effects of climate change, which raise atmospheric CO₂ levels and increase toxic secondary metabolites (TSMs) in foraging trees (Hovenden and Williams 2010). Further impacts of native timber harvesting on greater gliders may be mitigated by excluding known habitat from harvesting, applying landscape-level strategies that limit overall forest disturbance, retaining suitable habitat corridors and hollow-bearing trees, and preventing or reversing declines in the nutritional quality of foraging trees (Kavanagh 2000; Eyre et al. 2010; Woinarski et al. 2014; Au et al. 2019; Lindenmayer et al. 2020).

### 3.2 Habitat degradation

Forest degradation leads to the reduction or loss of biological complexity within the ecosystem and can result in diminished species diversity, altered forest structure, reduced ecosystem services, and disrupted ecological processes (FAO 2011; Betts et al. 2022).

The silvicultural practice of thinning is the selective removal of trees from a stand and is aimed at enhancing the growth and health of the remaining trees to improve the quality of the timber yield (Gonçalves 2021). Thinning typically targets regrowth trees and when done excessively not only degrades habitat but can also reduce habitat connectivity and increase fragmentation. The consequences of tree thinning for greater gliders can be complex and variable, including how it interacts with fire severity and other threats. Thinning can reduce the availability of hollows and foraging resources while limiting the recruitment of preferred tree species, leading to an age-class bias within the forest.

Insufficient retention and recruitment of hollow bearing trees is a key threat to the ongoing persistence of greater glider populations particularly in production forests (Lindenmayer et al. 1997; Ball et al. 1999; Eyre et al. 2010). Keto and Scott (2024) outlined the impact that selective tree harvesting has on *P. volans* in Queensland, suggesting that, under current conditions, greater glider habitat is likely to be seriously degraded across 31–70% of the species’ range within the coming decade. In contrast, Horner et al. (2010) suggested that selective thinning can promote ecological benefits by increasing the density of large trees more quickly, potentially accelerating long-term habitat development in forest stands. Gonçalves (2021) indicated that thinning of smaller understory trees while retaining larger, hollow-bearing canopy trees helps maintain a more mature forest structure, canopy connectivity, and resources.

Additional pressures that can degrade habitat quality and alter forest composition include transportation and infrastructure developments, inappropriate fire regimes (e.g., burning to clear land) and weed invasion, with all having the potential to reduce the availability of preferred forage tree species and movement pathways.

In eastern Victoria and southeast NSW fast-growing tree species like silvertop ash (*E. sieberi*) or brown barrel (*E. fastigata*) respond to disturbance events like intensive or repeated timber harvesting with rapid regrowth, leading to altered forest composition and reduced browse quality (Au et al. 2019). Limitations on food and hollow resources can increase mortality and stress and reduce successful breeding opportunities, all of which can lower abundance and threaten the long-term viability of populations. Without ongoing management to alleviate habitat degradation, many fragmented populations are predicted to decline or disappear (Lindenmayer et al. 1999; Taylor et al. 2007; Youngentob et al. 2012; Youngentob et al. 2013).

### 3.3 Fire regimes that cause declines in biodiversity

Fire regimes across the greater gliders’ distribution have substantially altered over the past two centuries through changes in burning practices and the effects of climate change and other threats (e.g., timber harvesting, land clearing). Ongoing and projected climate change is expected to result in warmer, drier conditions across much of Australia, increasing the intensity, scale, and frequency of wildfires and altering seasonality, while also elevating the incidence of extreme fire-danger days (Sharples et al. 2016; Andrew 2020; CSIRO 2024). The most substantial changes are predicted for ash forests and sclerophyll-dominated vegetation along the east coast of Australia (Williams et al. 2009), which overlap with the distribution of both species.

Habitat clearance and fragmentation can increase the negative impacts of fires by limiting the movement of greater gliders and reducing their ability to recolonise burnt areas (May-Stubbles et al. 2022). Hard forest boundaries or ecotones also tend to create drier microclimates, not only reducing habitat suitability for greater gliders but also increase the susceptibility of these areas to fire risk (Armenteras et al. 2013; De Frenne et al. 2021). The structural integrity of forests is critical to the microclimate buffering capacity, with understorey vegetation density (&lt;1.5 m) and biophysical effects (e.g., temperature, humidity, season) all playing an important role in regulating conditions at the forest floor (Brown et al. 2022). While disturbance events occur dynamically in forest systems, their effects on forest structure and understorey processes can strongly influence fire behaviour and associated risk.

Changes in fire regimes, together with climate change, have complex feedback interactions with biodiversity, being both positive and negative (Williams et al. 2009; Driscoll et al. 2024). Following severe fires, greater gliders often remain absent for many years (e.g., &gt;10 years) from previously occupied sites, or they recolonise at a very slow rate (Andrew 2001; Campbell‐Jones et al. 2022; Smith and Smith 2025). Understanding the impact of fire regimes on species is complex due to the variability in the nature and context of individual fire events across space and time (Whelan 2002; Williams et al. 2009).

As greater glider occupancy is strongly associated with high (&gt;50%) levels of canopy cover, fires that burn the forest crown significantly influence glider distribution and pose a serious threat to the species’ survival (Campbell‐Jones et al. 2022; Ridley et al. 2024). In 2019–2020, bushfires impacted 6,920,462 ha (28%) of known or likely *P. volans* habitat (Ward et al. 2020). Fires not only cause direct mortality and injury but also alter habitat conditions, increase temperature extremes, reduce the availability of tree hollows and food resources, elevate stress levels, and increase vulnerability to landslides in heavy post-fire rain, weeds, feral herbivores and predation (Lindenmayer et al. 2013; McLean et al. 2018; Wagner et al. 2020; Starr et al. 2021; Jolly et al. 2022; May-Stubbles et al. 2022; Smith and Smith 2022; Robinson et al. 2025).

A single high-intensity bushfire or too-frequent lower intensity burns can result in extensive losses of hollow-bearing trees, and can change the composition, age and structure of forests to be more prone to fire and less resilient to drought (Gibbons and Lindenmayer 2002; Eyre et al. 2010; Lindenmayer et al. 2013; Lindenmayer and Sato 2018; Trouvé et al. 2021). Conversely, fire can also promote hollow development, particularly following low severity burns (Adkins 2006; Wagner et al. 2024; Taylor et at. 2025). Such fires have the potential to enhance habitat suitability in areas that do not result in a net loss of hollows for forest dwelling species like the greater glider. McLean et al. (2018) found that fire frequency increased hollow and hollow-bearing tree densities in unlogged sclerophyll forests. Sites that were both logged and frequently burnt had the lowest hollow densities, while unlogged but similarly burnt sites had the highest hollow density. The results suggest that fire frequency alone does not limit hollow abundance or potential availability for arboreal animals, provided trees are not removed and remain standing (McLean et al. 2018). Under these conditions, hollow abundance was positively related to fire frequency, although the suitability of fire-created hollows for occupation still requires further investigation, particularly across different habitat types.

In harvested forests, fire regimes are frequently altered by the combined effects of shifts toward more fire-prone or disturbance-adapted plant species, changes to forest structure and age, and the intentional use of fire for regeneration and asset protection (Lindenmayer and Sato 2018; Lindenmayer et al. 2021). These altered regimes can significantly affect the abundance of greater gliders in some landscapes. Landscape configuration, proximity to source populations, and the intensity and extent of fire influence the rate at which greater gliders re-populate habitat following a fire event, as does exposure to post-fire threats, e.g., predation, ability to forage or find hollows (Ensbey et al. 2023; Robinson et al. 2025). In areas subjected to both high fire frequency and high intensity timber harvesting, greater glider abundance declined more than in areas subjected to one of these threats (McLean et al. 2018). Further research is needed to better understand landscape-scale impacts of changing fire regimes and resultant shifting habitat mosaic on the recolonisation processes and population-level recovery for both species of greater glider.

*Management of fire*

Fire management includes prevention, preparedness, response and recovery (Holley and McArthur 2022). Prescribed burning is widely applied to mitigate the risk and severity of bushfires by deliberately and expertly lighting fires under specific conditions (AFAC 2015) for the purpose of protecting human life, as well as natural and built assets. Similarly, strategic fuel breaks (SFBs) are linear features where vegetation is modified and actively managed to reduce fuel loads and support wildfire suppression (Oliveira et al. 2016). They are designed to slow fire spread, provide safe access and egress for firefighting crews, and act as control lines for suppression activities such as backburning and prescribed burning. SFBs are commonly used in pre-season fuel management and during active fire events to prevent large fire runs from overtaking control lines and to reduce the likelihood of spotting downwind of the fire front.

Across the greater glider’s range, fire management is guided by jurisdiction-specific policies, strategies, planning procedures, tools and research that collectively inform fire management activities. Implementation varies depending on local vegetation type, land tenure, topology, the organisation(s) undertaking the fire management action, and the objectives of the burn. SFBs typically involve the reduction or removal of shrubs and understorey fuels, management of coarse woody debris, and selective treatment of hazardous trees, while retaining some canopy cover (Oliveira et al. 2016).

The expanded implementation of strategic fuel breaks, particularly in southeastern Australia, has prompted increasing discussion, reflecting concerns about both the perceived and actual effects on ecological values, including the removal of hollow-bearing trees, reduced canopy connectivity, and the creation of hard edges that alter forest microclimates. Future use of strategic fuel breaks should carefully consider their placement relative to key ecological assets and the risk reduction benefits they provide. Incorporating the ecological requirements of greater gliders into strategic fuel break design can balance protection of assets and personnel with biodiversity conservation and a functional habitat.

Other land-use practices including timber harvesting and farming can influence fire behaviour. Like forestry, fire management practices are frequently integrated into grazing systems to help promote pasture growth (Earl, 2014; Reid et al. 2014). Burn frequency, intensity, and season must be carefully managed to minimise tree mortality, protect hollow-bearing trees, and support regeneration, particularly in landscapes already stressed by drought or extreme heat (Nolan et al. 2020; DES 2022). If regimes are poorly applied, they can cause inadvertent or irreversible changes that may lead to population declines and biodiversity loss (Baker 2000). To minimise adverse effects on populations a holistic understanding of species’ responses and consideration of both the ecological benefits and costs of different fire regimes should be undertaken (Whelan 1995; 2002; Clarke 2008; Driscoll et al. 2010).

Fire has been used as a tool by First Nations peoples for millennia to maintain healthy, resilient landscapes. Cultural fire management can include burning or prevention of burning on Country for the health of particular plants and animals (Steffensen 2020). It may involve patch burning to create different fire intervals across the landscape and can result in fuel and hazard reduction (Steffensen 2020; Atkinson and Montiel-Molina 2023). Fire is applied as part of a broader suite of cultural land management practices, including habitat stewardship, species monitoring, and protection of culturally and ecologically significant sites. Strengthening Traditional Owner leadership and partnerships in on-ground fire management actions can deliver improved ecological outcomes, enhance biodiversity resilience, and embed caring for Country within conservation and recovery efforts.

Fire management regimes must be tailored to regional ecological conditions and cannot be applied uniformly between habitat types, and notably between northern and southern Australia (AFAC 2015). In the wetter regions of northern Queensland, planned burning may be an objective at a particular site at appropriate intervals to limit rainforest encroachment into eucalypt-dominated forests and prevent transition to habitat unsuitable for greater gliders (DES 2022). Conversely, in the generally drier forests of southern Australia, less frequent and less intense fires are typically required to avoid damage to hollow-bearing trees and other critical habitat structures. Too frequent fires can also cause severe loss of recruitment and seed banks for some eucalypt forest types. Fire regimes must also consider seasonal timing, soil moisture and fuel conditions to ensure planned burns remain at a low intensity to avoid damage, or loss of, hollow-bearing trees. Planned burns that are too intense or frequent may contribute to population losses or declines across the greater gliders range. Bluff (2016) reported that hollow-bearing trees affected by fire during planned burns in Victoria were 27.9 times more likely to collapse than those that were not burnt (0.9% collapse rate). Parnaby et al. (2010) found that following low intensity prescription burns in the Pilliga forests (NSW), mean collapse rates for burnt hollow-bearing trees ranged between 14-26%. This was consistent with the collapse rate of 25.6% found by Bluff (2016).

Overall, there is little data on the impact that fire management regimes specifically have on greater gliders at the individual or population level; however, initial investigations indicate that risks to greater gliders remain regardless of mitigation approach (Smith and Smith 2025). It is generally agreed that planned burns of lower severity and intensity will minimise canopy scorch and are likely to have reduced impact on greater gliders. While tree hollows offer some protection from radiant heat, the effects of smoke remain unclear. Monitoring for the presence of greater gliders occurs during some prescribed burns but generally there is not a consistent approach across all operations and likely varies between jurisdictions. Significant knowledge gaps remain regarding the ecological effects of changed fire management regimes on forest-dependent species like the greater glider.

### 3.4 Climate change

Greater gliders are highly susceptible to a warming climate because of their physiology and dependence on cool, stable forest microclimates, although climate impacts are likely to vary across their range and between species. Climate change poses a significant threat to their recovery, particularly with predicted increases in the frequency, intensity, and duration of extreme weather events such as heatwaves, droughts, severe storms and bushfires (Smith and Smith 2020; Wagner et al. 2020; Wagner 2021; Smith and Smith 2025). Heatwaves can also cause high mortality rates through heat stress, dehydration and starvation (Rubsamen et al. 1984; Foley et al. 1990; Beale et al. 2018; Wagner et al. 2020; Starr et al. 2021; Youngentob et al. 2021).

Recent rapid declines and local extinctions have been observed in some greater glider populations, even in apparently intact and otherwise healthy habitats with changes in climate considered a causal factor (Smith and Smith 2020, Wagner et al. 2020a, Goldingay et al. 2022, Smith and Smith 2025). In another specialised folivore (also of eucalypts), the koala (*Phascolarctos cinereus*), extreme climate events can limit breeding success by altering foliar chemistry, diminishing both the nutritional quality and moisture content of browse trees (Davies et al. 2014; Youngentob et al. 2021).

Climate change has complex effects on vegetation flammability, fire behaviour and ignitions, which can influence the severity of a bushfire (Nolan et al. 2020). Conditions leading to intense canopy-scorching fires are expected to increase under climate change, along with predicted declines in winter rainfall that will contribute to increased drought-induced habitat dieback and increased fuel loads (Mitchell et al. 2014; CSIRO 2024). The intensity and extent of extreme bushfire events have direct implications on populations through increased mortalities, loss and fragmentation of habitat, and will limit the availability of food for greater gliders. In the longer term, there could be shifts or changes in the distribution and composition of foraging trees so that less palatable species (e.g., silvertop ash) are given a competitive advantage (Butt et al. 2013; Booth 2017; Lindenmayer et al. 2018a; Matusick et al. 2018; Nolan et al. 2021).

Climate change is also expected to exacerbate widespread tree stress and dieback through prolonged and intense drought, heat stress, and altered rainfall patterns, leading to canopy thinning, reduced leaf production, and increased tree mortality (Allen et al. 2010; Anderegg et al. 2015; CSIRO 2024). These changes can diminish both the availability and quality of forage resources and accelerate the loss of mature and hollow-bearing trees, reducing denning opportunities and structural complexity (Gibbons and Lindenmayer 2002; Lindenmayer et al. 2012). Declines in canopy cover may further expose forest interiors to hotter, drier conditions, compounding thermal stress for greater gliders and reducing the suitability of otherwise intact habitats through altered microclimates (De Frenne et al. 2021). Collectively, vegetation dieback has the potential to degrade habitat condition at broad spatial scales, even in the absence of clearing or fire, thereby limiting population persistence and recovery.

It is anticipated that the total area of habitat that is climatically suitable for greater gliders will decline substantially within the next decade (Wagner et al. 2020). Kearney et al. (2010) predicted a ‘stark’ and ‘dire’ decline of suitable habitat for *P. minor* under a 3°C increase in mean annual temperature, with modelling indicating an ‘almost complete loss’ (≈90%) relative to its historical range. With future climate change both greater glider species will likely become increasingly restricted to higher elevations and forested areas with cooler, wetter microclimates (Smith and Smith 2020; Wagner et al. 2020). Range shifts to higher elevations may however be constrained by nutritional limitations, such as lower foliar sodium and higher concentrations of herbivore-deterrent formylated phloroglucinol compounds in these areas and homogenous distribution of tree species e.g., *Eucalyptus pauciflora* in the ACT (Moore et al. 2004b; Marsh et al. 2006; Martin et al. 2020). While some populations may adapt to or recover from these events over time, the increasing frequency and cumulative impacts of other threats, such as habitat fragmentation and changes in land use practices suggest that many populations may not recover without a level of intervention.

Severe windthrow events such as cyclones, or tornadoes, or intense storms are predicted to become more intense but less frequent in coming decades (CSIRO 2021). These events can cause high mortality rates to greater gliders and can significantly alter forest structure by damaging or destroying hollow-bearing and foraging trees, as seen at Seven Mile Beach National Park in 2013 (Vinson et al. 2021). A severe storm in Victoria’s Wombat Forest also reduced greater glider abundance by approximately one-third, with this study highlighting that mature habitat not only supported a higher abundance of individuals but also improved the species’ capacity to persist through a disturbance event (Cally et al. 2025).

Future impacts of climate change and the ways in which different climate processes interact with each other and other threats, remain key knowledge gaps in the conservation of greater gliders. Addressing these uncertainties is critical to supporting the long-term recovery efforts of both species. An important strategy to support greater glider recovery is the identification of climate refugia and the protection, restoration, and expansion of habitat within climate refugia, which will provide more stable environmental conditions in the face of climate change.

### 3.5 Other threats

Table 5 lists other threats that may cause regional or localised impacts to greater glider populations. Powerful owls (*Ninox strenua*), other raptors and spotted tailed quolls (*Dasyurus maculatus*) are natural predators of greater gliders. When predators become too abundant or there is greater competition for resources within an ecosystem, they may place additional pressure on greater glider populations (Kavanagh 1988; Kavanagh 2002; Belcher et al. 2007).

Competition with other forest-dwelling species can limit greater gliders’ access to suitable tree hollows. These can include native animals such as brushtail possums and cockatoos, as well as invasive species like feral European honeybees (*Apis mellifera*) or common mynas (*Acridotheres tristis*). Additional threats may include predation from foxes or cats (particularly if a glider comes to ground), and entanglement in barbed wire fencing with occasional deaths being recorded (DCCEEW 2022a; b). Compromised health in individuals may also lower survival rates and reduce reproductive success, threatening long-term population viability. An indirect threat that affects the health of eucalypts is *Phytophthora* fungus and myrtle rust (*Austropuccinia psidii*). ## 4. Recovery objectives and actions

### 4.1 Vision

By 2050, the long-term survival of greater gliders in the wild is secured. Populations are increasing so they are genetically diverse and resilient. Effective control of threats has achieved healthy, connected and high-quality habitats. This is strengthened by areas of refugia to support resilience in a changing environment.

### 4.2 Objectives

The objectives of this recovery plan are that:

| **Objective 1** | Population size and trends of greater gliders are monitored effectively, and populations show a measurable increase across their geographic range |
|---|---|
| **Objective 2** | The extent, quality, and connectivity of greater glider habitat is maintained and increased to support population persistence |
| **Objective 3** | Monitoring and implementation of recovery actions are achieved with the participation of stakeholders |
| **Objective 4** | Key knowledge gaps are addressed, and information is used to improve decision-making, conservation action and recovery of greater gliders |

### 4.3 Actions

Strategies, actions and performance criteria to guide greater glider protection and recovery are detailed below. Two primary actions are highlighted as follows:

***National monitoring strategy and program***

The creation and implementation of a greater glider National Monitoring Program is a key action necessary for the protection of the species. Due to the wide distribution of greater gliders in eastern Australia and occurrence across different forest types and land tenures, population dynamics and threats vary significantly across their range. A nationally coordinated monitoring program is essential to deliver an integrated picture of each species' status and population trend, while allowing flexibility to accommodate local variation in ecology, threats, and land management practices.

The aim of the program would be to:

Deliver a robust estimate of greater glider occurrence, abundance, population size and trends at regional levels and nationally

Provide a baseline against which conservation outcomes and recovery efforts can be evaluated

Inform assessments of threat severity and species conservation status

Support the design and targeting of conservation investments and land management responses

Identify and address knowledge gaps in species ecology, threats, and monitoring coverage

Foster the development and integration of innovative monitoring tools and technologies

Support inclusive participation from First Nations people, researchers, land managers, prescribed burns operators and citizen scientists

Build synergies with other initiatives, such as the National Koala Monitoring Program, by drawing on shared methods to improve efficiency and cost-effectiveness to strengthen biodiversity monitoring

A greater glider National Monitoring Strategy would aim to guide the implementation of the monitoring program by guiding:

Monitoring methods appropriate to local population demographics, forest structure, and threat context

Survey frequency for key populations, including repeat surveys across land tenures and under varying management practices

Standardised data collection protocols that ensure consistency in recording population metrics such as occupancy, relative abundance, body condition, behaviour, and habitat condition

Documentation of site-level threats and associated land management practices to inform adaptive conservation

Data management protocols to ensure monitoring data is recorded in a consistent, accessible, and interpretable format across jurisdictions

Consistent genetic sampling to track gene flow, population connectivity, and detect isolated or at-risk populations across the range

***Habitat protection and restoration***

Avoiding further habitat clearance and fragmentation, securing and formally protecting existing high-quality habitat is essential to ensure the long-term survival of greater glider populations and maintain connectivity across the landscape. While there will be a significant lag in the development of suitable hollows for greater gliders, restoration efforts will support the improvement of degraded forest habitats and help to minimise fragmentation.

Habitat restoration is essential for re-establishing critical structural and ecological features of forest ecosystems that support greater gliders. Depending on site condition and ecological context, restoration activities may include soil preparation, weed suppression, management of invasive animals, direct seeding, and planting of preferred food and hollow-forming tree species. This should include both active replanting and natural regeneration and be guided by the ecological requirements of the greater glider.

Factors that should be prioritised in greater glider habitat and restoration include the following:

Identify climate refugia and prioritise protection and expansion of suitable habitat. Focus on areas likely to retain suitable microclimates under projected climate scenarios

Reconnect fragmented landscapes. Restore and expand habitat corridors between refugia, remnant patches and larger habitat blocks to enhance connectivity and facilitate dispersal

Manage disturbance frequency and intensity by minimising and managing too frequent and high-intensity fire, land clearance and other disturbances in important habitat areas through strict guidelines and controls to protect key structural elements such as hollow-bearing trees and canopy continuity

Protect and recruit hollow-bearing trees. Protect existing trees with hollows &gt; 8 cm entrance diameter and promote regeneration of species known to develop suitable hollows over time

Ensure regeneration of multiple tree species. Avoid high-density monocultures (e.g., mountain ash forests, silver top ash) unless ecologically appropriate and support diverse species assemblages to provide a more palatable and resilient food base

Protect foraging trees with &gt;30 cm DBH and encourage recruitment of trees used for foraging. Promote regeneration of tree species preferred by greater gliders, including local eucalypt species and individual trees known to be utilised by gliders

Source seed stock adapted to local provenance. Where possible, collect seed from trees actively used by greater gliders, as these may produce more palatable foliage

Prioritise landscape-level restoration. Integrate protected areas, regenerating forests, riparian buffers and managed plantations to support a functional mosaic of forest habitat

Prioritise the protection of intact remnant vegetation and implement restoration to enhance or recover degraded areas. Limit conversion of natural ecosystems into tree plantations

Adopt an adaptive management framework that monitors outcomes over time and adjusts management strategies as conditions change or new knowledge becomes available

Embed stakeholder collaboration in on-ground activities. Support engagement of Traditional Owners, local communities, landholders and other key partners in restoration action

Implement species protection through policies, procedures, regulations and the allocation of land with the primary purpose of conserving key greater glider habitat and populations

### 4.4 Implementation performance

Implementation of this recovery plan will help to achieve the following outcomes:

Population trends indicate stabilisation and an increase in greater glider abundance across priority areas, with improved connectivity supporting genetic diversity and long-term population resilience

High-quality habitat and priority refugia are maintained, restored and protected across land tenures, with measurable increases in spatial extent and improvements in forest structure, forage species, and hollow-bearing tree recruitment across the species’ range

Climate-informed habitat planning guides reserve design, restoration priorities, and threat mitigation, with key climate refugia protected

Key knowledge gaps relating to genetics, glider movement, physiology, diet, fire and climate response are addressed and incorporated into adaptive management plans

Cultural knowledge and traditional fire management are embedded in fire planning and implementation across the species’ range, improving habitat quality.

### 4.5 Recovery partners and affected interests

Greater gliders occur across an extensive range and a variety of land tenures throughout eastern Australia. People with an interest in the recovery of the species include (but are not limited to) First Nations people and organisations, Australian Government, state and territory governments, environmental non-government organisations, local councils, private landowners and managers, researchers, forestry and other relevant primary industry sectors, tourism operators, and conservation/wildlife and community groups.

Conservation of the greater glider is also led by dedicated champions and researchers, and many community groups that play an active role in surveys, monitoring and education.

### 4.6 Effects on other native species and biodiversity

The greater glider is a flagship species for forest habitats across eastern Australia. Enhanced forest protection and recovery actions focused on greater gliders are likely to provide broad biodiversity benefits, supporting many other listed threatened species, as well as forest and woodland-dependent fauna and ecological communities. This includes other arboreal species that rely on mature hollow-bearing trees for nesting and foraging, such as other gliders (e.g., yellow-bellied glider, *Petaurus australis*), possums (e.g., Leadbeater’s possum, *Gymnobelideus leadbeateri*), marsupials (e.g., spotted-tailed quoll, *Dasyurus maculatus*), and owls (e.g., Australian masked owl, *Tyto novaehollandiae*). Many other mammals, invertebrates, and plant species will also benefit from the measures implemented to protect and restore greater glider habitat, including a variety of woodland birds and those that depend on large, intact forest areas for their long-term survival.

Several Ecological Communities listed as threatened under the EPBC Act are of importance to both species of greater glider, such as: ‘Upland Basalt Eucalypt Forests of the Sydney Basin Bioregion’, ‘River-flat eucalypt forest on coastal floodplains of southern New South Wales and eastern Victoria’, and ‘Subtropical eucalypt floodplain forest and woodland of the New South Wales North Coast and South East Queensland bioregions’. There are also several Ecological Communities listed under state legislation that are of importance to the persistence and recovery of greater gliders.
