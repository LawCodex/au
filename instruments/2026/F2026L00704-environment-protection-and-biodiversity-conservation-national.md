### 1 First Nations

The Australian lungfish is found on the lands of many First Nations peoples, encompassing the six river catchments/subpopulations of Burral Burral (Burnett River), Moonaboola (Mary River), Tinana Creek, Maiwar (Brisbane River), North Pine River, and Coomera Creek. The Wakka Wakka and Taribelang Bunda people in the Burnett area know the species as theebine (also dhil’vain, dhil’bvain; Watson 1933) and the Kabi Kabi, Butchulla and Jinibara peoples of the Mary, Tinana, North Pine and mid-upper Brisbane areas know it as dala (also dhal’la, dhal'lara, djelleh; Semon 1899; Watson 1933). To reflect this, an extinct species of lungfish from Tunuba (Fitzroy River) was formally named *Neoceratodus djelleh* (Kemp 1982b).

First Nations peoples have a close and ancient relationship with the Australian lungfish, which continues to be important spiritually, culturally and ecologically. The Australian lungfish is a key ancestral spirit in Kabi Kabi cosmology and is considered one of the first animals (Appudurai 2016). Information shared by Kabi Kabi Elder, Dr Evelyn Fesl AM, includes the following:

*Many years ago, thousands of years ago, there were no animals on the Earth, and the Earth Mother and our ancestral spirits looked down and decided that they wanted to have animals on the Earth, which would be their children. So, they designated that Dala would, first of all, get a lung, then it could get vertebrae and be able to walk, and that would be the forefather and foremother, really, of all the animals in the world* (Fesl & Franklin 2007).

This information accords with non-Indigenous scientific understanding of the place of the lungfish in evolutionary theory as the sister to all other tetrapods (Zhao et al. 2021).

First Nations peoples maintain stories and knowledge of the lungfish and remain as custodians of the species (Finney 2022). The lungfish is a totem for some families and clans, and is protected, together with its habitat, as part of family (Fesl & Franklin 2007; Fesl 2008, 2009; Kind 2011).

Indigenous Ecological Knowledge has provided for the protection of the lungfish (and its habitats) for millennia (Gibbs et al. 2025). This has required deep understanding of the species’ biology, spawning habits and ecology. For example, the “shallows where the water weeds grow” (Fesl 2008) are known to be of particular importance as a “breeding place would be near the edges of the river” (Fesl & Franklin 2007). The behaviour of the lungfish and its response to low-oxygen environments by breathing at the water's surface (Monteith 2001; Finney 2022) is also well understood and celebrated: “At night the family would see the reflection of the stars in the water, and hear the ripple of the water and the occasional explosion of air as Dala, our sacred fish, chose to come to the surface to expel the breath from his lung” (Fesl 2008).

Early European collectors relied on the knowledge of First Nations people to catch lungfish:

*Collaboration with local Indigenous people (in this case, unusually, local women and children) in the Ceratodus hunt was essential. Instead of fishing with pole and line, Masters noted that the local Indigenous people used a more behavioural approach based in their long experience of living alongside Dala (Finney 2022).*

Indigenous Ecological Knowledge and science can provide complementary approaches in a way of “two-eyed seeing” (Reid et al. 2020; Gibbs et al. 2025). This may include incorporating ideas such as *Mimburi*, which means “continuous flow” in Kabi Kabi, and represents a form of environmental law and understanding in the Mary River that seeks to preserve the system and its biodiversity, including by an awareness and protection of the lifecycles of its flora and fauna (Bond 2024).

An example of this is provided by Elders of the Auburn Hawkwood People in the upper Burnett River area who identified that the lungfish was not previously present in their local waterways (Auburn, Boyne, Burnett rivers) (Auburn Hawkwood People 2025). Lungfish only appeared locally and established in the wake of European settlement, likely explained by lungfish translocation, stocking, and subsequent dispersal during flood events, and further maintained by altered natural flow due to dam and weir construction (Auburn Hawkwood People 2025).

When a new dam (Traveston Crossing Dam) was proposed for the Mary River in 2006 First Nations groups, scientists, conservation groups and community came together to share information on the lungfish (Fesl undated; Arthington 2009). This prompted new research on the lungfish (Arthington 2009) which revealed the central role of the lungfish to First Nations life (Fesl undated; Fesl & Franklin 2007; Fesl 2008, 2009).

First Nations people are critical partners in in the protection and management of lungfish based on community partnerships around co-design, co-implementation and social learning. This approach has been taken in a number of projects based in the Burnett and Mary Catchments (BMRG 2023, 2024).

*The lungfish’s longevity of life and occupancy of our waterways is undoubtedly due in part, to its protection by our people over tens of thousands of years. We are still bound by this duty of care – the reason for my penning this document in an appeal for help for the survival of Dala* (Fesl undated).

##### 2.1.4 Distribution and habitat

The Australian lungfish is found in a series of coastal river systems in south east Queensland (Qld), Australia, namely the Burral Burral (Burnett), Moonaboola (Mary), Tinana, Maiwar (Brisbane), North Pine, and Coomera as well as a number of other small subpopulations. Adults typically inhabit deep, permanent, slow flowing waterholes, and spawning occurs predominantly in shallow river reaches and banks with dense beds of aquatic plants (macrophytes).

#### 2.2 Habitat critical to survival

Habitat critical to the survival of the Australian lungfish includes all areas within its known distribution, encompassing both flowing and still waters that meet specific ecological criteria. These habitats typically feature shallow areas (up to 1.5 m deep) with dense macrophyte growth (over 40 % cover); structures like submerged woody debris, boulders, overhanging vegetation, and undercut banks; pools exceeding 1.5 m in depth; and unregulated river systems that enable movement. Additionally, any newly identified locations supporting subpopulations should be recognised as habitat critical to survival (Section 4.5).

#### 2.3 Long-term vision

Maintain and increase recruitment and spawning habitat of the Australian lungfish and increase subpopulations to an extent that the species is no longer regarded as threatened and has been removed from the EPBC Act list of threatened species.

#### 2.4 Recovery plan objective

Ensure the Australian lungfish has healthy, self-sustaining subpopulations in all catchments in which it occurs.

#### 2.5 Recovery plan strategies

Strategies to achieve the objective of the recovery plan are detailed in Section 7, namely:

Improve stream flow and the extent, condition, and connectivity of habitat critical to the survival of the Australian lungfish across its range to optimise breeding and recruitment opportunities.

Minimise population impacts from artificial barriers to movement and recruitment, including by the removal of any redundant barriers.

Minimise the impact of invasive fish and invasive alien aquatic weeds

Minimise the impacts of climate change on the Australian lungfish and its habitat.

Minimise the impacts from water-based recreational and commercial activities.

Address key biological and ecological knowledge gaps to improve management of the Australian lungfish through monitoring and other research methods.

Increase public awareness and participation in conservation of the Australian lungfish and its habitat.

### 3 Introduction

#### 3.1 About the recovery plan

This National Recovery Plan for the Australian Lungfish (*Neoceratodus forsteri*) outlines conservation requirements and actions to ensure self-sustaining and healthy Australian lungfish subpopulations. This is the first recovery plan for the species.

A primary purpose of recovery plans is to give effect to Australia’s international obligations to protect, recover and conserve biodiversity and threatened flora and fauna species. The international obligations for threatened species and ecological communities are outlined in the Convention on Biological Diversity and its Global Biodiversity Framework. This plan sets out the management and research actions necessary to address these obligations with respect to the recovery of the Australian lungfish.

While the Australian lungfish remains relatively common within its limited range, the species is at risk of extinction through the loss of spawning, nursery and foraging habitat that has led to a population dominated by older individuals (DoE 2003). In 2003, the species was listed as Vulnerable under the *Environment Protection and Biodiversity Conservation Act* (EPBC Act) with the breeding population assessed to be small and at risk of declining, resulting in unsustainably low levels of recruitment, and with a limited area of occupancy.

The Australian lungfish faces significant habitat degradation due to human activities and environmental changes, which have altered both water flow dynamics and river habitat structure. The resulting loss of macrophyte beds impacts all life-stages, such as spawning, nursery, foraging and refugia habitat.

The impoundment of rivers has created large, deep reservoirs with fluctuating water levels, disrupting natural flow regimes and macrophyte proliferation, and can intermittently or permanently reduce key undercut bank habitat. Artificial barriers (dams, barrages, weirs and road crossings) also obstruct movement to and from spawning grounds and can cause injury or death of fish during over topping events and managed flow releases.

Further degradation of riverine habitat has occurred due to climate change (large floods, severe droughts, more frequent bushfires, increased temperatures), introduced invasive species, land clearing, and interactions with fishing and boating activities. Other threats to the species include de-snagging activity involving the removal of trees, branches, and root masses in rivers, trampling by livestock, and sand or gravel extraction from riverbanks and beds.

Understanding and responding to these threats is constrained by key knowledge gaps about the species’ ecology and biology, particularly regarding juvenile recruitment processes, as juveniles are rarely observed in the wild and current ageing methods do not allow for accurate back calculation of subpopulation level age distributions (Brooks & Kind 2002; Fisheries Qld 2017; Schmidt et al. 2018; Fallon et al. 2019). Additionally, the lack of data on population dynamics and the species’ assumed low and sporadic recruitment make it challenging to detect population changes over time (Kemp 1986, 2020; Brooks & Kind 2002; Kind 2011; Espinoza et al. 2013). Given the long lifespan of the species and its sporadic recruitment, assessing recovery and evaluating the effectiveness of management actions is difficult.

To support species recovery, this recovery plan outlines key research and management actions aimed at ensuring healthy population structures in all catchments where the species occurs. These actions include improving scientific knowledge to guide recovery efforts, enhancing habitat quality, optimising stream flow and water quality parameters, mitigating the impact of artificial barriers, building resilience in the face of climate change, managing threats from introduced invasive fish and aquatic plant species, and increasing public awareness and community participation in conservation initiatives.

#### 3.2 Conservation status

The Australian lungfish was listed as Vulnerable under the EPBC Act on 6 August 2003 (DoE 2003). It is also listed under the Queensland state *Nature Conservation (Animals) Regulation 2020* (Qld) under the *Nature Conservation Act 1992* (Qld). It is listed as a ‘no take’ species under Schedule 2 of the *Fisheries Regulation 2008* under the *Fisheries Act 1994* (Qld) (Table 1). The species was assessed as Endangered by the International Union for Conservation of Nature (IUCN) (Brookes et al. 2019) and is included in Appendix II of the Convention on International Trade in Endangered Species (CITES).

At the time of listing under the EPBC Act, it was suspected that the general population would undergo a reduction of at least 30 % over three generations (including the past and the future) based on changes to core habitat (main channels of the Burnett and Mary rivers), leading to an unsustainably low recruitment rate. The species was also assessed to have a limited geographic distribution (an area of occupancy of less than 2000 km2), which was suspected to continue to decline (due to ongoing threats), that is precarious for species survival. The species was also listed as Vulnerable under the *Nature Conservation Act 1992* (Qld) (Parisi 2023) based on a limited area of occupancy.

### 4 Background

#### 4.1 Taxonomy

Generally accepted as *Neoceratodus forsteri* (Krefft 1870).

The Australian lungfish is an iconic freshwater fish endemic to south east Qld, Australia (Kemp 1986; Joss 2011; Kind 2011) and is part of the ancient lobe-finned fishes that are sister to all tetrapods (four-limbed vertebrates) (Zhao et al. 2021). It is distinct within the lungfish species (Class Dipnoi), as it is the only one in Australia, diverging from the other five lungfish species in the world (in Africa and South America) over 180 million years ago (Zhao et al. 2021). Fossil records indicate that there were nine lungfish species in Australia during the Miocene, but by European settlement only the Australian lungfish remained (Kemp 1986; Pusey et al. 2004). The Australian lungfish is one of the oldest extant vertebrate species, with fossilised remains dating back more than 100 million years (Kemp & Molnar 1981; Berra 2007).

Unlike the African and South American lungfishes, which have paired lungs, the Australian lungfish has a single lung located dorsally to the gut, internally divided into two distinct lobes that interconnect along its length (Grigg 1975; Berra 2007; Hart et al. 2008). This lung is highly vascularised and compartmentalised, providing increased surface area for gaseous exchange, and is connected to the oesophagus ventrally, similar to air-breathing vertebrates (Berra 2007). However, while other lungfishes must breathe air to survive, the Australian lungfish primarily relies on its gills, using air breathing only during spawning, stressful situations or when water quality is poor (Burggren & Johansen 1986, cited in Lippolis et al. 2009; Brooks & Kind 2002; Kind 2011).

#### 4.2 Description

The following description of the adult Australian lungfish is drawn from Krefft (1870), Spencer (1892), Kemp (1986), Watt et al. (1999), Allen et al. (2002), and Kind (2011). It is a large, long-lived freshwater fish, with a lifespan of 50–80 years in the wild (Fallon et al. 2019). It can grow up to approximately 1700 mm in length and reach a maximum weight of approximately 48 kg, although individuals of this size are now rare (Brooks & Kind 2002). Adults typically have a brown to olive-coloured back, tending to white to salmon pink on the underside. Their bodies are covered in large, thick, overlapping scales. They lack fin spines or other sharp surfaces on their bodies or gill covers. They have four pairs of functional gills, protected beneath fleshy covers that close over the base of the pectoral fins. The pectoral and pelvic fins are large, fleshy, and flipper-like, which, along with the presence of a ‘lung,’ led to the species being likened to a gigantic amphibian (Krefft 1870). The head is conical but flattened to produce a broad dorsal surface. The snout and head have clusters of sensory pits, or ampullary organs, thought to be used to detect weak electric fields emitted by prey. The eyes are small, typically bluish or dark brown. The mouth is small with a limited gape and contains large crushing teeth on both the palate and the lower jaw.

Juveniles are similar in appearance to adults (Kemp 1986; Kind 2011). However, they are proportionally smaller with a slimmer trunk, have a more pronounced and rounded head, and display darker spots on the body that often cluster to create a mottled appearance. They also have more prominent sensory pores on the head and snout, and their dorsal fin is positioned further forward, close to the back of the head.

#### 4.3 Distribution

##### 4.3.1 National distribution and subpopulation delineation

The Australian lungfish is known to occur in six coastal river systems in south east Qld, namely Burral Burral (Burnett River), Moonaboola (Mary River), Tinana Creek, Maiwar (Brisbane River), North Pine River, and Coomera Creek (Figure 1). The last three of these rivers received translocated lungfish specimens in the 1890s in a bid to safeguard the species (O'Connor 1897). The species is found largely in waters running through private property (&gt;91 %), roughly one quarter of which is native forest. Approximately 3 % of the distribution is in protected areas.

While the Australian lungfish is reported from the Burnett south to the Coomera, the distinction between areas with native subpopulations and those with translocated ones remains unclear, as does the currently viability of some subpopulations. The Burnett and Mary river catchments (including Tinana Creek sub-catchment) are known to host viable, and uncontestably native subpopulations. However, there is debate about whether subpopulations found elsewhere are solely the result of conservation translocations from the Mary River in the 1890s (and possibly afterwards) (O'Connor 1897), or whether these rivers also supported pre-existing, endemic subpopulations. If the latter is true, then these contemporary subpopulations may have both native and translocated origins. Even within the Burnett system, the distribution of the species may have changed, as some areas now host the species that was not present before European settlement (Auburn Hawkwood People 2025). Therefore, the contemporary distribution of the species has been reshaped somewhat over time by human intervention (Auburn Hawkwood People 2025).

The phylogeographic relationships amongst the different areas, and their translocated or native status, has been considered in many genetic studies (Frentiu et al. 2001; Lissone 2003; Lissone et al. 2004; Hughes et al. 2015; Bishop et al. 2018; Schmidt et al. 2018; Biello et al. 2024). While differentiation between the areas is relatively limited, at least three distinct genetic groups have been identified, corresponding to rivers that host native subpopulations: the Burnett River, Mary River (main channel), and Tinana Creek (Hughes et al. 2015; Bishop et al. 2018). Although a tributary of the Mary system, Tinana Creek has a confluence with the Mary River main channel near the mouth in estuarine conditions, and so the two systems are distinct geographically and demographically for the Australian lungfish and many other freshwater species (Hughes et al. 2015).

To the south, subpopulations in the Brisbane and North Pine rivers are most closely related to the Mary River subpopulation, which matches with the historical documentation of their translocation (Hughes et al. 2015; Bishop et al. 2018). However, a recent modelling analysis of a sub-set of previously available genetic data concluded the most likely population structure, of those analysed, is that the Brisbane and North Pine subpopulations may contain a mix of local endemic and translocated gene pools (Biello et al. 2024). This study also concluded that the proportion of endemism in the Brisbane River is only 10 %, suggesting the current subpopulation is dominated by translocated genes. For the North Pine River subpopulation, the proportion of endemism is higher at 70 %, although this could relate to incomplete sampling of source populations. The possible native status of the species in Brisbane and North Pine aligns with some archaeological evidence (Kemp & Huynen 2014) but there is an absence of known documented or oral history in this area of the presence of lungfish.

Regardless of their origin, all areas currently supporting Australian lungfish are covered by this plan. Any translocated subpopulation meets the IUCN definition of wild, given the length of time since translocation, the original purpose being conservation, the lack of direct ongoing intervention, and the viability of the subpopulations over a 10-year time frame (Parisi 2023; IUCN Standards & Petitions Committee 2024).

The subpopulations (and management units) for the Australian lungfish are Burnett, Mary, Tinana, Brisbane, North Pine and Coomera based on geographic and genetic differentiation (see Figs. 1–5).

##### 4.3.2 National population status

Australian lungfish recruitment is naturally sporadic, influenced by environmental conditions that dictate the regularity and success of spawning (Kemp 1986, 2020; Brooks & Kind 2002; Kind 2011; Espinoza et al. 2013). Successful spawning conditions may be rare, with limited juvenile survival outside of peak periods (Kemp 1986; Brooks & Kind 2002). Some subpopulations are composed of larger, ageing adults (e.g., North Pine) with few juveniles having been recorded (Brooks & Kind 2002; Fisheries Qld 2017; Schmidt et al. 2018; Fallon et al. 2019). However, there is also evidence of periodic spawning and recruitment (Fallon et al. 2019), and so the status of the species’ recruitment is still an area of active research (D. Roberts 2025 pers comm 2 Apr).

The lack of juveniles being observed in the wild has been a longstanding challenge for researchers, with a general absence documented since the Australian lungfish was first described (Illidge 1893; Brooks & Kind 2002; Kind 2011; Fallon et al. 2019). It is unknown if the lack of observations is due to environmental pressures (e.g., the loss of suitable spawning habitat), survey method limitations, or naturally low recruitment rates outside of peak breeding periods (survivorship) (Brooks & Kind 2002; MRTSRT 2011; Fisheries Qld 2017).

Genetic diversity in the population is extremely low, suggesting past population bottlenecks (Kemp 1997; Frentiu et al. 2001; Lissone 2003; Hughes et al. 2015), although this could also be the species’ natural state. Despite low diversity, genetic analyses indicate that all subpopulations are genetically stable, with sufficient historical recruitment over the past 50 years to prevent genetic losses over the species’ long lifespan, despite varying levels of inbreeding (Bishop et al. 2018; Schmidt et al. 2018; Biello et al. 2024). Burnett, Mary and Tinana subpopulations have larger effective population sizes (a genetic measure of diversity) than either the Brisbane or North Pine (Biello et al. 2024). This is expected if a relatively large component of the latter two subpopulations’ genomes is derived from a small number of translocated individuals (Biello et al. 2024; see Section 4.3 Distribution). Nevertheless, genetic diversity of all subpopulations could be eroded over the coming generations, especially if reproductive opportunities are reduced by anthropogenic impacts (Hughes et al. 2015; Schmidt et al. 2018; see Section 5: Threats).

Over the past 50 years, major impoundments have inundated large areas of habitat in all catchments, creating unfavourable conditions for successful spawning and recruitment (Brooks & Kind 2002; Espinoza et al. 2013; Roberts et al 2014; Marshall et al. 2015; Kemp 2017a & b, 2020). Remaining downstream riverine reaches are affected by regulated stream flows and climate change (droughts and flooding), resulting in significant alterations to riverine structure and degradation of habitat (Kemp 2017b, 2019, 2020). Without sufficient recruitment, future population decline is likely as older individuals begin to die (Kemp 2020).

##### 4.3.3 Burnett River subpopulation and status

**Distribution**\
The Burnett River is the northern limit of the Australian lungfish’s range (Arthington 2009), and accounts for approximately 40 % of the known range of the species (Fanson & Todd 2017). The Burnett is a highly regulated system with extensive water infrastructure (Todd et al. 2025). It flows 435 km from the Burnett Ranges to Burnett Heads, north of Bundaberg, covering a 33,000 km2 catchment (DoEE 2014; see Figure 2). The Australian lungfish inhabits the Burnett River from Ben Anderson Barrage (tidal limit, approximately 26 km from the mouth of the watercourse (Adopted Middle Thread Distance [AMTD]) to approximately 10 km upstream of John Goleby Weir (AMTD 334 km) (Brooks 1995; Tait 2009). However, the core part of the subpopulation is within a shorter 250 km stretch of the river (AMTD 26–275 km), with higher catch per unit effort in the lower Burnett River (Brooks & Kind 2002). Key tributaries of the Burnett include Three Moon Creek, Barambah Creek, and the lower reaches of both the Boyne and Auburn rivers (Brooks & Kind 2002). However, some of these areas of the upper Burnett may not have hosted the species before European settlement (Auburn Hawkwood People 2025). An isolated site exists in Splitters Creek (Tait 2009).

**Population estimates**\
The genetic effective population size for the Burnett is estimated to be in the thousands (Biello et al. 2024), but the actual subpopulation census size is unclear. One study estimated 10,000 individuals in the Burnett River and its tributaries (Fanson & Todd 2017). In addition, estimates exist for sampled river reaches following a ten-year mark and recapture survey (2006-16; Fisheries Qld 2017). Sampled reaches include:

**Isis** (downstream of Ned Churchward Weir, at AMTD 64 km): ~ 10,000 individuals, stable (over the study period).

**Figtree** (downstream of Paradise Dam, at AMTD 119 km): ~ 5,000 individuals.

**Gray’s Waterhole** (upstream of Paradise Dam, AMTD 183 km): ~ 2000 individuals, stable.

**Claude Wharton Weir** (AMTD 202 km): ~ 5,000 individuals, fluctuating.

**Population structure and recruitment**\
The Burnett subpopulation is adult-dominated, with size distributions skewed towards larger individuals (Brooks & Kind 2002; Fisheries Qld 2017; Schmidt et al. 2018; Fallon et al. 2019). Fish in the Burnett are generally larger than in the Mary and Brisbane rivers (Fallon et al. 2019). Average total length of surveyed individuals (1997-2016; Fisheries Qld 2017) was over 900 mm, with a length peak around 1100 mm (Fallon et al. 2019). Few individuals below 750 mm in length were recorded (Fallon et al. 2019), and larger fish were predominantly female (Brooks & Kind 2002).

Spawning has been observed in flowing reaches of the lower Boyne River and the upper Burnett River (Brooks & Kind 2002). Key sites include areas upstream of Paradise Dam  such as Grey’s Waterhole (Brooks & Kind 2002; Clayton et al. 2006; Espinoza et al. 2013)  and a 7 km vegetated riffle section below Ned Churchward Weir (Brooks & Kind 2002; Espinoza et al. 2013; DRDMW 2024). Successful recruitment has been observed at Gayndah downstream of Claude Wharton weir (T. Espinoza 2025 pers comm 15 May), but there is no evidence of successful recruitment within any impoundment in the catchment (Brooks & Kind 2002; Fisheries Qld 2017; DRDMW 2024).

Carbon dating of fish scales, collected 2013-15, suggests a dominant cohort from 1955-60, with some more recent recruitment (Fisheries Qld 2017; Schmidt et al. 2018; Fallon et al. 2019). While the method of ageing has been questioned (Kemp 2015), inferred mark-recapture dates and known age fish have aligned with the radiocarbon dates (Fallon et al. 2019). Juvenile numbers since 1996 have been low (Brooks & Kind 2002; Fisheries Qld 2017; Schmidt et al. 2018; Fallon et al. 2019), but substantial spawning events, including the capture of larval fish and juveniles, have been reported (DRDMW 2024).

Water regulation has significantly impacted the Burnett subpopulation. Models estimate a 10 % decline in the upstream portion of the subpopulation, 64 % in the middle, and 1% downstream, amounting to an overall system-wide decline of 22 % over 80 years (Todd et al. 2025). Overtopping events at various dams and weirs, when lungfish can be damaged by impacting on the spillway, contribute to upstream declines (Fanson & Todd 2017; Todd et al. 2025).

##### 4.3.4 Mary River subpopulation and status

**Distribution**\
The Mary River flows 310 km from the Conondale Range, in the Sunshine Coast hinterland, to River Heads, covering a 9600 km2 catchment (~8410 km2 excluding Tinana Creek) (MRCCC 2001; DoEE 2014). The Mary is largely unregulated, with the exception of Lake Borumba and Baroon Pocket Dam on two of its headwater tributaries. Tinana Creek joins the Mary River close to the town of Maryborough in the lower estuarine reaches (Figure 3) and is not included in this subpopulation. The Australian lungfish is widely distributed in the Mary River catchment and its tributaries (Kind et al. 2008; BMRG 2024). In the main river channel, it inhabits reaches from the Mary Barrage (tidal limit, ATMD 59 km) to near Conondale township (ATMD 220 km) (Brooks & Kind 2002). However, its relative abundance is unclear (Kind 2011).

All major tributaries host the species (Simpson 1994; Kind 2002; Arthington 2009). Individuals have also been observed in Lake Borumba on Yabba Creek (Qld DAF unpub data, cited in Kind et al. 2008; BMRG 2024). The species can be found upstream in these tributaries until a physical barrier prevents further movement (Simpson 1994; Kind 2002; Table 2).

**Population structure and recruitment**\
Analysis of over 500 fish collected from the Mary River (2013-15) shows a subpopulation with mostly larger fish, with few fish below 750 mm (Fallon et al. 2019). Similar results were found in an earlier ‘snapshot’ survey of 46 fish, with mostly larger fish recorded, ranging from 476–1220 mm (Hydrobiology 2007), and a later sampling of 91 fish (440–1220mm, mean 928 mm) (BMRG 2024). However, electrofishing surveys do not always sample the smallest cohorts well.

Carbon dating of fish scales from 2013-15 suggests recruitment had recently occurred (Schmidt et al. 2018; Fallon et al. 2019). However, data on spawning events in the Mary River are scarce (Arthington 2009).

##### 4.3.5 Tinana Creek subpopulation and status

Tinana Creek joins the Mary River close to the river mouth (Figure 3). Salinity at the confluence restricts movement between the two waterways, as do several barrage barriers, necessitating separate management to preserve local adaptations (Hughes et al. 2015; Bishop et al. 2018). Tinana Creek is roughly 107 kilometres long, with a catchment area of 1190 km2. The Australian lungfish is distributed along much of Tinana Creek, but its relative abundance is unclear. Data on the population structure and recruitment of Tinana Creek are limited. However, there is likely a low population size, which is threatened by loss of habitat from impoundments and loss of individuals downstream (DES 2022).

##### 4.3.6 Brisbane River subpopulation and status

**Distribution**\
The Brisbane River flows 310 km from Mount Stanley to Moreton Bay, with a total catchment area of approximately 13,600 km2 (DoEE 2014). The upper catchment is dominated by two large, connected water impoundments, Lake Somerset and Lake Wivenhoe (Kemp 1986), which can store 134 % of the catchment’s mean annual discharge (Olden et al. 2019).

The highest density of Australian lungfish occurs in the highly regulated middle Brisbane River between Wivenhoe Dam (150 km AMTD) and Mt Crosby Weir (91 km AMTD) (Hydrobiology 2007). The species is less abundant downstream to the tidal limit (85 km AMTD) and upstream of the Wivenhoe Dam, including Lake Wivenhoe, Lake Somerset, and the upper reaches of the Brisbane and Stanley rivers (Kind 2011). The species is relatively abundant in the Upper Brisbane but has low numbers in the Stanley (D. Roberts 2025 pers comm 3 Apr). Surveys in the lower Bremer River in 2017, near its confluence with the Brisbane River, have confirmed the species’ presence there (GBIF 2025).

**Population structure and recruitment**\
The Australian lungfish is abundant in parts of the Brisbane River (Hydrobiology 2007). Before 1998, successful spawning was recorded for many years, with healthy eggs showing good survival and development rates (Kemp 2011, 2020). However, more recent evidence suggests a decline in successful recruitment, in particular below Wivenhoe Dam since major flooding in 2011 and afterwards (D. Roberts 2025 pers comm 3 Apr), with an associated decline in macrophyte beds in some places. An estimate for the mid Brisbane River section, between Wivenhoe Dam and Mt Crosby Weir, placed the subpopulation at between 10,000–15,000 individuals (Parisi 2023).

A ‘snapshot’ survey recorded 245 fish, ranging from 405–1175 mm (Hydrobiology 2007). Similarly, Fallon et al. (2019) found the Brisbane River subpopulation had a relatively uniform distribution centred around 900 mm (500 fish collected 2013-15). This study showed a higher abundance in the 600–900 mm size range (middle-age classes) compared to the Mary and Burnett Rivers at that time (Fallon et al. 2019).

Carbon dating analysis of fish scales collected between 2013-15 indicated a low number of fish aged within the 2010-2015 five-year bin range, and a more evenly distributed age structure compared to subpopulations in the Burnett and Mary rivers (Schmidt et al. 2018; Fallon et al. 2019). The presence of younger fish and a more even distribution of ages was linked to the likelihood of regular recruitment, possibly aided by regulated flows from Wivenhoe Dam (Kind 2011; Espinoza et al. 2013; Fallon et al. 2019). In the Brisbane River, lungfish tend to be smaller for the same age compared to Burnett and Mary, which may indicate some density effects (Fallon et al. 2019). In addition, population modelling based on flow impacts to macrophyte beds suggests adult numbers likely declined from the mid-1950s to the early 1990s, increased around the mid-1990s, and have been declining ever since (Todd et al. 2019). However, this may be the result of natural cycles for a long-lived species with low fecundity.

**Impoundment populations**\
Within Lake Somerset and Lake Wivenhoe, only larger sized individuals have been recorded, indicating low (or absent) recruitment (Hydrobiology 2007; Kemp 2017a). In particular, Lake Wivenhoe is a poor environment for the Australian lungfish, containing fluctuating levels of deep, cold water, with low dissolved oxygen concentrations, and few refuges (Roberts et al. 2014). The unsuitability of the habitat is evident from the poor condition of eggs collected from a spawning event in 2009, which failed to develop normally, and the few resulting hatchlings had such severe abnormalities that they quickly died (Kemp 2017b, 2019). The spawning event in Lake Wivenhoe has not been repeated (Kemp 2019).

**Enoggera Creek and Gold Creek Reservoir subpopulations**\
The Enoggera Creek and Gold Creek Reservoirs drain into the estuary of the Brisbane River. These subpopulations are considered functionally extinct, with no known spawning events occurring since 1974 (Pusey et al. 2004; Kemp 2011, 2019), although occasional adults are sampled in Enoggera Creek (Howell 2017). It has been suggested that herbicide use to control water hyacinth resulted in the loss of spawning habitat (Kemp 1995, 2019).

##### 4.3.7 North Pine River subpopulation and status

**Distribution**\
The North Pine River flows 54 km from the D'Aguilar Range in the Mount Mee State Forest to its confluence with the South Pine River at Lawnton, before flowing into Moreton Bay, north of the Brisbane River mouth (Figure 4). The Australian lungfish is found in Lake Samsonvale, Lake Kurwongbah and a short distance downstream to the tidal limit at Young’s Crossing (17 km AMTD).

**Population structure and recruitment**\
The subpopulation in the North Pine River is poorly understood, with uncertain recruitment, which is the subject of ongoing research (D. Roberts 2025 pers comm 22 Apr). A ‘snapshot’ survey collected 102 adult fish ranging in length from 675–1205 mm, suggesting a subpopulation dominated by large individuals (Hydrobiology 2007). The North Pine has an estimated total subpopulation of approximately 3,000 individuals (Parisi 2023).

**Impoundment populations**\
The North Pine Dam (20 km ATMD), built in 1976, forms Lake Samsonvale, inundating half of the river and blocking Australian lungfish passage (Kemp 2020).

Lake Samsonvale is unsuitable for successful spawning due to variable water levels, limited vegetation, and scarce availability of prey (Kemp 2017a,b). During recorded spawning events (2010-16), eggs were laid loose on the sand or gravel substrate among sparce plants, leaving them unprotected (Kemp 2020). Many washed ashore and died from desiccation or sun exposure. Eggs collected in 2010 and 2012-15 failed to develop, while those from 2016 survived only for 14 months after hatching (Kemp 2019). The cause for this lack of viability is unclear, but poor adult condition in 2009 may indicate food shortages.

The Coomera River flows 80 km from the Lamington Plateau to the Gold Coast within the South Coast Basin (Figure 5). ##### 4.3.9 Other Areas

The Australian lungfish was translocated into a number of other areas in the 1890s and since, including the Albert River, the Condamine River near Warwick, Blue Lake and 18-Mile Swamp on North Stradbroke Island, and impoundments in the Logan and Caboolture rivers. None of these areas are known to host extant subpopulations (Pusey et al 2004; Ecosure 2015; R. O’Connell 2025 pers comm 18 July; B. Rogers 2025 pers comm 29 Aug).

#### 4.4 Biology and ecology

##### 4.4.1 Climate

The climate within the distribution of the Australian lungfish trends from being sub-humid (warm, humid summers and cold, dry winters) on the inland slopes near the upstream limits to subtropical (hot, humid summers and mild, dry winters) along the coast (DoEE 2014).

South east Qld’s mean annual rainfall is 917 mm, ranging from 637–3081 mm. Rainfall is highly variable and seasonal, peaking from January to April (DoEE 2014). This seasonality results in periods of high runoff, regular high-flow pulses, and occasional floods, interspersed with droughts that severely limit water resources. December and January are the hottest months (mean 21–29 °C), while July is the coolest (mean below 10 °C). River water temperatures generally follow air temperatures, except in impounded areas and during water releases.

##### 4.4.2 Habitat

The Australian lungfish inhabits river channels, tributaries, and impounded areas across its distribution (Kind et al. 2008). Natural, unregulated habitat is characterised by short riffles (a few metres to tens of metres in length) and long pools (hundreds of metres to 1–2 km long, sometimes longer) (DoEE 2014). The species is restricted to permanent water (Brooks & Kind 2002) and cannot tolerate saline waters or migrate through seawater (Günther 1871; Pusey et al. 2004). Unlike its African and South American counterparts, it does not aestivate (a dormancy period during drought), but can survive for months in damp leaves, water weeds, or mud (Kemp 1986; Berra 2007; Hart et al. 2008; Arthington 2009).

Preferred habitats have structural complexity, including overhanging riparian vegetation, woody debris, boulders, and dense macrophyte beds (Kemp 1995; Brooks & Kind 2002; Kind 2002, 2011; Arthington 2009). Breeding, nursery, and foraging areas overlap due to shared habitat features (Kind 2002; Kind et al. 2008). Adults are commonly found in deep pools (&gt; 1.5 m in depth) (Kemp 1995), while juveniles remain in dense macrophyte cover (Kind 2002, 2011). Substrates vary by catchment geology, but typically include cobble, gravel, and sand (DoEE 2014).

Macrophytes are critical for food supply, predator refuge, and spawning (Kemp 1984, 1986; Brooks & Kind 2002; Kind 2002, 2011; Burke et al. 2024). They reduce turbidity (through increasing bed and bank stability, trapping sediment, and absorbing nutrients), reduce water velocities to intermediate flows (~ 0.2 m/s), regulate water temperatures, and generate oxygen, creating ideal conditions for embryo development (Kemp 1984; Brooks 1995; Brooks & Kind 2002; DoEE 2014). Spawning generally occurs in shallow sections (20–60 cm), among dense (~75–90 %), short macrophytes (&lt; 5 cm) (Brooks & Kind 2002; Fesl & Franklin 2007; Fesl 2008; Espinoza et al. 2013). The preferred macrophytes species include *Vallisneria nana*, *V. annua, Potamageton crispus, P. perfoliatus*, as well as *Nitella* and *Hydrilla* species, which provide structural support for adhesive eggs (Brooks & Kind 2002; Kind 2002; Kind et al. 2005; Arthington 2009; Burke et al. 2024).

##### 4.4.3 Hydrology

Stream flow is the primary driver of physical and biological processes in river systems. It shapes river geomorphology and sediment regimes, influencing habitat availability for organisms. Stream flow regulates food webs by transporting carbon and nutrients and triggers reproductive behaviour (Bunn & Arthington 2002; Humphreys et al. 2008; DoEE 2014). Variable low-flows during winter and spring are particularly important for sustaining dense macrophyte beds and stimulating spawning activity (Espinoza et al. 2013).

In their natural state, the lowland south east Qld rivers that the Australian lungfish inhabits have variable (often seasonal) flow patterns, characterised by fluctuations in magnitude, frequency, duration, and timing (Marshall et al. 2015). Natural flow functions as a ‘pulse’ disturbance. Floods, while potentially large, typically subside quickly, but can scour macrophyte beds having a multi-year impact on spawning habitat.

Low-flow and no-flow periods are part of the natural hydrological cycle, though their timing and duration are unpredictable due to irregular rainfall (DoEE 2014). These events typically occur in early summer (November-December) after the dry spring months and before the seasonal rains (see Section 4.4.1: Climate). During dry periods, base flow is often maintained by groundwater.

##### 4.4.4 Longevity

The Australian lungfish is a slow-growing and long-lived species, with a natural life expectancy estimated at 50–80 years (Pusey et al. 2004; Kind 2011; Fallon et al. 2019; Todd et al. 2019, 2025). Growth rates are highly dependent on environmental conditions and slow with age, sometimes reaching as little as 5 mm a year after sexual maturity (Kemp 1986; Brooks & Kind 2002).

Males appear to mature earlier than females, with the length of sexually mature males collected by Brooks & Kind (2002) averaging 767 mm compared to 834 mm for females. These lengths correspond to an estimated age of 15 years for males and 20 years for females. Both sexes are considered fully mature at 1000 mm in length (Kind 2011; Todd et al. 2019, 2025).

The reproductive lifespan of the species remains unclear (Kemp 2017a). The highest reproductive value is estimated between 20–40 years of age (Todd et al. 2019, 2025). Size alone may be an imperfect indicator of age due to the possible variability in growth rates influenced by environmental factors (Kemp 1986). The modelled generation time for Australian Lungfish in the Burnett River was calculated as approximately 46 years (Todd et al. 2025).

##### 4.4.5 Breeding biology and early-stage development

**Recruitment patterns**\
Recruitment in the Australian lungfish follows a ‘boom or bust’ cycle, dictated by environmental conditions (Kemp 1986, 2020; Brooks & Kind 2002; Kind et al. 2008; Kind 2011; Espinoza et al. 2013). Although modelling by Todd et al. (2019) calculated that a female can produce an average of five offspring reaching adulthood over a lifetime, this can only occur if conditions allow for successful spawning every year.

**Spawning season and triggers**\
Spawning occurs from August to November (Grigg 1965) when water temperatures range between 18–28 °C (Allen et al. 2002; Pusey et al. 2004; Espinoza et al. 2013), though the season may extend from July to January (Kind et al. 2005). Spawning is triggered by a sustained increase in day length for 6–11 consecutive weeks (Kemp 1984, 2020; Espinoza et al. 2013) and the presence of suitable macrophyte habitat (Kemp 1984). Additionally, low-flow pulses (30–750 ML/day) precede spawning events (Espinoza et al. 2013), and the timing of spawning coincides with the period of highest prey availability for juveniles (Kemp 1984, 1994; Brooks 1995; Brooks & Kind 2002).

**Spawning behaviour and egg deposition**\
Spawning occurs in both flowing and still water (Brooks 1995). In still water, shallow depths (&lt; 10 cm) are used where the highest dissolved oxygen levels occur, while flowing water maintains oxygen at greater depths, particularly with increased flow rates.

Spawning occurs predominantly at night, when aggregations of up to 100 individuals gather at dense macrophyte beds or other suitable submerged vegetation for egg deposition (Brooks 1995; Brooks & Kind 2002). These sites provide protection from predators, maintain appropriate dissolved oxygen levels for developing embryos, and provide abundant prey for hatchlings (Kemp 1984; Brooks 1995; Brooks & Kind 2002; Mueller et al. 2011a).

Mating involves elaborate courtship behaviour, typically characterised by repetitive circling, with male fertilizing eggs as they emerge (Kemp 1984; Brooks 1995; Allen et al. 2002). Courtship can be between pairs or small groups of one female and up to eight males. During these periods of heightened activity, individuals may breathe via lungs rather than relying solely on gills (Kind 2011; Todd et al. 2019, 2025).

Females usually lay 50 to 100 eggs per spawning, although they are capable of laying many more. Eggs are large (3 mm in diameter) and are usually laid singly, occasionally in pairs, and very rarely in clumps (Kemp 1986, 1994; Brooks 1995). Females deposit adhesive eggs within macrophyte beds, which provide structural support (Brooks & Kind 2002; Kind 2002; Kind et al. 2005; Arthington 2009), with eggs unlikely to survive if they fall to the riverbed (Kemp 1986). Females do not lay eggs in stagnant water, on plants covered with slimy algae or sediment, or if there is loose debris on the water surface (Bancroft 1928; Kemp 1986; Brooks 1995; Espinoza et al. 2013; Roberts et al. 2014). Unlike other lungfish species, the Australian lungfish does not construct nests or exhibit parental care (Kemp 1984; Kind 2011).

**Development and early life stages**\
The embryonic period lasts 23–30 days, after which hatchlings  initially photo-negative (move away from light) and sedentary  begin to feed after 2–3 weeks (Kemp 1982a). During this period, they are defenceless and avoid predation by lying motionless on their side in dense macrophyte cover (Brooks & Kind 2002; Kemp 2011). The period from spawning to free swimming can exceed 50 days (Kemp 1986, 1995) and underscores the importance of macrophyte cover. Cover is also critical for the approximate 8-month period post-spawning as the hatchlings develop into more robust juveniles (Kind 2011; Todd et al. 2019, 2025). Juveniles typically remain within macrophyte beds until they have reached at least 100 mm in length, at which point they may disperse to alternative cover such as large woody debris or undercut banks (Brooks & Kind 2002; Kind et al. 2008).

**Challenges to recruitment**\
Successful recruitment is not guaranteed following spawning events (Kemp 2020). In the absence of suitable habitat, females will shed their eggs into the water column, where many are lost to deep water or washed up into the shallows, exposing them to dehydration and sun exposure (Kemp 2011, 2020). Additionally, recruitment is affected by the condition of females prior to spawning (see Section 5.2.1: Availability of high-quality food sources), refuge sites and prey availability for young fish, and predation by various animals, including adult Australian lungfish (Kind et al. 2008; Kind 2011). Even under optimal conditions, mortality over the first year of life is extremely high, with only one in 500 eggs estimated to survive (Todd et al. 2019, 2025). Maintaining habitat connectivity is critical for seasonal flow event induced movement activities by lungfish (Espinoza et al. 2021).

##### 4.4.6 Diet and foraging

The Australian lungfish is primarily nocturnal, emerging in the late afternoon to forage in habitat such as channels, riffles, bars, and areas with dense vegetation and high macro-invertebrate productivity (DoEE 2014). Their diet shifts as they grow, adapting to their developing mouthparts (Kind 2011). Foraging activity levels decrease during the winter, when prey die back and are limited (Kind 2002).

**Diet and Feeding Habits**\
Recently hatched juveniles feed on *Tubifex* worms and small crustaceans, such as *Daphnia* and *Artemia* species, while adults feed on crustaceans, gastropods (*Thiara* species and *Plotiopsis balonnensis*), bivalves (*Corbicula* species), insect larvae, worms, tadpoles and small fish (Kemp 1981, 1986; Kind 2011; Tao et al. 2020). Stable isotope analysis of adult fin tissue indicates the most common prey are crustaceans, gastropods, and bivalves, however their relative importance depends on whether the habitat is upstream or downstream of a dam (Tao et al. 2020). While filamentous algae have been found in the guts of hatchlings and adults (which also contained macrophytes, fruits and seed pods), these materials are not digested, confirming all stages are essentially carnivorous (Kemp 2017a).

**Foraging Strategies**\
When feeding, the Australian lungfish uses its fins to brace its body against the substrate (Kemp 1986). The presence of ampullary organs suggests an ability to detect hidden prey through electric sense (Kemp 1986). Additionally, the presence of large quantities of silt in the gut, mixed in with food material, indicates that foraging occurs beneath the substrate. However, the extent to which the species relies upon this sensory modality for foraging is unknown (Watt et al. 1999).

##### 4.4.7 Movement patterns

In relatively unregulated riverine areas, the Australian lungfish is largely sedentary, displaying strong site fidelity with a home pool of approximately 1–1.5 km in length (Brooks & Kind 2002; Kind 2011; Espinoza et al. 2021). Adults often return to the same submerged log, rock, or macrophyte patch to rest during the day, sometimes for years on end (Brooks & Kind 2002). However, in the late afternoon and evening, they move freely throughout pools while foraging, leading to extensive home range overlap among individuals. Despite their large size, adults are capable of traversing riffle zones as shallow as 12 cm (Kind 2002). Although primarily nocturnal, movement has been observed during the day, including lungfish often seen surfacing to take a breath (Fesl 2008; CFCA 2017 pers comm 17 May). Adults have also been shown to undertake large one-way migrations of greater than 20 km during large flow events (Espinoza et al. 2021).

Adults can move across wet grass with the assistance of a downhill slope but cannot support their body on their fins without water for buoyancy (Illidge 1893). During large floods, adults have been observed avoiding main channel currents and turbulence by seeking shelter among bankside vegetation (Kind 2002).

**Environmental triggers**\
Movement in Australian lungfish is stimulated by the combination of environmental flow pulses and natural flow events. It is typically seasonal, with most movements occurring in spring (September to November), when individuals respond to lower flow thresholds than those required during other seasons (Espinoza et al. 2021). Nonetheless, movement has also been observed in response to flow events across other seasons, including summer and winter (Kind 2011; Espinoza et al. 2021).

Naturally timed elevated stream flows are essential to facilitate fish movements between pools (Brooks & Kind 2002; Berghuis & Broadfoot 2004a,b; Kind 2011; Espinoza et al. 2013, 2021). Adults take advantage of increased connectivity to move between pools (Brooks & Kind 2002; Berghuis & Broadfoot 2004a,b; Kind 2011; Espinoza et al. 2013, 2021). Large numbers of fish congregating in small areas during spawning can create competition for prey, making a facilitated return to home pools through managed flow releases beneficial (Brooks & Kind 2002).

Although site fidelity is strong, adults have been tracked moving significant distances, with a mean cumulative distance 6 km (Espinoza et al. 2021). Smaller water level rises (&lt; 30 cm) can trigger movement, while larger flow events, such as those occurring in late summer to early autumn (February to May), have led to movements of up to 23 km. Some individuals do not return to their previous home pool after such events (Espinoza et al. 2021).

**Movements in regulated and unregulated systems**\
Fish in impoundments move greater distances than those in natural river reaches. A tagged individual in the Burnett River was recorded travelling approximately 48 km between Ned Churchward Weir to Ben Anderson Barrage on at least four occasions (Brooks & Kind 2002). The reason for this increased movement is unclear but may be related to the search for suitable spawning habitat. Observations of large-scale movements in both regulated and unregulated areas emphasise the importance of maintaining fish passage throughout catchments (Todd et al. 2019, 2025).

#### 4.5 Habitat critical to survival

Habitat critical to survival of a species refers to areas that are necessary:

For foraging, spawning, juvenile grounds, refuge or dispersal;

For the long-term maintenance of the species (including the maintenance of other species essential to the survival of the species);

To maintain genetic diversity and the potential for long-term evolutionary change;

For the reintroduction of subpopulations or recovery of the species.

The spawning, foraging, and refuge habitats of the Australian lungfish share similar features and frequently coincide or overlap.

##### 4.5.1 Spawning habitat

All known or likely spawning habitat within the distribution is critical to the survival of the Australian lungfish. Typical spawning habitat consists of shallow, flowing stream sections with dense beds of submerged native macrophytes. Key environmental factors include low-turbidity, high dissolved oxygen, and water temperatures between 18–28 °C. Continuous flows must be maintained during embryo and hatchling development. Periods of rapid inundation or water level drawdown can lead to macrophyte loss. Drawdown can degrade beds through exposure and desiccation, while inundation can scour beds or take plants past their light attenuation point.

##### 4.5.2 Foraging habitat

All known and likely foraging habitat within the distribution is habitat critical to the Australian lungfish. Shallow riffles and runs support high productivity of macroinvertebrates and microscopic organisms, which are essential prey for the Australian lungfish. Dense macrophyte beds are vital for hatchlings, which employ a sit-and-wait ambush hunting strategy (Kind 2011; DoEE 2014).

If adults have not been able to gather sufficient suitable high quality food sources, such as crustaceans, gastropods and bivalves, eggs may be deficient in nutrients for development (see Section 5.2.1: Availability of high-quality food sources).

##### 4.5.3 Refuge habitat

Refuge areas for hatchlings are critical for recruitment success (Kemp 1995, 2007). Dense macrophytes beds provide shelter for hatchlings and juveniles, while partially submerged riparian vegetation, undercut banks, and submerged woody debris and boulders offer protection for larger fish (Kemp 1995; Brooks & Kind 2002; Kind 2002). Adults typically rest during the day in deep or shady permanent pools (&gt; 1.5 m in depth) that also share these features.

##### 4.5.4 Habitat for the long-term maintenance of the species

All known and likely natural riverine habitats between spawning and foraging grounds are critical to the survival of the Australian lungfish. This includes deep pools, as well as seasonal and perennial riffle, run, and glide habitats that enable movement between reaches within the distribution. Naturally timed, elevated stream flows are essential to facilitate fish movements between pools (Espinoza et al. 2021).

Maintaining habitat connectivity is critical to prevent fragmentation and allow movement and genetic exchange. The Australian lungfish also likely moves between different sections of the river in response to local conditions, including loss of macrophyte beds due to disturbance or scouring.

### 5 Threats

#### 5.1 Background to threats

While adult Australian lungfish are relatively common in some areas, concerns persist about their long-term survival due to low juvenile recruitment, as indicated by surveys (Kemp 1986, 2020; Brooks & Kind 2002; DoE 2003; Kind 2011; Espinoza et al. 2013). Recruitment is naturally sporadic, and adults may delay or skip breeding if their spawning habitat is disturbed or unavailable (Kemp 1984, 1986, 1993, 2020; Brooks & Kind 2002; Kind 2011; Espinoza et al. 2013). Population viability depends on periodic recruitment peaks, when suitable environmental conditions support large spawning events and hatchling survival. However, habitat loss and degradation over the past half century have reduced successful spawning, leaving subpopulations dominated by older individuals (DoE 2003).

Analyses indicates that the Burnett, Mary, and Brisbane subpopulations are genetically stable, and low recruitment has not translated into genetic erosion (Schmidt et al. 2018). However, a decline in the adult population, together with a continued reduction in the number of recruitment opportunities, through anthropogenic and natural causes, could lead to a loss in genetic diversity. In long-lived species with low adult mortality (such as the Australian lungfish), failed recruitment can be masked for extended periods, with an ageing population eventually rapidly declining (IUCN Standards & Petitions Committee 2024).

Ecological knowledge gaps remain regarding the early lifecycle of the Australian lungfish, particularly hatchling and juvenile habitat use and the impact of threats, which slow recovery efforts. Large areas of flowing riverine habitat have been converted into ponded reservoirs, with regulated downstream flows altered from the natural state (Brooks & Kind 2002; Espinoza et al. 2013; Marshall et al. 2015; Kemp 2019, 2020). Climate change appears to be altering flow regimes, resulting in extended dry periods, and destructive, more frequent flooding. In the riparian zone, agricultural land-clearing, livestock trampling, invasive weed species and river channels modification through sand and gravel extraction and de-snagging activity have exacerbated habitat degradation (Arthington 2009).

Beyond habitat loss, threats to the species include obstructed movement from instream barriers reducing genetic exchange, injury and death from dam over-topping and water releases, interaction with fishing and boating activities, and the establishment of introduced invasive species (Kind et al. 2008; Arthington 2009; Brooks et al 2019).

The relative order of the severity of various threats to the Australian lungfish can vary temporally and spatially. However, specific threats do not exist in isolation but interact and intensify each other. For example, habitat can be degraded by both altered hydrology and climate change-induced drought, leading to more intense pressure from invasive species.

#### 5.2 Current threatening processes

##### 5.2.1 Water impoundments and stream flow regulation

The Australian lungfish inhabits Queensland’s most densely populated and fastest growing regions, where increasing demand on water resources have led to widespread impoundments (Kind 2011). These barriers have significantly altered riverine environments by inundating upstream habitat, modifying downstream flows, degrading water quality, and limiting adult migration. The Burnett and Brisbane rivers are heavily regulated, with 196 % and 134 % of mean annual discharge stored in impoundments, compared to just 9 % in the Mary River (Olden et al. 2019). There are plans for a new pumped hydro scheme for the Mary at Lake Borumba (Queensland Hydro 2024). There are significant numbers of dams, gauging weirs, and barrages throughout the range of the species (see Table 2).

###### Habitat degradation and water quality

Within the distribution of the Australian lungfish, flowing water reaches have been replaced by reservoirs and weir pools with largely standing water habitat, which are suboptimal for the species, particularly for spawning, egg and hatchling survival (Roberts et al. 2014). Compared to natural riverine systems, water level fluctuations are more pronounced (often &gt; 1 m) and occur more frequently, limiting the establishment of macrophytes and reducing the length of time beds are sustained (Duivenvoorden 2008; Kemp 2011; DoEE 2014; Marshal et al. 2015). Even under suitable conditions, macrophyte colonisation remains minimal within reservoirs (Qld government data cited in Marshal et al. 2015). Slower-moving water causes propagules to lose buoyancy more rapidly, reducing dispersal distances (Jones et al. 2020; Burke et al. 2024). Dams and barriers can also trap propagules, further hindering macrophyte recovery by reducing dispersal (Jones et al. 2020; Burke et al. 2024). Moreover, flow events that do not reach downstream habitats limit macrophyte growth in areas that would otherwise provide the greatest benefit to the species (Espinoza et al. 2013; Marshall et al. 2015).

The decline of macrophyte beds can have compounding effects, as these habitats support critical lungfish life history functions, including spawning, foraging, and refuge. Reductions in macrophyte abundance may lead to overgrazing of remaining patches by other species, further depleting plant availability and impeding natural regeneration.

Lungfish spawning within reservoirs is rare, with little evidence of successful recruitment (Kemp 1986, 2011, 2017a,b, 2019; Brooks & Kind 2002). Impoundments are largely unsuitable for embryo and hatchling survival (Kemp 1994, 2007, 2011, 2014, 2017b; Brooks & Kind 2002; Mueller et al. 2011b; Roberts et al. 2014). Where the egg or embryo is damaged due to adverse conditions, there is an increased rate of bacterial or fungal infections, further reducing survivorship (Kemp 1994; Brooks & Kind 2002; Kind et al. 2005). There is also an unnaturally large biomass of potential predators and invasive fish, that can impact eggs and embryos. Even within the more productive littoral zone of reservoirs (where plants can become established), water temperatures can vary by ~ 20 °C and dissolved oxygen levels can be close to zero (Roberts et al. 2014).

Impoundments and other barriers also disrupt nutrient, sediment, and organic matter flow to downstream reaches, leading to sediment starvation, bed armouring, and reduced riffle and pool habitat formation (Brizga et al. 2000; Bunn & Arthington 2002; DoEE 2014; Olden et al. 2019). Flood control reductions further impact channel-forming processes, diminishing available habitat (Brizga et al. 2000; DoEE 2014). During water releases, water quality can also be diminished, particularly if cold, deoxygenated water is released from the bottom of a dam or weir (Kemp 1994; Gordos et al. 2007).

###### Altered flow regimes

Barriers can significantly alter the natural variability of stream flows and the magnitude and duration of flow events downstream (Kingsford 2000; Qld DSITIA 2013). In the Mary River, an average of 85 % of natural flow reaches the lower reaches, whereas in the Burnett and Brisbane it is 70 % and 66 %, respectively (Qld government data, cited in Marshal et al. 2015). This can lead to low or no flows conditions, impacts on environmental triggers, fragmented subpopulations and stranded fish (Brizga et al. 2000).

Altered flow regimes impact macrophyte abundance and distribution (Qld DSITIA 2013). Bulk water releases can scour macrophyte beds, which can take years to recover (Brooks & Kind 2002; Espinoza et al. 2013; Qld DSITIA 2013; Marshall et al. 2015). Conversely, managed flows from large impoundments could attenuate minor flood peaks, reducing the frequency and severity of small unseasonal flood events by storing water and releasing it gradually to meet downstream demand (Mackay 2014). This approach may help sustain or enhance macrophyte habitat and spawning opportunities that would otherwise be negatively affected. A reduction in available spawning habit can result in overcrowding and competition, and increased rates of predation and disease (Brooks & Kind 2002).

Hydrological changes also promote invasive species to proliferate, lead to sediment deposition, degrade water quality, and disrupt movement and spawning cues (Brizga et al. 2000; Bunn & Arthington 2002; DoEE 2014; Espinoza et al. 2021). Climate change further exacerbates these effects (see Section 5.2.2: Climate change).

Environmental flow regulation exists in the Burnett River, requiring dam operators to release water for Australian lungfish spawning (Qld DERM 2014). A Water Plan has been enacted for the Mary River catchment, which sets environmental flow targets (State of Queensland 2024), and other relevant water plans are in review (D. Roberts 2025 pers comm 23 Apr).

###### Instream barriers to movement

Barriers prevent movement of the Australian lungfish, with only a small proportion of barriers fitted with fishways capable of passing large species (Stuart & Berghuis 2002; Todd et al. 2019, 2025). Fishways rely on adequate water flow and often fail during drought or from flood damage (Berghuis & Broadfoot 2004a,b; Kind 2011). Barriers restrict access to spawning habitat, reduce genetic flow, limit juvenile dispersal, and increase the risk of disease and predation through congregation at barrier walls (Kind et al. 2005; Fisheries Qld 2017; Espinoza et al. 2021). Fish passage is mandatory only for new structures or modified structures, and removing or modifying existing barriers is costly (DoEE 2014).

Dams and weirs with stepped spillways (e.g., Paradise Dam), and steep faces with shallow associated plunge pools (e.g., Ned Churchward Weir), injure fish as they move over the top during high flow or flood events (Brooks & Kind 2002; Kind 2002; Stuart & Berghuis 2002; Berghuis & Broadfoot 2004a; Hoffman 2009; Qld DEEDI 2012). The numbers of individuals killed via dam over-topping is unknown (Kind et al. 2008; Fisheries Qld 2017), but anecdotal reporting of mortality is high (Qld DEEDI 2012; Kemp 2023). Tidal barrages (e.g., Ben Anderson Barrage, Mary Barrage) trap fish in estuarine waters (Brooks & Kind 2002; Berghuis 2001; Stuart & Berghuis 2002), where they suffer high mortality due to salinity intolerance and predation (Pusey et al. 2004).

###### Availability of high-quality food sources

Crustaceans, gastropods, and bivalves are the primary source of volatile fatty acids for the Australian lungfish. Fatty acids cannot be synthesized by the species, making diet-dependant intake critical for healthy egg production and hatchling survival (Kemp 2017a,b, 2019; Tao et al. 2020). Low fatty acid levels are more pronounced in lungfish found upstream of impoundments than in individuals downstream (Tao et al. 2020). It is uncertain why a difference in fatty acid profiles (food quality marker) exists, but food availability, hydrological alteration and natural features are possible causes (Tao et al. 2020).

Stable isotope analysis reveals dietary shifts in impounded areas: gastropods dominate upstream diets (45–80 %), while crustaceans comprise only 2–30 %. Downstream, it is reversed, with crustaceans represent 50–70 %, while gastropods comprise only 5–25 % (Tao et al. 2020). Bivalves contributed approximately 20 % of the diet in both upstream and downstream habitats (Tao et al. 2020).

Primary consumers, including crustaceans, gastropods and bivalves obtain volatile fatty acids from eating and digesting filamentous algae (Kemp 2017a,b, 2019). Riparian clearing and nutrient runoff alter fatty acid profiles in algae, affecting the entire food web (Tao et al. 2020). Increased regulation may have shifted primary production from benthic macrophytes and algae systems to plankton systems, further reducing fatty acid availability (Olden et al. 2019; Tao et al. 2020).

##### 5.2.2 Climate change

By 2050, Climate projections for Qld are for temperatures to increase by 1.3–2.5 °C under a high emission (RCP8.5) scenario, and 0.7–1.7 °C under a low emission (RCP2.6) scenario (CSIRO 2021). More hot days (&gt; 35 °C), lower annual rainfall, and intensified heavy rainfall events are projected, increasing the frequency and severity of droughts, floods and bushfires (Dowdy et al. 2015; CSIRO 2021).

With a 1–2°C temperature rise, streamflow is projected to change significantly, affecting low and high flow events (Dyer et al. 2014). In the relatively unregulated Mary River, climate change will likely result in stream flow changes. In heavily regulated rivers like the Burnett and Brisbane, changes will be more extreme, further degrading habitats.

**Extreme weather events in south east Qld**\
Extreme events, such as flooding, is a long-recognised feature of these catchments (Fesl 2009). However, over the last 25-years, south east Qld has experienced severe droughts and record floods (CoA 2013; BOM 2024; Queensland Government 2025), significantly degrading riverine and impoundment habitat (Kemp 2019). Major events include:

Millennium Drought 2001-09.

Flooding events in 2010-13.

Continued drought from 2017-19 which lead to severe bushfires in 2019-20, and

Flooding events in 2020-22 and 2025.

**Droughts**\
Droughts reduce stream flow, isolate deep pools, degrade water quality, and promote conditions for weed growth (Bunn & Arthington 2002). Increased hot days raise water temperatures, with shallow reaches mirroring air temperatures (DoEE 2014). Higher water temperatures result in reduced hatchling survival, with an increase in egg mortality observed in temperatures over 30 °C (Kemp 1981; Roberts et al. 2014).

During droughts, the Burnett, Mary and Brisbane rivers have ceased to flow in parts of the catchment and surface waters receded to isolated waterholes that provide refuges for lungfish.

During recent droughts, stream flow on the Burnett River was low to absent, and the water level in the Paradise Dam was reduced to ~ 20 % of capacity by late 2021 due to substantial downstream releases to lower the storage to complete engineering works to lower the spillway height in response to increased drought brought about through climate change (BOM 2021). On the Mary River, unprecedented low levels (near zero) were recorded at stream flow gauging stations, with flows minimal or absent, and connections lost between deeper pools (ABC 2020). On the Brisbane and North Pine rivers, flows were low to absent, with water levels in Lake Wivenhoe and the North Pine dams falling to ~ 40 % and 60 % of capacity, respectively, by late 2021 (BOM 2021). In contrast, below Wivenhoe Dam, flows are constant, providing potential breeding habitats in macrophyte beds.

**Floods**\
Floods erode riverbanks, weaken riparian vegetation, scour instream habitat features, macrophyte beds and invertebrate fauna, and remove macrophyte seed banks (Kemp 2011, 2020; Burke et al. 2024). This has serious impacts for critical Australian lungfish foraging and breeding habitat. Lost macrophyte beds can take years to recover, with scoured areas vulnerable to aquatic weed incursion (see Section 5.2.4 below) (Brooks & Kind 2002; Bunn & Arthington 2002; Seqwater 2017 pers comm 13 June; Burke et al. 2024). Instream barriers further limit macrophyte re-establishment (Seqwater 2017 pers comm 13 June; Burke et al. 2024). Floods can displace lungfish both upstream or downstream, enabling them to cross barriers and establish themselves in new environments, which can then also serve as redistribution points (Auburn Hawkwood People 2025).

Historic flooding from December 2010 to January 2011 resulted in more than 78 % of Qld declared a disaster zone (Qld FCI 2012). This was followed by further flooding in 2012 and 2013, surpassing the peak reached during the 2010-11 floods (CoA 2013). Following periods of high rainfall in south east Qld, severe floods also occurred in 2021, 2022 and 2025 (Queensland Government 2025).

Macrophyte beds were nearly non-existent in the main trunk and tributaries of the Mary River for around 3 years during the 2011-2013 flooding events. This intense flooding also severely reduced the extent and abundance of submerged macrophytes in the Brisbane River downstream of Wivenhoe Dam (Kennard et al. 2021). In addition, post the 2010 floods, only occasional small patches of macrophytes remained in places in the Burnett River (AMTD 64–242 km), with increased turbidity and considerable river channel alterations recorded (Fisheries Qld 2017). In the Mary River, extreme turbidity levels (over 600 NTU’s) were recorded during the 2010-11 floods, equivalent to approximately 237 tonnes of sediment (or seven dump trucks) flowing every minute (MRCCC 2011). Fewer opportunities for successful recruitment due to increasing flood frequency could have serious consequences for the lungfish since it relies on episodic recruitment.

**Bushfires**\
Bushfires in 2019-20 affected the Wide Bay-Burnett, Darling Downs, Brisbane and Moreton Bay regions (Qld RA 2020). The extent of the impact on Australian lungfish subpopulations is not known. However, the location and magnitude of the 2019-20 fires in these regions are unlikely to have significantly impacted its habitat (CoA 2021). However, the increased risk of more frequent and severe bushfires does provide a significant risk to the species.

Bushfires cause short-term water temperature increases and alterations to water chemistry (Lyon & O’Connor 2008). Post-fire rainfall leads to sediment runoff into waterways, which can include ash, toxic contaminants, nutrients, and fire retardant (Lyon & O’Connor 2008; Alexandra & Finlayson 2020; Silva et al. 2020; Ward et al. 2022). This can clog gills, smother macrophytes, and cause toxic algal blooms which deoxygenate the water (Alexandra & Finlayson 2020). Impacts have been recorded up to 80 km downstream of burn sites (Lyon & O’Connor 2008; Silva et al. 2020).

##### 5.2.3 Land clearing for primary industry and urbanisation

**Land use**\
Agriculture covers large areas of the Australian lungfish’s distribution range, covering 48 % of the Burnett, 41 % of the Brisbane, and 39 % of the Mary River catchments (Olden et al. 2019). Less than 20 % of riverine habitat in these catchments remains in a natural state, with the Burnett River being the most altered, where less than 9 % of the catchment is considered a natural environment.

Instream habitat loss and degradation results from land use activities such as land clearing, the removal of riparian vegetation along waterways and the installation of dams and weirs. The environmental impacts from these land use activities causes decreased water quality from sediment entering the waterway and light attenuation, a decline in waterway shading and cooling, a decline in undercut banks important for refuge, and a decline in instream woody debris important for all life-cycle stages of the Australian lungfish (Arthington 2009). It also promotes invasive weeds, alters flow dynamics, disrupts food chains (including macroinvertebrate loss), and limits the supply of instream snags (Arthington 2009; Kind 2011; DoEE 2014). Livestock access to riverbanks is widespread, with trampling of eggs and breeding habitat observed (Kind 2011).

**Salinity issues**\
The Burnett and Mary River catchments are among Australia’s most salinity-affected areas (Kind et al. 2008). Australian lungfish are intolerant to saline conditions, which create movement barriers (Pusey et al. 2004; Hughes et al. 2015). The sub-lethal effects of salinity on early life stages are unclear, but any habitat loss as a result of suboptimal water parameters would negatively impact the population (Kind et al. 2008).

**Catchment condition and decline**\
Assessments of river health indicate significant degradation:

Burnett River  overall, substantially modified condition, being rated moderate-poor condition in 82 % of the central Burnett (33 % in poor condition) and 77 % of the lower Burnett (25 % in poor condition) (DoEE 2014).

Mary River  overall, moderately modified condition, being rated moderate-poor condition in 100 % of the river (88 % in poor condition) (DoEE 2014).

Brisbane River  The mid-Brisbane declined from fair rating in 2001 to a fail rating in 2012-14, before slowly recovering to fair condition by 2020 (HLW 2021). The lower-Brisbane declined from a fair rating in 2001 to a fail rating in 2004-11, recovering to a fair condition by 2020.

##### 5.2.4 Introduced species

**Invasive and non-native introduced fish and other pest species**\
*Oreochromis mossambicus* (Mozambique tilapia), a declared noxious and threatening alien species in Qld, is widespread in the Brisbane River (Arthington 2009; Kind 2011) and the Mary River (BMRG 2024). Established subpopulations also exist in Lake Samsonvale (North Pine River) and Lake Boondooma (Boyne River, upper Burnett catchment) and may have colonised the lower reaches following dam over-topping during flood flows (A. Berghuis 2017 pers comm 25 July). Some areas may be less prone to Mozambique tilapia as higher tannin can negatively impact them (Liu et al 2025).

Common carp (*Cyprinus carpio*) is also widespread in the Brisbane River, including large numbers in the Lower Brisbane, where they may be responsible for the slow recovery of macrophytes since 2011 (D. Roberts 2025 pers comm 3 Apr). Carp are also reported in the lower North Pine River.

Nesting behaviour in carp and tilapia disturb and sometimes completely alter native fish habitats by seriously raising turbidity and destroying submergent macrophytes, thereby degrading spawning habitat (Roberts et al. 1995). They also eat eggs and small fish as part of their diet, posing an additional threat to juvenile lungfish (Arthington 2009; Kind 2011). Tilapia, carp and *Carassius auratus* (goldfish) graze on macrophytes when food sources are scarce, limiting the ability for macrophytes to recover (Burke et al. 2024).

Another non-native fish that may negatively affect lungfish is the eastern gambusia (*Gambusia holbrooki*), which occurs throughout all of the systems where lungfish are found. It is one of the world’s worst invasive species (Lowe et al. 2000) and has been implicated in declines and local extinctions of fish species in Australia and around the world. Its impacts are primarily through direct interactions, such as egg and larval predation.

Sooty grunter (*Hephaestus fuliginosus*) and barred grunter (*Amniataba percoides*) have expanded their ranges out of their native ranges into the Burnett and Mary rivers (BMRG 2024) and may prey on juveniles.

**Stocking of native fish**\
Native fish have been widely introduced across the Australian lungfish’s distribution (Hollaway & Hamlyn 2001). Annual stocking includes *Macquaria novemaculeata* (Australian bass), *M. ambigua* (golden perch), *Scleropages leichardti* (saratoga), *Bidyanus bidyanus* (silver perch) and *Lates calcarifer* (barramundi). Golden perch, silver perch, and saratoga are stocked outside of their natural range.

The impact of stocked fish on the Australian lungfish is unclear. These species may prey on juveniles or eggs, compete for food and habitat, disturb spawning habitat, or spread diseases and parasites (Kind 2011).

**Invasive weeds**\
Introduced aquatic weeds, such as *Eichhornia crassipes* (water hyacinth), *Salvinia molesta* (salvinia), *Egeria densa* (dense waterweed) and *Cabomba caroliniana* (cabomba) are present within the distribution range of the Australian lungfish. It is unclear what impacts these non-native aquatic plant species are having on lungfish. Terrestrial weeds in the riparian zone, such as *Dolichandra unguis-cati* (cat's claw creeper), can also threaten waterway health by destabilising banks (J. McCann 2025 pers comm 11 July).

Altered flow regimes have facilitated weed establishment, with weeds particularly abundant in reservoirs (Bunn & Arthington 2002). Weeds which cover the water surface, like water hyacinth and salvinia, reduce water temperature and oxygen levels, inhibiting the growth of submerged native plants. Australian lungfish eggs have been found in water hyacinth stands, but submerged weeds with dense growth, such as cabomba, are unsuitable for spawning (Kemp 1984).

##### 5.2.5 Fishing and boating activities

The Australian lungfish is a ‘no take’ species under the *Fisheries Act 1994* (Qld). However, fishing competition data and other reports confirm that incidental captures occur. Anecdotal evidence suggests illegal take for human consumption or for pet food, with discarded carcasses found on riverbanks at popular angling sites (Kind 2002; Qld DAF unpub data, cited in Kind et al. 2008). The extent of illegal take has not been quantified, and no data exist on the post-release survival of accidentally caught and released individuals.

Australian lungfish deaths from boat strike have been recorded in the lower Burnett, Mary, and Brisbane Rivers (Qld DPIF unpub data, cited in Kind et al. 2008). This increases adult mortality rates, though the overall impact to the overall population is unclear.

#### 5.3 Threat prioritisation

Each of the threats has been evaluated using a risk matrix (Figure 6) to determine its impact on the population and prioritise actions in Section 7: Actions to achieve the objective. The risk matrix considers the likelihood of occurrence and the severity of the consequence.

Threats may vary across the species’ range and throughout the year. However, following the precautionary principle, the risk category is based on the subpopulation at highest risk. In some cases, the consequences of certain activities are unknown, however they are still likely to pose a serious threat to the species. Population-wide threats are generally assigned a higher risk level.

The risk matrix (Figure 6) uses a qualitative assessment drawing on peer reviewed literature and expert opinion. In some cases, the consequences of activities are unknown. In these cases, the precautionary principle has been applied.

Unknown – probability that threat will have an impact on the species within the next 3 generations or 10 years, whichever is longer, could be 0-100%.

### 6 Vision and objective

#### 6.1 Long-term vision

Maintain and increase recruitment and spawning habitat of the Australian lungfish and increase subpopulations to an extent that the species is no longer regarded as threatened and has been removed from the EPBC Act list of threatened species.

#### 6.2 Recovery plan objective

Ensure the Australian lungfish has healthy, self-sustaining subpopulations in all catchments in which it occurs.

### 7 Actions to achieve the objective

This section describes the actions identified for the recovery of the Australian lungfish. Due to naturally sporadic spawning and variable recruitment, recovery could take as long as 30–50 years (Kemp 1986; Arthington 2009).

Priorities assigned to actions should be interpreted as follows:

**Priority 1:** taking prompt action is necessary to mitigate the key threats to the Australian lungfish and also provide valuable information to help identify long-term population trends.

**Priority 2:** action would provide a more informed basis for the long-term management and recovery of the Australian lungfish.

**Priority 3:** action is desirable, but not critical to the recovery of the Australian lungfish or assessment of trends in that recovery.

### 8 Current policy and management practices

**Legislative protections**

**EPBC Act (Commonwealth)**: the Australian lungfish is listed as Vulnerable on the threatened species list under the EPBC Act. As such, it is an offence to kill, injure, take, trade, keep, or move any individual without a permit on Commonwealth lands. In addition, all listed threatened species are considered matters of national environmental significance (MNES), and any action that may have a significant impact on an MNES must be referred to the Minister of the Environment for approval.

The Department of Climate Change, Energy, the Environment and Water (DCCEEW), as the Australian Government department responsible for administering the EPBC Act, maintains a suite of interactive tools that allow users to search, find and generate reports on information and data describing MNES, including the Australian lungfish.

***Biosecurity Act 2015*** (**Commonwealth**): manages biosecurity risks to plant, animal, human and environmental health.

The species is also protected across its range by Qld state legislation.

***Nature Conservation Act 1992*** **(Qld):** the Australian lungfish is listed as Vulnerable.

***Fisheries*** **Act** ***1994*** **(Qld)**: the Australian lungfish is listed as a ‘no take’ species.

***Biosecurity Act 2014*** **(Qld):** deals with biosecurity risks, including invasive plants, animals, pests, diseases, and contaminants.

**International protection**

**Convention on International Trade in Endangered Species (CITES)**: the Australian lungfish is included in Appendix II. This Appendix contains species that are not necessarily threatened with extinction but may become so unless trade in specimens of such species is subject to strict regulation in order to avoid utilisation incompatible with the survival of the species in the wild. International trade in specimens of Appendix II species may be authorised by the granting of an export permit.

**Water Resource Management**

***Water Act 2000*** **(Qld)**: Plans for sustainable management of water supply are detailed in Water Resource Plans published as subordinate legislation under the *Water Act*. Rules for the use and management of water are encompassed in Resource Operating Plans (ROP).

Water infrastructure management and bulk water supply are provided largely by Sunwater, a Qld Government-owned corporation. Within the Brisbane and North Pine river catchments, major water storages are managed by Seqwater.

### 9 Biodiversity benefits and impacts on other native species

Threats to the Australian lungfish also impact a number of other native species. Implementation of this recovery plan will benefit many threatened species protected under the EPBC Act, including:

*Maccullochella mariensis* (Mary River cod) – Mary – Endangered

*Nannoperca oxleyana* (Oxleyan pygmy perch) – Mary – Endangered

*Elseya albagula* (white-throated snapping turtle) – Mary/Burnett – Critically Endangered

*Elusor macrurus* (Mary River turtle) – Mary – Critically Endangered

*Euastacus hystricosus* (Conondale spiny crayfish) – Mary – Endangered

*Euastacus urospinosus* (Maleny crayfish) – Mary – Endangered

Other native fish species are likely to benefit from remedial actions around fish passage, such as *Macquaria novemaculeata* (Australian bass), *Ambassis marianus* (estuary perchlet), *Mugil cephalus* (sea mullet), *Neoarius graeffei* (blue catfish), *Trachystoma petardi* (freshwater mullet) and *Nematalosa erebi* (bony herring), which form part of the fish communities and readily migrate up and downstream river channels across the Australian lungfish’s range (Berghuis 2001).
